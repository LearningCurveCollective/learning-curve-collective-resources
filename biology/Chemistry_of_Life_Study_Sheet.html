Why a biology class starts with chemistry

The short version

Every living thing is built from atoms, and everything a cell does — storing energy, copying DNA, moving muscles — is atoms making and breaking bonds. Most of that happens in water. So before biology can talk about cells, it needs three ideas: how atoms are put together, how atoms bond, and why water behaves the way it does.

Almost every question on this material is one of those three in disguise.

The whole topic in one picture

The one sentence to memorize: Oxygen pulls the shared electrons harder than hydrogen does, so water is a polar molecule (O is partially negative, each H partially positive); the partial charges let water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and to other charged or polar things, and that is the source of every property that makes water essential for life.

Reading an element: protons, electrons, neutrons

An atom has a nucleus (protons + neutrons) with electrons orbiting in shells. Two numbers on the periodic table tell you everything:

Atomic number Z = number of protons. This defines the element. In a neutral atom, electrons = protons.

Atomic weight / mass number A = protons + neutrons (rounded to a whole number).

Three particles, three jobs

Protons decide which element it is

Change the proton count and you have a different element. 6 protons is always carbon; 7 is always nitrogen. That's why the atomic number is the element's ID.

Electrons decide the charge

Same element, different electron count = same element with a charge (an ion). Na and Na⁺ are both sodium; one has 11 electrons, the other 10.

Neutrons decide the isotope (mass)

Same element, different neutron count = a heavier or lighter version (isotope). Carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon. This is why the table shows 15.999 for oxygen — it's an average over isotopes.

Also, the periodic table's atomic weight is a decimal average (15.999) — round it to the nearest whole number before subtracting. And the electron count only equals the proton count for a neutral atom; ions are different (next section).

2

Electron shells and the 2n² rule

Electrons don't orbit randomly — they fill shells from the inside out. The maximum number of electrons a shell can hold is

capacity of shell n = 2n²  ⇒  1st: 2(1)² = 2,   2nd: 2(2)² = 8,   3rd: 2(3)² = 18

The outermost occupied shell is the valence shell, and the electrons in it are valence electrons. Valence electrons are the only ones that matter for bonding. Atoms "want" a full valence shell (8 for most elements, 2 for H and He) — that's the octet rule, and it's the reason atoms bond at all.

A subtlety that trips people up: the 3rd shell can hold 18, but for the elements you'll be asked about (up to calcium, #20) it takes only 8 before the 4th shell starts. So the filling pattern to use is 2, 8, 8, 2. Sodium is 2-8-1, chlorine is 2-8-7, argon is 2-8-8, potassium is 2-8-8-1. Use 2n² for "how many can fit"; use 2-8-8 for "how do I draw it."

Bohr models. Sodium's lone valence electron (red) is easy to give away; chlorine's empty slot (dashed) is easy to fill. That mismatch is the whole story of ionic bonding. The Atom Builder tab draws any of the first 20 elements like this.

3

Ions: when electrons ≠ protons

An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it carries a net charge. Protons never change — only electrons move.

Cation = positive ion. The atom loses electrons. Sodium: 11 p⁺, 10 e⁻ → net +1 → Na⁺. (Memory hook: cation has a "t" that looks like a plus sign.)

Anion = negative ion. The atom gains electrons. Chlorine: 17 p⁺, 18 e⁻ → net −1 → Cl⁻. (A negative ion.)

Pattern: metals (left side of the table, 1–3 valence electrons) lose electrons and become cations. Nonmetals (right side, 5–7 valence electrons) gain electrons and become anions. Elements with 4 valence electrons (carbon, silicon) are in the middle and prefer to share — that's covalent bonding. Noble gases (8) don't react.

Three bonds, one idea: atoms want full valence shells

Every bond type is a different way of getting a full outer shell. The question to ask about any bond is: are the electrons transferred, shared equally, shared unequally, or is this just an attraction between molecules?

Ionic and covalent bonds make substances (they're how atoms join into NaCl or H₂O). Hydrogen bonds make substances stick to each other — a water molecule is complete before any hydrogen bond forms. Break every hydrogen bond in a glass of water and you still have water; break the covalent bonds and you have hydrogen and oxygen gas.

2

Ionic bonds — give and take

An ionic bond is the complete transfer of one or more electrons from one atom to another. This creates oppositely charged ions that are attracted to each other by electrostatic forces. The atom that loses electrons is the cation (positive); the one that gains is the anion (negative). Ionic bonds form between metals and nonmetals.

Sodium ends up more positive because it still has 11 protons but only 10 electrons. Chlorine ends up negative: 17 protons, 18 electrons. Both now have full outer shells. Same story for Na + F → NaF.

3

Covalent bonds — sharing

A covalent bond forms when two atoms share electrons so that both get a full valence shell. This makes a molecule. Covalent bonds happen between nonmetals, and they're the strongest bonds in biology — the backbone of every biomolecule. Two things to classify about any covalent bond: how many pairs are shared and how fairly they're shared.

Single bond — share 2 electrons (1 pair)

Water, H₂O: H–O–H. Each H shares its 1 electron with O; O shares one of its 6 with each H. H gets 2 (full), O gets 8 (full). Also Cl₂: Cl–Cl.

Double bond — share 4 electrons (2 pairs)

Carbon dioxide, CO₂: O=C=O. Carbon has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 more; each oxygen needs 2. Two double bonds fix everyone. Also O₂: O=O.

Triple bond — share 6 electrons (3 pairs)

Nitrogen gas, N₂: N≡N. Each N has 5 valence electrons and needs 3 more, so they share 3 pairs. Extremely strong, which is why N₂ in the air is so unreactive.

Counting rule

Each line in a structural formula = one shared pair = 2 electrons. Count the lines, multiply by 2. Each atom needs (8 − valence electrons) more, except H needs 1.

Polar vs. nonpolar covalent

"Polar" just means one end is a little positive and the other a little negative. The lowercase delta (δ) means partial charge, as opposed to the full +/− charge on an ion.

How do you know which atom pulls harder? Oxygen, nitrogen, and fluorine are electron hogs (high electronegativity). Hydrogen and carbon are weak pullers. So: H–O, H–N, H–F are polar. Two of the same atom (Cl–Cl, O=O) are always nonpolar — identical atoms pull identically. C–H is close enough to equal that it counts as nonpolar, which is why CH₄ and lipids (fats) are nonpolar and won't dissolve in water.

4

Hydrogen bonds — attraction between molecules

A hydrogen bond is not a bond inside a molecule. It's an attraction between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen (or nitrogen or fluorine) of another. It can only happen if the molecules already have polar H–O, H–N or H–F covalent bonds. In water: the hydrogen on one H₂O is attracted to the oxygen on the next H₂O.

Key factWhy it's true

The classic trick question: "What kind of bond holds the H to the O inside a water molecule?" → polar covalent. "What kind of bond holds one water molecule to another?" → hydrogen bond. In diagrams, solid lines are the H–O covalent bonds within a molecule and dotted lines are the hydrogen bonds between molecules.

1

Structure of a water molecule

H₂O: one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms. Oxygen has 8 protons; hydrogen has 1. The oxygen nucleus pulls the shared electrons much harder, so the electrons spend more time near the O.

Result: a partial negative charge (δ−) on the oxygen and a partial positive charge (δ+) on each hydrogen. A difference in charge between parts of a molecule is called polarity. Water is a polar molecule.

The molecule is bent, not straight (about 104.5° between the H's), because oxygen's two unshared electron pairs push the hydrogens to one side. That bend is what makes one end negative and the other positive — a straight H–O–H would cancel out.

2

Properties of water — and the scientific explanation for each

Every row below is the same argument: polarity → hydrogen bonds → property → why life needs it. If a short-paragraph question asks you to explain a property, give all four links in the chain.

Cohesion (water sticks to water) → surface tension

Hydrogen bonds pull water molecules toward each other, so the surface behaves like a stretched skin. Why it matters: insects walk on ponds; water moves up a plant as a continuous column (each molecule drags the next one up) — that's how trees get water to their leaves.

Adhesion (water sticks to other things) → capillary action

Polar water molecules are attracted to other polar or charged surfaces (glass, cellulose in plant cell walls). Why it matters: water climbs narrow tubes against gravity — the meniscus in a straw, water moving through soil and through the xylem of plants.

Universal solvent

Water dissolves ionic and polar substances because its charged ends pull them apart and surround them (see the NaCl diagram below). Why it matters: every chemical reaction in a cell happens in water; blood carries dissolved nutrients, gases, and waste; the cell's interior is a water-based solution.

High specific heat (resists temperature change)

To warm water you first have to break hydrogen bonds, which absorbs a lot of energy; to cool it, bonds re-form and release energy. Why it matters: oceans and lakes stay at stable temperatures, organisms (which are mostly water) don't overheat or freeze easily, and sweating cools you because evaporating water carries away lots of heat.

Ice is less dense than liquid water (ice floats)

When water freezes, hydrogen bonds lock the molecules into a rigid hexagonal lattice that holds them farther apart than in the liquid. Why it matters: ice floats and insulates the water below, so lakes don't freeze solid and aquatic life survives winter. Almost every other substance is denser as a solid.

Stabilizes the structures of proteins and DNA

Hydrogen bonds between bases hold the two strands of DNA together, and hydrogen bonds fold proteins into their functional shapes. Because each bond is weak, the strands can unzip for copying and proteins can flex — but many bonds together are strong. Why it matters: proteins and DNA are "the building blocks of cells."

3

How water dissolves salt (solvent, solute, solution)

Solvent = the substance doing the dissolving (H₂O).  Solute = the substance being dissolved (NaCl, sugar).  Solution = solute evenly mixed in solvent (salt water).

Table salt is an ionic compound: Na⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions locked together. Drop it in water and water's partial charges pull the crystal apart: the δ− oxygen ends of water surround each Na⁺, and the δ+ hydrogen ends surround each Cl⁻. Each ion ends up in a shell of water molecules (a "hydration shell"), and the salt has dissolved. Sugar dissolves the same way — it's not ionic, but its many polar O–H groups hydrogen-bond with water. Oils and fats won't dissolve because they're nonpolar: nothing for water to grab.

Each dissolved ion ends up in a "hydration shell" of water molecules: Na⁺ among the oxygen ends, Cl⁻ among the hydrogen ends.

Model short-paragraph answers

"Explain why water is a polar molecule and why that matters for life."

Water (H₂O) is made of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to two hydrogen atoms. Oxygen's nucleus (8 protons) attracts the shared electrons much more strongly than hydrogen's (1 proton), so the electrons spend more time near the oxygen. This gives the oxygen a partial negative charge and each hydrogen a partial positive charge; because the molecule is bent, the charges don't cancel, so water is polar. The partial charges let water molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other and with other polar or charged substances. Hydrogen bonding gives water the properties life depends on: it dissolves the ions and polar molecules cells need (universal solvent), it resists temperature change so organisms and oceans stay stable (high specific heat), it is cohesive and adhesive so it moves up through plants, and ice floats so lakes don't freeze solid. Hydrogen bonds also hold DNA strands together and fold proteins. Without polarity, none of this would happen — life wouldn't exist without this bond.

"Compare ionic, covalent, and hydrogen bonds."

An ionic bond forms when one atom completely transfers one or more electrons to another, usually a metal giving to a nonmetal. The atom that loses electrons becomes a positive cation and the one that gains becomes a negative anion, and the opposite charges attract (NaCl). A covalent bond forms when two nonmetal atoms share electrons so both fill their valence shells, making a molecule. If the electrons are shared equally the bond is nonpolar (Cl₂, O₂); if one atom pulls harder the bond is polar and the atoms carry partial charges (the H–O bonds in water). A hydrogen bond is not a bond within a molecule but a weak attraction between the partially positive hydrogen of one polar molecule and the partially negative oxygen, nitrogen, or fluorine of another. Individually hydrogen bonds are weak and easily broken, but together they hold water molecules to each other and stabilize DNA and proteins.

"Explain what happens when salt dissolves in water."

Salt (NaCl) is an ionic compound made of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Water is the solvent and salt is the solute. Because water is polar, the partially negative oxygen ends of water molecules are attracted to the positive sodium ions and the partially positive hydrogen ends are attracted to the negative chloride ions. These attractions pull the ions away from the crystal, and each ion becomes surrounded by a shell of water molecules. The ions spread evenly through the water, forming a solution. This works because water is polar; a nonpolar liquid like oil could not pull the ions apart.
