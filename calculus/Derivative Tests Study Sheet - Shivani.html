1

What each derivative tells you

$f'$ = which way $f$ is going

$f'(x) > 0 \Rightarrow f$ increasing
$f'(x) < 0 \Rightarrow f$ decreasing

Velocity is $f'$ for position: positive means moving right.

$f''$ = which way $f$ is bending

$f''(x) > 0 \Rightarrow$ concave up ☺
$f''(x) < 0 \Rightarrow$ concave down ☹

Smiley face holds water (min). Frowny face spills it (max).

Critical point (candidate for max/min)

$f'(c) = 0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist

Only a candidate: it could be a max, a min, or neither (a "saddle").

Candidate for inflection

$f''(c) = 0$ or $f''(c)$ does not exist

It only counts if $f''$ actually changes sign there and $f$ is continuous.

3

Justification sentences (what gets the points)

The number line is your work. The sentence is your justification. Every sentence names the function (say $f'$, never "it" or "the graph") and states the sign.

Local max$f$ has a local maximum at $x = c$ because $f'$ changes from positive to negative at $x = c$.
Local min$f$ has a local minimum at $x = c$ because $f'$ changes from negative to positive at $x = c$.
2nd deriv test$f$ has a local maximum at $x = c$ because $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$.
Neither$f$ has no extremum at $x = c$ because $f'$ does not change sign at $x = c$.
Inflection$f$ has a point of inflection at $x = c$ because $f''$ changes sign at $x = c$.
Two conditionsOn $(a, b)$, $f' > 0$ so $f$ is increasing, and $f'' < 0$ so $f$ is concave down.
ParticleThe particle changes direction at $t = c$ because $v(t)$ changes sign at $t = c$.

Traps from Tuesday

  • Number line with no sentence. Always finish with the sentence from section 3.
  • $f''(c) = 0$ doesn't mean anything yet. For max/min, the second derivative test is inconclusive. For inflection, you still need a sign change.
  • Differentiate the real $f'$, not the simplified one. On card R, $h'(t) = 3t^2 + 9t - 12$, so $h''(t) = 6t + 9$. Dividing out the 3 first gives $2t + 3$: same sign, wrong value.
  • Quick arithmetic check. $f''(-3) = 2(-3) + 2 = -4$, not $-1$. The sign saved you this time, but the value counts if they ask for it.
  • Don't forget $\pm$ when you take a square root: $x^2 = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow x = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$.
  • Compute intercepts, don't eyeball them. The segment from $(-6,-10)$ to $(-2,2)$ crosses at $x = -\tfrac83 \approx -2.67$, not $-2.75$. Use slope and point.
  • $v = 0$ is not the same as a direction change. If $v(t) = (t-2)^2$, then $v(2) = 0$, but $v$ is positive on both sides, so the particle pauses at $t = 2$ and keeps going right. Check the sign on both sides every time.

1

The three ways, on one problem

This is card P from Tuesday: $f'(x) = x^2 + 2x - 3$. Find and justify the local max and min.

Step 0: Find the critical points

Set $f'(x) = 0$ and factor: $(x+3)(x-1) = 0$, so $x = -3$ and $x = 1$. These are only candidates. Now classify them.

Way 2: First derivative (sign) test

Concept

Put the critical points on a number line for $f'$ and plug one test value into $f'$ from each interval. Then draw an arrow for each: positive is uphill ↗, negative is downhill ↘. The arrows show you the shape.

Interval$x < -3$$-3 < x < 1$$x > 1$
Test value$f'(-4) = 5$$f'(0) = -3$$f'(2) = 5$
Sign of $f'$+ ↗− ↘+ ↗

Uphill then downhill at $x = -3$ makes a peak. Downhill then uphill at $x = 1$ makes a valley.

Write$f$ has a local max at $x = -3$ because $f'$ changes from positive to negative there. $f$ has a local min at $x = 1$ because $f'$ changes from negative to positive there.

Way 3: Second derivative test

Concept

Differentiate $f'$ (the original form, not the factored one): $f''(x) = 2x + 2$. Plug in each critical point.

$$f''(-3) = 2(-3) + 2 = -4 < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{concave down } ☹ \;\Rightarrow\; \text{max}$$ $$f''(1) = 2(1) + 2 = 4 > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{concave up } ☺ \;\Rightarrow\; \text{min}$$
Write$f$ has a local max at $x = -3$ because $f'(-3) = 0$ and $f''(-3) = -4 < 0$.
When $f''(c) = 0$, the test says nothing. Example: $f(x) = x^4$ and $f(x) = x^3$ both have $f'(0) = 0$ and $f''(0) = 0$. The first has a min at 0; the second has neither. Fall back to Way 2.

Way 1: Plug-in check

Concept

If you have $f$ itself, compare $f(c)$ with values just to the left and right. Suppose $f(c-1) = 0$, $f(c) = 2$, and $f(c+1) = 1.5$. Then $f(c)$ beats both neighbors, so it's a max. If the values read $0, 1, 2$ from left to right, $f(c)$ is between its neighbors, so it's neither.

Use this to check an answer. For the written justification, use Way 2 or Way 3. Also keep the neighbors close: if another critical point sits between $c$ and $c+1$, the check can fool you.

2

When $f'$ doesn't change sign

Take $f'(x) = (x-2)^2(x+1)$. The critical points are $x = -1$ and $x = 2$.

Interval$x < -1$$-1 < x < 2$$x > 2$
Sign of $f'$−++

At $x = -1$: $-$ to $+$, so a local min. At $x = 2$: $+$ to $+$. The squared factor $(x-2)^2$ can't go negative, so $f$ just flattens out for a moment and keeps climbing. That's a "saddle" (neither). Try $f''(2)$ and you get $0$, so the second derivative test wouldn't have settled it either.

Write$f$ has no local extremum at $x = 2$ because $f'$ does not change sign there.
3

Endpoints (the boundary question)

On a closed interval like $[0, 4]$, the endpoints can't have a sign change because there's nothing on the other side. To decide what an endpoint is, look at which way $f$ is going just inside it:

$f$ increasing near it$f$ decreasing near it
Left endpointendpoint min (you climb away from it)endpoint max (you fall away from it)
Right endpointendpoint max (you climb into it)endpoint min (you fall into it)

From Tuesday: on card C, both boundaries turned out to be maxes, so they couldn't add another minimum. On the table card on $[0, 4]$, you caught that $x = 4$ is an endpoint max because $f' > 0$ on $(3, 4)$, and the answer key agreed. Nice catch.

Conventions differ. Some teachers count endpoints as "local" extrema and some don't. On a quiz, give the interior answer with the sign-change sentence, then add one line for any endpoint: "Also, $f(4)$ is an endpoint maximum because $f$ is increasing on $(3, 4)$." That covers you either way.

An inflection point is the sign test again, but with $f''$. Concavity flips from smiley to frowny or back. Use the tool on tab 1 with "f″" selected to see the same logic.

1

The inflection recipe

Three steps

  1. Find $f''$. If you're given $f'$, take one derivative. If you're given $f$, take two.
  2. Find the candidates: $f''(x) = 0$ or $f''$ does not exist.
  3. Number line for $f''$. Keep only the points where the sign changes (and $f$ is continuous).
Worked example: card E

$g'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x^2}$. Where does $g$ have a point of inflection?

1. Product rule. $g''(x) = 2e^{-x^2} + 2x\cdot(-2x)e^{-x^2} = 2e^{-x^2}(1 - 2x^2)$.

2. Set it to zero. $e^{-x^2}$ is never $0$, so $1 - 2x^2 = 0 \Rightarrow x^2 = \tfrac12 \Rightarrow x = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$. Don't forget the $\pm$.

3. Check signs. Plug in $0$: $g''(0) = 2 > 0$. Plug in $\pm 1$: $1 - 2 = -1$, negative. So the signs are $-, +, -$.

Write$g$ has points of inflection at $x = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ because $g''$ changes sign at each of those values.

Calculator version: card N

$f'(x) = -\tfrac12 x^3 - 2x^2 + 2x + 8$, so $f''(x) = -\tfrac32 x^2 - 4x + 2$. Solve $f''(x) = 0$ with the quadratic formula or the solver: $x \approx -3.097$ and $x \approx 0.431$.

Test points: $f''(-4) < 0$, $f''(0) = 2 > 0$, $f''(1) = -\tfrac32 - 4 + 2 < 0$. Signs $-, +, -$, so both are inflection points.

Write$f$ has points of inflection at $x \approx -3.097$ and $x \approx 0.431$ because $f''$ changes from negative to positive at $x \approx -3.097$ and from positive to negative at $x \approx 0.431$.

Inflection where $f''$ doesn't exist: card G

On card G's table, $f''$ is DNE at $x = -1$ and $x = 0$, but its sign goes $+, -, +$ across them and $f$ is continuous. So both are inflection points. $f''$ doesn't need to exist at the point. It only needs to change sign.

2

Velocity zero vs. acceleration zero

$v(t) = 0$

The particle is stopped for an instant. Position isn't changing. It only turns around if $v$ also changes sign.

$a(t) = 0$

Velocity isn't changing. The particle could be cruising at 30 m/s. It's just holding a constant speed.

Calculator problem: card O

$v(t) = t^2\sin(t^2 + 2t)$ on $[-2.2, 1.8]$. When is the particle moving left? CALCULATOR

Graph method (fastest on a quiz). Radian mode. Graph $y = x^2\sin(x^2+2x)$ with Xmin $= -2.2$ and Xmax $= 1.8$. Use the zero finder on each crossing: $-2$, $0$, $1.035$, $1.699$. Read off where the graph is below the axis.

Algebra method (Torin's way). $t^2 \ge 0$, so the sign of $v$ is the sign of $\sin(t^2 + 2t)$. Sine is negative when its input is between $-\pi$ and $0$ or between $\pi$ and $2\pi$. Solve $t^2 + 2t = 0$, $t^2 + 2t = \pi$, and $t^2 + 2t = 2\pi$ to get $t = -2, 0$, $t \approx 1.035$, and $t \approx 1.699$.

$(-2.2,-2)$$(-2,0)$$(0,1.035)$$(1.035,1.699)$$(1.699,1.8)$
$v$+−+−+
WriteThe particle moves left on $(-2, 0)$ and $(1.035, 1.699)$ because $v(t) < 0$ on those intervals.

Check $t = 0$ from the table: $v$ is negative on the left and positive on the right, so the particle does turn around there. The $t^2$ factor alone wouldn't flip the sign, but $\sin(t^2+2t)$ does. Always read the signs instead of assuming. (If the question asked "when does it change direction," the answer would be $t = -2,\ 0,\ 1.035,\ 1.699$.)
