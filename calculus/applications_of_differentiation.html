Callback — the over/under question, finally answered.

Last time we left a question hanging: what property of the function decides whether a linear approximation over- or under-estimates? The answer is concavity, and it lives in section 4.3. The one-line version:

$$f'' > 0 \;(\text{concave up}) \Rightarrow \text{tangent line is } \textbf{below} \text{ the curve} \Rightarrow \textbf{underestimate}$$ $$f'' < 0 \;(\text{concave down}) \Rightarrow \text{tangent line is } \textbf{above} \text{ the curve} \Rightarrow \textbf{overestimate}$$

Theorem & Test Reference

Everything you need for the Chapter 4 test on one page. Each tile names the result precisely — on a test, citing the right theorem by name earns the credit.

Extreme Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a,b]$, then $f$ attains an absolute maximum value and an absolute minimum value on $[a,b]$.

Existence guarantee. Both hypotheses matter: drop "closed" or "continuous" and it can fail.

Fermat's Theorem

If $f$ has a local max or min at $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.

Extrema hide only at critical numbers. The converse is false: $f'(c)=0$ does not guarantee an extremum (e.g. $x^3$ at $0$).

Critical Number

A number $c$ in the domain of $f$ where $f'(c)=0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist.

The complete list of candidate locations for local extrema.

Closed Interval Method

Abs. extrema of continuous $f$ on $[a,b]$: evaluate $f$ at (1) all critical numbers in $(a,b)$ and (2) the endpoints $a,b$. Largest value = abs max; smallest = abs min.

No derivative test needed — just compare the finite list of values.

Rolle's Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

The special case of the MVT with a horizontal secant.

Mean Value Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then for some $c\in(a,b)$, $$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$

Instantaneous slope $=$ average slope, somewhere. Tangent parallel to the secant.

Increasing / Decreasing Test

$f' > 0$ on an interval $\Rightarrow f$ increasing there. $\;f' < 0 \Rightarrow f$ decreasing there.

Sign of the first derivative = direction of travel.

First Derivative Test

At a critical number $c$: $f'$ changes $+\!\to\!-$ gives a local max; $-\!\to\!+$ gives a local min; no sign change gives neither.

Always works (even when $f''$ is messy or zero).

Concavity Test

$f'' > 0 \Rightarrow$ concave up (holds water). $\;f'' < 0 \Rightarrow$ concave down.

Concave up $\Rightarrow$ tangent below curve $\Rightarrow$ linear approx underestimates.

Inflection Point

A point where $f$ is continuous and concavity changes. Candidates: where $f''=0$ or $f''$ is undefined.

$f''=0$ alone is not enough — the sign must actually flip (e.g. $x^4$ at $0$ does not).

Second Derivative Test

If $f'(c)=0$: $\,f''(c)>0\Rightarrow$ local min; $\,f''(c)<0\Rightarrow$ local max; $\,f''(c)=0\Rightarrow$ inconclusive (fall back to the First Derivative Test).

Fast when $f''$ is easy to evaluate.

Optimization Recipe

Name $Q$ → write $Q$ from the picture → use the constraint to make $Q$ a function of one variable → state the domain → find & classify critical numbers → check endpoints → answer with units.

Same discipline as related rates: givens first, substitute last.

4.1 — Maximum & Minimum Values

The first question of the chapter: where can the biggest and smallest values of a function occur? We answer it with three ideas — a vocabulary (local vs. absolute), a guarantee (the Extreme Value Theorem), and a search rule (critical numbers, via Fermat).

Concept

Local vs. absolute extrema

$f$ has an absolute (global) maximum at $c$ if $f(c)\ge f(x)$ for all $x$ in the domain. It has a local (relative) maximum at $c$ if $f(c)\ge f(x)$ for all $x$ near $c$. (Minima: flip the inequality.) A local extremum is a "tallest hill in its own neighborhood"; an absolute extremum is the tallest hill anywhere.

An absolute max on a closed interval can occur either at a local max inside the interval or at an endpoint. Endpoints never get to be local extrema in Stewart's convention, but they absolutely compete for the global title — which is exactly why the Closed Interval Method checks them separately.

Concept

Two theorems that bracket the search

Extreme Value Theorem (EVT) tells us the extrema exist: a continuous function on a closed interval $[a,b]$ always attains an absolute max and min. Fermat's Theorem tells us where to look inside the interval: if a local extremum happens at $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$.

Contrapositive of Fermat, in plain terms: if the slope isn't zero (and the derivative exists), you're not at a local extremum. So the only interior candidates are the critical numbers — where $f'=0$ or $f'$ fails to exist.

Fermat's converse is false. $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$, but $0$ is not a local extremum — the curve just flattens and keeps climbing. A critical number is a suspect, not a conviction. You still have to test it.

Worked Example

Closed Interval Method

Find the absolute maximum and minimum of $f(x)=x^3-3x^2+1$ on $\left[-\tfrac12,\,4\right]$.

0

The plan. $f$ is a polynomial, so it is continuous on the closed interval and the EVT promises both extrema exist. By the Closed Interval Method we only need to compare $f$ at the interior critical numbers and at the two endpoints.

1

Differentiate and find critical numbers. $$f'(x)=3x^2-6x=3x(x-2).$$ This is zero at $x=0$ and $x=2$; it exists everywhere, so those are the only critical numbers. Both lie in $\left(-\tfrac12,4\right)$.

2

Evaluate at critical numbers and endpoints.

$$f\!\left(-\tfrac12\right)=-\tfrac18-\tfrac34+1=\tfrac18 \qquad f(0)=1$$ $$f(2)=8-12+1=-3 \qquad f(4)=64-48+1=17$$

3

Compare. The largest of $\{\tfrac18,\,1,\,-3,\,17\}$ is $17$; the smallest is $-3$.

$$\boxed{\text{abs max } f(4)=17, \qquad \text{abs min } f(2)=-3}$$

Notice the absolute max lives at an endpoint, not at a critical number — precisely the case the method is built to catch.

4.2 — The Mean Value Theorem

A short but load-bearing section. The MVT is the theorem that lets us turn statements about $f'$ into statements about $f$ — it is the reason "if $f'=0$ everywhere then $f$ is constant" is actually provable, and it quietly underwrites the entire next chapter on antiderivatives.

Concept

Rolle → Mean Value

Rolle's Theorem. If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and the endpoints match ($f(a)=f(b)$), then somewhere in between the tangent is flat: $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$. Intuitively, if you leave and return to the same height on a smooth path, at some moment you were moving horizontally.

Mean Value Theorem. Drop the requirement that the endpoints match, and the flat tangent becomes a tangent parallel to the secant:

$$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\quad\text{for some } c\in(a,b).$$

In words: the instantaneous rate of change equals the average rate of change at least once. If you average 60 mph over a trip, at some instant your speedometer read exactly 60.

Concept

Why we care (the consequence)

The MVT's real job is this corollary: if $f'(x)=0$ for all $x$ in an interval, then $f$ is constant there. And therefore, if $f'(x)=g'(x)$ on an interval, then $f$ and $g$ differ by a constant, $f=g+C$.

That second statement is the seed of the "$+\,C$" in every antiderivative you will write next chapter. The constant of integration is not bookkeeping — it is exactly the freedom the MVT says you have.

Worked Example

Finding the guaranteed $c$

Verify the MVT for $f(x)=x^2$ on $[1,3]$ and find the value $c$ it guarantees.

1

Check hypotheses. $f(x)=x^2$ is a polynomial: continuous on $[1,3]$ and differentiable on $(1,3)$. The MVT applies.

2

Compute the average slope (the secant). $$\frac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1}=\frac{9-1}{2}=4.$$

3

Set $f'(c)$ equal to it and solve. Since $f'(x)=2x$, $$2c=4 \;\Rightarrow\; c=2.$$

$$\boxed{c=2}\in(1,3)\;\checkmark$$

At $x=2$ the tangent line has slope $4$, exactly parallel to the secant from $(1,1)$ to $(3,9)$.

4.3 — Derivatives & the Shape of Graphs

This is the heart of the chapter. Two derivatives, two questions. $f'$ answers which way is the function going? (up or down). $f''$ answers which way is it bending? (cupped up or down). Put them together and you can describe a graph completely without ever plotting a point.

Concept

$f'$: direction of travel

Increasing/Decreasing Test. On any interval where $f'>0$, $f$ is increasing; where $f'<0$, $f$ is decreasing. So to find where a function rises and falls, build a sign chart for $f'$: mark the critical numbers, then test the sign of $f'$ in each resulting interval.

First Derivative Test. Once the sign chart is built, classifying each critical number $c$ is automatic:

  • $f'$ goes $+\to-$ at $c$  ⇒  local maximum (rises then falls — a peak).
  • $f'$ goes $-\to+$ at $c$  ⇒  local minimum (falls then rises — a valley).
  • $f'$ keeps the same sign  ⇒  neither (a flat spot that isn't a turn, like $x^3$ at $0$).

Use the Derivative Sign Explorer in the Visualizers tab to watch the sign chart drive the shape live.

First Derivative Test: the function turns around where the sign of $f'$ flips.

Concept

$f''$: direction of bending

Concavity Test. Where $f''>0$, the graph is concave up (a cup that holds water, slopes increasing). Where $f''<0$, it is concave down (a frown, slopes decreasing). An inflection point is where the concavity actually changes — look where $f''=0$ or is undefined, but confirm the sign genuinely flips.

Second Derivative Test. A shortcut for classifying a critical number $c$ with $f'(c)=0$: if $f''(c)>0$ the cup opens up so $c$ is a local min; if $f''(c)<0$ it's a local max; if $f''(c)=0$ the test is silent and you fall back to the First Derivative Test.

$f''(c)=0$ does not mean "inflection point." Example: $f(x)=x^4$ has $f''(0)=0$, but the graph is concave up on both sides — no change, no inflection. The sign must flip.

Concept — the callback

Concavity decides over- vs. under-estimate

Here is the answer to the question we left hanging last session. A linear approximation uses the tangent line to stand in for the curve. Whether that line sits above or below the true curve is governed entirely by concavity:

$$f''>0:\ \text{curve is concave up} \Rightarrow \text{tangent lies } \textbf{below} \Rightarrow L(x)\ \textbf{underestimates } f(x)$$ $$f''<0:\ \text{curve is concave down} \Rightarrow \text{tangent lies } \textbf{above} \Rightarrow L(x)\ \textbf{overestimates } f(x)$$

This is also why your $\sqrt{x}$ estimates always come out a hair high and your $x^2$ estimates a hair low. Same idea returns in integration as the left/right/trapezoid over- and under-estimates — concavity is king. The Linear Approximation visualizer lets you slide the base point and read the verdict.

Same tangent-line idea, two concavities — the curve always bends away from its tangent in the direction of $f''$.

Worked Example

Full analysis of one function

For $f(x)=x^4-4x^3$, find the intervals of increase/decrease, all local extrema, the concavity, and the inflection points.

1

First derivative and its sign chart. $$f'(x)=4x^3-12x^2=4x^2(x-3).$$ Critical numbers: $x=0$ and $x=3$. The factor $4x^2\ge0$ always, so the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $(x-3)$: negative for $x<3$, positive for $x>3$.

Decreasing on $(-\infty,3)$, increasing on $(3,\infty)$. At $x=3$, $f'$ goes $-\to+$: local min $f(3)=81-108=-27$. At $x=0$, $f'$ does not change sign (negative on both sides): neither — a flat inflection-type spot.

2

Second derivative and concavity. $$f''(x)=12x^2-24x=12x(x-2).$$ Zeros at $x=0$ and $x=2$. Sign: $f''>0$ on $(-\infty,0)$, $f''<0$ on $(0,2)$, $f''>0$ on $(2,\infty)$.

Concave up on $(-\infty,0)$ and $(2,\infty)$; concave down on $(0,2)$. Concavity flips at both, so inflection points at $(0,0)$ and $(2,-16)$.

3

Sanity check with the Second Derivative Test. At $x=3$: $f''(3)=12(3)(1)=36>0$, confirming a local min. At $x=0$: $f''(0)=0$ — test silent, consistent with the First Derivative Test calling it neither.

$$\boxed{\text{local min } (3,-27);\quad \text{inflections } (0,0),\,(2,-16)}$$

4.4 — Curve Sketching

No new theorems here — this section is the synthesis. You run a fixed checklist that pulls together limits, $f'$, and $f''$ into one fully-justified graph. The skill being trained is organization: gather every piece of information before you draw, so the sketch is a conclusion rather than a guess.

The sketching checklist

  1. Domain. Where is $f$ defined?
  2. Intercepts. $y$-intercept $f(0)$; $x$-intercepts where $f=0$ (if easy).
  3. Symmetry. Even ($f(-x)=f(x)$), odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$), or periodic?
  4. Asymptotes. Horizontal (limits at $\pm\infty$), vertical (where $f\to\pm\infty$).
  5. Increase/decrease. Sign chart of $f'$.
  6. Local extrema. First or Second Derivative Test.
  7. Concavity & inflection. Sign chart of $f''$.
  8. Draw. Plot the special points, then connect respecting slope and bend.
Worked Example

Assemble. 4.5 — Optimization Problems

The payoff. An optimization problem hands you a real-world quantity to make as large or as small as possible, plus a constraint that ties the variables together. The calculus is just 4.1 and 4.3 again — the new skill is the setup. The discipline is identical to related rates: write the givens first, substitute numbers last.

The five-step recipe

  1. Picture & variables. Draw it. Name the quantity $Q$ to be optimized and the variables it depends on.
  2. Objective. Write $Q$ as a formula in those variables, straight from the geometry/setup.
  3. Reduce to one variable. Use the constraint to eliminate variables until $Q$ is a function of a single variable. State the domain.
  4. Optimize. Find critical numbers, classify with the First or Second Derivative Test, and check the endpoints of the domain.
  5. Answer. Report the actual quantity asked for, with units, and sanity-check it.
Classic Stewart · Worked Example

The farmer's field (max area, fixed fence)

A farmer has $2400$ ft of fencing and wants to enclose a rectangular field that borders a straight river. He needs no fence along the river. What dimensions enclose the largest area?

1

Picture & variables. Let the side parallel to the river have length $x$ and each of the two perpendicular sides have length $y$. We maximize area $A$.

2

Objective. $$A=x\,y.$$

3

Constraint → one variable. Only three sides are fenced (river replaces the fourth): $x+2y=2400$, so $x=2400-2y$. Substitute: $$A(y)=(2400-2y)\,y=2400y-2y^2,\qquad 0<y<1200.$$

4

Optimize. $A'(y)=2400-4y=0\Rightarrow y=600$. Since $A''(y)=-4<0$, the curve is concave down everywhere, so this critical point is the absolute max (endpoints give $A=0$).

5

Answer. $y=600$ ft, $x=2400-1200=1200$ ft.

$$\boxed{1200\text{ ft (along river)}\times 600\text{ ft},\quad A_{\max}=720{,}000\ \text{ft}^2.}$$

The optimal field spends half the fence on the single long side and half on the two short sides — a recurring theme in these problems.

Economics Capstone applied math

Cobb–Douglas: most happiness for your money

A consumer spends a budget $M$ on two goods at prices $p_x,\,p_y$. Buying $x$ and $y$ units gives utility $U(x,y)=x^{a}y^{b}$ (a "satisfaction score"). Maximize $U$ subject to the budget constraint $p_x x+p_y y=M$.

Roadmap

  1. Constraint → one variable. Solve the budget line for $y$, sub into $U$ to get $U(x)$.

The straight line is the budget (everything you can just afford). The curves are indifference curves (bundles giving equal utility). The optimum is where the highest reachable indifference curve just kisses the budget line — the tangency point.

0

The plan. Solve the budget line for $y$, substitute into $U$ to get a function of $x$ alone, then maximize with logarithmic differentiation. The constant $p_y$ pieces will conveniently not affect where the max occurs.

1

Reduce to one variable. From $p_x x+p_y y=M$, $$y=\frac{M-p_x x}{p_y}.$$ Substitute: $$U(x)=x^{a}\left(\frac{M-p_x x}{p_y}\right)^{b},\qquad 0<x<\frac{M}{p_x}.$$

2

Take logs (logarithmic differentiation). Because $U$ is a product of powers, $\ln$ turns it into a sum: $$\ln U = a\ln x + b\big[\ln(M-p_x x)-\ln p_y\big].$$ The $\ln p_y$ term is constant, so it vanishes on differentiating — that's why the price $p_y$ won't move the optimal $x$.

3

Differentiate and set to zero. Since $\dfrac{U'}{U}=\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln U$ and $U>0$, the max of $U$ occurs where $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln U=0$: $$\frac{a}{x}+b\cdot\frac{-p_x}{M-p_x x}=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{a}{x}=\frac{b\,p_x}{M-p_x x}.$$

4

Solve for $x$. Cross-multiply: $a(M-p_x x)=b\,p_x x \Rightarrow aM=(a+b)\,p_x x$. Hence $$\boxed{\,x^{*}=\frac{a}{a+b}\cdot\frac{M}{p_x}\,}\qquad\text{and symmetrically}\qquad y^{*}=\frac{b}{a+b}\cdot\frac{M}{p_y}.$$

5

Interpret — the famous result. Multiply by the price: the amount spent on good $X$ is $p_x x^{*}=\dfrac{a}{a+b}\,M$. So the optimal strategy is to spend a fixed fraction of your budget on each good, set by the exponents (your "tastes") and independent of the prices. With $a=b$ you split your money 50/50; weight $a$ heavier and good $X$ gets a bigger slice. That constant-expenditure-share property is exactly why economists love Cobb–Douglas.

Tangency, geometrically. At the optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line. Their slopes match: the ratio of marginal utilities equals the price ratio $p_x/p_y$. The visualizer is drawing that kiss.

Classic. Find the dimensions of the rectangle of area $1000\,\text{m}^2$ with the smallest perimeter.

$y=1000/x$, $P=2x+2000/x$, $P'=2-2000/x^2=0\Rightarrow x=\sqrt{1000}\approx31.6$ m $=y$ — a square ($P''>0$ confirms min).

2

Classic. A box with a square base and open top must hold $32000\,\text{cm}^3$. Find the dimensions using the least material.

Base $x$, height $h$: $x^2h=32000\Rightarrow h=32000/x^2$. Material $A=x^2+4xh=x^2+128000/x$. $A'=2x-128000/x^2=0\Rightarrow x^3=64000\Rightarrow x=40$, $h=20$. $$\boxed{40\times40\times20\text{ cm},\ A=4800\ \text{cm}^2.}$$

3

Econ. With $U=x^{0.5}y^{0.5}$, $M=100$, $p_x=5$, $p_y=2$: find $x^{*},y^{*}$ from the boxed formula and confirm the budget split.

$a=b=0.5$, so $x^{*}=0.5\cdot\tfrac{100}{5}=10$, $y^{*}=0.5\cdot\tfrac{100}{2}=25$. Spending $p_x x^{*}=50$ and $p_y y^{*}=50$ — exactly half each. $\checkmark$

Mixed Practice

A spread across the whole chapter, shuffled on each load so you're classifying by reasoning, not by position. Try each before revealing the solution, then move to the Stewart Chapter 4 review, the true/false concept questions, and the Chapter 4 test.

1

Absolute extrema. Find the absolute max and min of $f(x)=x^3-12x+2$ on $[-3,4]$.

4.1 Closed Interval Method

$f'=3x^2-12=3(x-2)(x+2)$, crit $x=\pm2$. $f(-3)=11,\ f(-2)=18,\ f(2)=-14,\ f(4)=18$. $$\boxed{\text{abs max }18\ (x=-2\text{ and }x=4),\ \text{abs min }-14\ (x=2).}$$

2

Shape. For $f(x)=2x^3+3x^2-36x$, find intervals of increase/decrease, local extrema, and the inflection point.

4.3 First & Second Derivative Tests

$f'=6x^2+6x-36=6(x+3)(x-2)$: increasing on $(-\infty,-3)$ and $(2,\infty)$, decreasing on $(-3,2)$. Local max at $x=-3$ ($f=81$), local min at $x=2$ ($f=-44$). $f''=12x+6=0$ at $x=-\tfrac12$: inflection at $\left(-\tfrac12,\,18.5\right)$.

3

Optimization (classic). A rectangular pen is built against a barn (no fence on the barn side) using $60$ m of fencing. Find the dimensions of largest area.

4.5 Optimization

$x+2y=60$ ($x$ = side parallel to barn). $A=xy=(60-2y)y$, $A'=60-4y=0\Rightarrow y=15,\ x=30$. $$\boxed{30\text{ m}\times15\text{ m},\ A_{\max}=450\ \text{m}^2.}$$

4

Concavity callback. Use a tangent-line (linear) approximation at $a=8$ to estimate $\sqrt[3]{8.1}$. Is your estimate an over- or under-estimate, and why?

4.3 Concavity & Linear Approx

$f(x)=x^{1/3}$, $f'(x)=\tfrac13x^{-2/3}$, $f'(8)=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac1{12}$. $L(8.1)=2+\tfrac1{12}(0.1)\approx2.00833$. Since $f''(x)=-\tfrac29x^{-5/3}<0$, $f$ is concave down, so the tangent lies above the curve — an overestimate. (True value $\approx2.00830$.)

5

Optimization (econ). A consumer has utility $U=x^{0.3}y^{0.7}$, budget $M=200$, prices $p_x=10$, $p_y=5$. Find the optimal bundle and the share of the budget spent on each good.

4.5 Cobb–Douglas

$a+b=1$, so $x^{*}=a\cdot\tfrac{M}{p_x}=0.3\cdot\tfrac{200}{10}=6$, $y^{*}=b\cdot\tfrac{M}{p_y}=0.7\cdot\tfrac{200}{5}=28$. Spending: $p_xx^{*}=60$ (30% of budget), $p_yy^{*}=140$ (70%) — the shares equal the exponents. $$\boxed{x^{*}=6,\ y^{*}=28.}$$

6

MVT. Does $f(x)=|x|$ satisfy the hypotheses of the Mean Value Theorem on $[-1,1]$? Explain.

4.2 Mean Value Theorem

No. $f$ is continuous on $[-1,1]$, but it is not differentiable at $x=0$ (corner), so the MVT's differentiability hypothesis fails. Indeed no $c$ works: the secant slope is $0$, but $f'(x)=\pm1$ wherever it exists — never $0$. A hypothesis that fails can break the conclusion.
