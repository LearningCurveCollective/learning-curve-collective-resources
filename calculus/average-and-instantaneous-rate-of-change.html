Motivation: "How fast on average?" vs. "How fast right now?"

You drive 120 miles and it takes 2 hours. Everyone agrees your average speed was $\dfrac{120 \text{ mi}}{2 \text{ hr}} = 60$ mph. But nobody believes you were going exactly 60 the whole time: you sat at lights, you passed a truck at 75, you crawled through a construction zone. "60 mph" is a summary of the whole trip. It is a rate of change (distance per hour) computed from two snapshots: where you were at the start and where you were at the end.

Now look down at the speedometer. It says 43. That number is not a summary of anything; it is your speed at this instant. It is also a rate of change of distance per hour, but it belongs to a single moment, not an interval. The whole of Section 2.1 is the gap between those two numbers and how to compute the second one when all you have is a formula.

Trip odometer + clock = AROC

Two readings, one subtraction, one division. Uses only the endpoints of the interval. This is the average rate of change.

Speedometer = IROC

One instant, one number. No interval, no subtraction. This is the instantaneous rate of change. The puzzle: how do you compute it from a formula with no "two points" to subtract?

How a speedometer actually gets its number. It measures distance over a very short time (a fraction of a wheel rotation) and divides. So it is really an average rate over a tiny interval, so tiny that it is indistinguishable from "right now." That is exactly the calculus idea: instantaneous rate = the value that average rates approach as the interval shrinks to nothing. The rest of this sheet makes that sentence precise.

Physics: a Dropped Ball

A ball is dropped from a 100-foot building. Ignoring air resistance, its height after $t$ seconds is

$$h(t) = 100 - 16t^2 \quad (\text{feet},\ t \text{ in seconds})$$

Why not just set $\Delta t = 0$ from the start? Then the "interval" has zero width and the formula reads $\frac{0}{0}$. The ball did not move and no time passed; no information. You must keep $\Delta t$ alive long enough to cancel it, and only then send it to zero. That is the entire reason limits exist in this chapter.

Medicine: When Does a Dose Peak?

A patient takes a pill. The concentration of the drug in the bloodstream (mg/L) $t$ hours later is modeled by

$$C(t) = 10\,t\,e^{-t/2}$$

The body absorbs the drug (concentration rising) and clears it (concentration falling) at the same time. A doctor wants to know: at what moment does the concentration stop rising and start falling? That is a question about the instantaneous rate of change: the peak is exactly where the IROC switches from positive to negative, i.e. where the tangent line is flat. Drag $t$ and watch the sign.

4

Rate of Change in the Wild

Every field has its own name for "the instantaneous rate of change of $f$." Recognizing the pattern turns a page of vocabulary into one idea.

Physics: velocity and acceleration

$f$ = position. IROC = velocity (m/s). Do it again, $f$ = velocity, and the IROC is acceleration (m/s²). The "speed at this instant" your speedometer shows is a derivative you have been reading your whole life.

Economics: marginal cost, marginal revenue

$f$ = total cost of producing $q$ units. IROC = marginal cost, the cost of "one more unit" (dollars per unit). Same for revenue and profit. A firm maximizes profit where the IROC of profit is zero, exactly like the drug peak above.

Biology: growth rate

$f$ = population (bacteria, people, a tumor). IROC = growth rate (individuals per hour or per year). "The population is growing at 3% per year" is a statement about the IROC relative to the size.

Chemistry: reaction rate

$f$ = concentration of a reactant. IROC = reaction rate (mol/L per second), negative because the reactant is being used up. Chemists literally call it "the rate."

Geometry and engineering: slope and grade

$f$ = elevation along a road. IROC = the grade of the road at that spot (a 12% grade means 0.12 feet up per foot forward). The AROC over a whole mountain pass is the average grade; the sign at the summit says "steepest here."

Any graph at all: the tangent slope

Strip away the context and the IROC is just the slope of the tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at one point. That is the picture the Theory tab builds, and it is the one to hold in your head when the algebra gets heavy.

1

Average Rate of Change (AROC) = Slope of a Secant Line

The average rate of change of a function on an interval is just a slope: how much the output changed, divided by how much the input changed. Nothing about calculus is needed yet, it is the same "rise over run" you have used since algebra, applied to two points that happen to sit on a curve.

$$\text{AROC of } f \text{ on } [a,b] \;=\; \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} \;=\; \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

The line through the two points $(a, f(a))$ and $(b, f(b))$ is called a secant line, so the AROC is exactly the slope of the secant line.

Secant line on [a, b]

The gold line is the secant. Its slope is $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$: rise (dashed vertical) over run (dashed horizontal).

Reading the sign

Positive AROC: the function ended higher than it started. Negative: it ended lower. Zero: same height at both endpoints, even if it moved a lot in between.

Units. AROC always carries units of output per input. If $s(t)$ is in meters and $t$ is in minutes, the AROC is in meters per minute. If $b(w)$ counts cleaning bottles and $w$ counts window panes, the AROC is in bottles per pane. Write the units every time the problem gives them.

Common slip. The interval "$2 \le x \le 5$" means $a = 2$, $b = 5$: the denominator is $5 - 2 = 3$, not 5. And "$[-1, 3]$" has width $3-(-1) = 4$. Subtracting a negative is where most sign errors live.

Instantaneous Rate of Change (IROC) = Slope of the Tangent Line

An average rate needs two points. But what is the rate of change at a single instant, at one $x$-value $a$? With one point you cannot form a slope. The idea: keep one endpoint pinned at $a$, slide the other endpoint toward it, and watch what the secant slopes approach. That limiting value is the instantaneous rate of change at $a$, and the line it describes is the tangent line at $(a, f(a))$.

Drag $h$ toward $0$ and watch the secant slope settle onto the tangent slope (copper). Use Zoom to see why: up close, every smooth curve looks like a straight line.

Two names for the same second point

The $h$-form labels the moving point by its distance from $a$; the $x$-form labels it by its location. Substituting $x = a + h$ turns one into the other.

Definition (two equivalent forms). The instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $x = a$ is

$$\text{IROC at } a \;=\; \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h} \qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad \text{IROC at } a \;=\; \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

Each fraction inside the limit is an AROC on a tiny interval ($[a, a+h]$ or $[a,x]$). The limit is what those average rates settle down to. Later this number gets the name $f'(a)$, "the derivative of $f$ at $a$."

Which form should I use? The problem usually tells you. If it does not: the $h$-form is natural when $f(a+h)$ is easy to expand (polynomials, $1/x$); the $x \to a$ form is natural when $f(x) - f(a)$ factors or has a conjugate (square roots, polynomials whose difference factors as $(x-a)(\ldots)$). Both give the same number, always.

3

Why You Cannot Just Plug In (and the Three Fixes)

Try substituting $h = 0$ directly into $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$. The numerator becomes $f(a) - f(a) = 0$ and the denominator becomes $0$. The form $\dfrac{0}{0}$ is indeterminate: it does not mean the answer is 0, or 1, or undefined; it means the fraction has not been simplified enough to reveal the answer yet. Every IROC problem in this unit is really an algebra problem: rewrite the difference quotient until the $h$ (or the $x - a$) in the denominator cancels, and only then take the limit.

Fix 1: Expand, then cancel (polynomials)

Expand $f(a+h)$ fully. Every term without an $h$ will cancel against $f(a)$, leaving only terms that contain $h$. Factor one $h$ out of the numerator and cancel it with the denominator. Then let $h \to 0$.

Fix 2: Common denominator (rational functions like $\tfrac{1}{x}$)

When $f$ is a fraction, $f(a+h) - f(a)$ is a difference of two fractions. Combine them over a common denominator; the numerator collapses to something with a factor of $h$, which cancels. Watch the sign: $a - (a+h) = -h$.

Fix 3: Multiply by the conjugate (square roots)

Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{a}$ (the conjugate). Since $(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{a})(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{a}) = x - a$, the root disappears from the numerator and the $(x-a)$ cancels. The conjugate stays in the denominator and is safe to plug into.

The number one algebra error. $(a+h)^2 \ne a^2 + h^2$. It is $a^2 + 2ah + h^2$. The cross term $2ah$ is the whole point: it is where the answer comes from. The same goes for $(a+h)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3$.

Reading a Limit Backwards: "Which function, which point?"

The last problem type flips the question. You are handed a limit that is already in one of the two IROC forms, and you are asked to identify the original function $f$ and the point $a$. This is pattern matching, not computation.

$h$-form pattern

$$\lim_{h\to 0} \frac{\;f(\,\underbrace{a+h}_{\text{find }a}\,) - \underbrace{f(a)}_{\text{a plain number}}\;}{h}$$

Look for "something $+\,h$" inside the function: that something is $a$. Whatever is being subtracted is $f(a)$. Replace $a + h$ with $x$ to read off $f(x)$.

$x \to a$ pattern

$$\lim_{x\to a} \frac{\;\underbrace{f(x)}_{\text{expression in }x} - \underbrace{f(a)}_{\text{a plain number}}\;}{x-a}$$

The denominator hands you $a$ directly ($x - a$, so $x + 3$ means $a = -3$). The $x$-expression in the numerator is $f(x)$; the constant being subtracted should equal $f(a)$. Check it!

Verification habit. After you name $f$ and $a$, compute $f(a)$ yourself and confirm it matches the constant in the limit. If the limit is $\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{2(6+h)^2 + (6+h) - 3 - 75}{h}$, then $f(x) = 2x^2 + x - 3$ and $a = 6$, and indeed $f(6) = 72 + 6 - 3 = 75$. That check catches almost every mistake in this problem type, including the trap where the constant has been absorbed into the expression (see Round 3).

1

Average Rate of Change

Type A · AROC of a polynomial on $[a,b]$

Concept

Evaluate the function at both endpoints, subtract (right minus left), divide by the width of the interval. Three numbers, one fraction.

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$$
Worked Example

Find the average rate of change of $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$ on $[1, 4]$.

Givens: $f(x) = x^2 - 3x$, $a = 1$, $b = 4$.

Step 1 — Endpoints. $f(4) = 16 - 12 = 4$ and $f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2$.

Step 2 — Slope.

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{f(4) - f(1)}{4 - 1} = \frac{4 - (-2)}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$$

AROC $= 2$. The secant line through $(1,-2)$ and $(4,4)$ has slope 2.

Practice

Find the AROC of $g(x) = 2x^2 - x$ on $[-1, 2]$.

$g(2) = 8 - 2 = 6$, $g(-1) = 2 + 1 = 3$.

$$\frac{6 - 3}{2 - (-1)} = \frac{3}{3} = 1$$

AROC $= 1$.

Type B · AROC with a calculator function (trig, exponential)

Concept

Same formula, but the endpoint values are not nice numbers. Write the exact expression first, then evaluate. For trig functions the input is in radians unless the problem says otherwise.

Worked Example

Find the average rate of change of $f(x) = e^x$ on $0 \le x \le 2$.

Givens: $f(x) = e^x$, $a = 0$, $b = 2$.

Step 1 — Exact form. $f(2) = e^2$, $f(0) = e^0 = 1$.

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{e^2 - 1}{2 - 0} = \frac{e^2 - 1}{2}$$

Step 2 — Decimal. $e^2 \approx 7.3891$, so $\dfrac{7.3891 - 1}{2} \approx 3.1945$.

AROC $= \dfrac{e^2-1}{2} \approx 3.1945$. (Keep the exact form unless asked for a decimal.)

Practice

Find the AROC of $f(x) = \sin x$ on the interval $0 \le x \le \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Give an exact answer and a decimal.

$$\frac{\sin(\pi/2) - \sin 0}{\pi/2 - 0} = \frac{1 - 0}{\pi/2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.6366$$

AROC $= \dfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.6366$.

Type C · AROC with units

Concept

Identical computation; the answer must be labeled (output unit) per (input unit). A sentence interpretation is often expected: "on average, the [output] changed by ___ [units] for each [input unit]."

Worked Example

A ball's height is $h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t$, where $t$ is in seconds and $h$ is in feet. Find the average rate of change on $[0, 3]$.

Givens: $h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t$ (feet), $t$ in seconds, interval $[0,3]$.

Step 1 — Endpoints. $h(3) = -144 + 192 = 48$ ft, $h(0) = 0$ ft.

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{h(3) - h(0)}{3 - 0} = \frac{48 - 0}{3} = 16 \ \text{ft/s}$$

$16$ feet per second. Interpretation: over the first 3 seconds the ball rose an average of 16 ft each second (this is the average velocity).

Practice

The cost of producing $q$ widgets is $C(q) = 0.5q^2 + 20q + 100$ dollars. Find the AROC of cost on $[10, 20]$, with units.

$C(20) = 200 + 400 + 100 = 700$; $C(10) = 50 + 200 + 100 = 350$.

$$\frac{700 - 350}{20 - 10} = \frac{350}{10} = 35 \ \text{dollars per widget}$$

$\$35$ per widget.

2

IROC with $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$

Type D · Polynomial, $h$-form (expand & cancel)

Concept

Compute $f(a+h)$ by substituting $a + h$ for every $x$, expand, subtract $f(a)$, and every $h$-free term will vanish. Factor out $h$, cancel, take the limit.

Worked Example

Find the IROC of $f(x) = x^2 + 3x$ at $x = 1$ using the $h$-form.

Givens: $f(x) = x^2 + 3x$, $a = 1$.

Step 1 — $f(a)$ and $f(a+h)$. $f(1) = 1 + 3 = 4$.

$$f(1+h) = (1+h)^2 + 3(1+h) = 1 + 2h + h^2 + 3 + 3h = 4 + 5h + h^2$$

Step 2 — Difference quotient. The constant $4$ cancels, as promised.

$$\frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \frac{(4 + 5h + h^2) - 4}{h} = \frac{5h + h^2}{h} = \frac{h(5 + h)}{h} = 5 + h$$

Step 3 — Limit. Now $h = 0$ is safe to substitute.

$$\lim_{h\to 0}(5 + h) = 5$$

IROC at $x = 1$ is $5$.

Practice

Find the IROC of $f(x) = 3x^2 - x$ at $x = 2$ using the $h$-form.

$f(2) = 12 - 2 = 10$. $f(2+h) = 3(4 + 4h + h^2) - (2 + h) = 12 + 12h + 3h^2 - 2 - h = 10 + 11h + 3h^2$.

$$\frac{10 + 11h + 3h^2 - 10}{h} = 11 + 3h \ \xrightarrow{\,h\to 0\,}\ 11$$

IROC $= 11$.

Type E · Rational function, $h$-form (common denominator)

Concept

With $f(x) = \dfrac{c}{x}$-type functions, $f(a+h) - f(a)$ is a difference of two fractions. Put them over the common denominator $a(a+h)$; the numerator becomes a multiple of $h$. Then you are dividing by $h$: flip and multiply, or just cancel.

Worked Example

Find the IROC of $f(x) = \dfrac{2}{x}$ at $x = 1$ using the $h$-form.

Givens: $f(x) = \dfrac{2}{x}$, $a = 1$, so $f(1) = 2$ and $f(1+h) = \dfrac{2}{1+h}$.

Step 1 — Combine the numerator. Common denominator is $1 + h$.

$$f(1+h) - f(1) = \frac{2}{1+h} - 2 = \frac{2}{1+h} - \frac{2(1+h)}{1+h} = \frac{2 - 2 - 2h}{1+h} = \frac{-2h}{1+h}$$

Step 2 — Divide by $h$. Dividing by $h$ is multiplying by $\dfrac{1}{h}$; the $h$'s cancel.

$$\frac{f(1+h)-f(1)}{h} = \frac{-2h}{1+h}\cdot\frac{1}{h} = \frac{-2}{1+h}$$

Step 3 — Limit.

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{-2}{1+h} = \frac{-2}{1} = -2$$

IROC at $x = 1$ is $-2$. (Negative makes sense: $2/x$ is decreasing.)

Practice

Find the IROC of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ at $x = 2$ using the $h$-form.

$$\frac{1}{2+h} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2 - (2+h)}{2(2+h)} = \frac{-h}{2(2+h)}, \qquad \frac{-h}{2(2+h)}\cdot\frac{1}{h} = \frac{-1}{2(2+h)} \ \xrightarrow{\,h\to 0\,}\ -\frac{1}{4}$$

IROC $= -\dfrac{1}{4}$.

3

IROC with $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$

Type F · Polynomial, $x \to a$ form (factor & cancel)

Concept

Compute the number $f(a)$, form $f(x) - f(a)$, and factor it. It is guaranteed to have $(x - a)$ as a factor (that is the Factor Theorem: $x = a$ is a root of $f(x) - f(a)$). Cancel $(x-a)$, then substitute $x = a$.

Worked Example

Find the IROC of $f(x) = x^2 - 4$ at $x = 3$ using the $x \to a$ form.

Givens: $f(x) = x^2 - 4$, $a = 3$, so $f(3) = 9 - 4 = 5$.

Step 1 — Set up.

$$\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{f(x) - f(3)}{x - 3} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{(x^2 - 4) - 5}{x - 3} = \lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2 - 9}{x-3}$$

Step 2 — Factor and cancel. $x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$.

$$\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = \lim_{x\to 3}(x + 3)$$

Step 3 — Substitute. $3 + 3 = 6$.

IROC at $x = 3$ is $6$.

Practice

Find the IROC of $f(x) = x^2 + 2x$ at $x = -3$ using the $x \to a$ form.

$f(-3) = 9 - 6 = 3$. Then $f(x) - 3 = x^2 + 2x - 3 = (x+3)(x-1)$.

$$\lim_{x\to -3}\frac{(x+3)(x-1)}{x+3} = \lim_{x\to -3}(x-1) = -4$$

IROC $= -4$.

Type G · Square root, $x \to a$ form (conjugate)

Concept

A difference of square roots does not factor, so multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate. The numerator becomes $x - a$ (a difference of squares), which cancels the denominator.

$$(\sqrt{x} - \sqrt{a})(\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{a}) = x - a$$
Worked Example

Find the IROC of $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $x = 9$ using the $x \to a$ form.

Givens: $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $a = 9$, $f(9) = 3$.

Step 1 — Set up.

$$\lim_{x\to 9}\frac{\sqrt{x} - 3}{x - 9}$$

Step 2 — Multiply by the conjugate $\dfrac{\sqrt{x}+3}{\sqrt{x}+3}$.

$$\frac{\sqrt{x}-3}{x-9}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}+3}{\sqrt{x}+3} = \frac{x - 9}{(x-9)(\sqrt{x}+3)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+3}$$

Step 3 — Substitute $x = 9$.

$$\lim_{x\to 9}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+3} = \frac{1}{3+3} = \frac{1}{6}$$

IROC at $x = 9$ is $\dfrac{1}{6}$.

Practice

Find the IROC of $f(x) = 3\sqrt{x}$ at $x = 4$ using the $x \to a$ form.

$f(4) = 6$. Factor the 3 out first:

$$\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{3\sqrt{x} - 6}{x-4} = 3\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{\sqrt{x}-2}{x-4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2} = 3\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+2} = \frac{3}{4}$$

IROC $= \dfrac{3}{4}$.

4

Identify $f$ and $a$ from a Limit

Type H · Reverse-engineering the difference quotient

Concept

Match the given limit against one of the two templates. In the $h$-form, the "$a + h$" inside the function tells you $a$; in the $x\to a$ form, the denominator $x - a$ tells you $a$. The constant being subtracted must equal $f(a)$: always verify.

Worked Example

Each limit is the IROC of some function at some point. Identify $f(x)$ and $a$.

(i) $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h}$     (ii) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{x^3 - 8}{x - 2}$     (iii) $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{e^{h} - 1}{h}$

(i) $h$-form. The thing inside is $(3 + h)$, so $a = 3$. Replacing $3 + h$ with $x$ gives $f(x) = x^2$. Check: $f(3) = 9$ matches the subtracted constant.  $f(x) = x^2$, $a = 3$.

(ii) $x\to a$ form. Denominator $x - 2$ gives $a = 2$. Numerator is $f(x) - f(2) = x^3 - 8$, so $f(x) = x^3$. Check: $f(2) = 8$.  $f(x) = x^3$, $a = 2$.

(iii) $h$-form, but where is $a$? Write $e^h = e^{0 + h}$: then $a = 0$ and $f(x) = e^x$. Check: $f(0) = e^0 = 1$, which matches the $-1$.  $f(x) = e^x$, $a = 0$.

Bonus: the values of these limits are $6$, $12$, and $1$ respectively, but the question only asks you to name the function and point.

Practice

Identify $f(x)$ and $a$:   $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{4}}{x - 4}$    and    $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + h\right) - 1}{h}$

First: $x\to a$ form with $a = 4$; $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ and indeed $f(4) = \dfrac{1}{4}$. $f(x) = 1/x$, $a = 4$.

Second: $h$-form with $a = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$; $f(x) = \sin x$ and $f(\pi/2) = 1$ matches. $f(x) = \sin x$, $a = \pi/2$.

Formulas

Average rate of change on $[a,b]$

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$$

Slope of the secant line through $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. Right endpoint minus left, over width. Units: output per input.

IROC at $x = a$ — $h$-form

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$$

Slope of the tangent line at $(a, f(a))$. Substitute $a+h$ for $x$, expand, cancel the $h$, then let $h\to 0$.

IROC at $x = a$ — $x \to a$ form

$$\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

Same number as the $h$-form ($x = a + h$). Factor $f(x)-f(a)$ to pull out $(x-a)$, or use a conjugate, then substitute $x = a$.

Why $\frac{0}{0}$ shows up

$$\frac{f(a)-f(a)}{0} = \frac{0}{0}\ \text{(indeterminate)}$$

Not an answer, a signal: simplify first. The whole job is making the $h$ or $(x-a)$ in the denominator cancel.

Expansions you will need

$$(a+h)^2 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2$$ $$(a+h)^3 = a^3 + 3a^2h + 3ah^2 + h^3$$

Never drop the cross terms. They are the answer.

Conjugate identity

$$(\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{a})(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{a}) = x - a$$

Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{a}$. For $\sqrt{a+h}-\sqrt{a}$, the product is just $h$.

Common-denominator move

$$\frac{1}{a+h}-\frac{1}{a} = \frac{a-(a+h)}{a(a+h)} = \frac{-h}{a(a+h)}$$

The numerator is $-h$, not $h$. Then dividing by $h$ leaves $\dfrac{-1}{a(a+h)} \to \dfrac{-1}{a^2}$.

Factor Theorem shortcut

$$f(x)-f(a) = (x-a)\cdot q(x)$$

For any polynomial $f$, $x = a$ is a root of $f(x) - f(a)$, so $(x-a)$ always divides out. The IROC is $q(a)$.

Procedure Checklists

AROC

  1. Identify $a$ (left) and $b$ (right). "$2 \le x \le 5$" means $a=2$, $b=5$.
  2. Compute $f(b)$ and $f(a)$ separately. Write both down.
  3. $\dfrac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Careful subtracting negatives in both top and bottom.
  4. Attach units (output per input) if the problem has them.

IROC, $h$-form

  1. Compute the number $f(a)$.
  2. Compute $f(a+h)$: replace every $x$ with $(a+h)$, keep parentheses.
  3. Expand / combine fractions. Subtract $f(a)$; the constant terms cancel.
  4. Factor $h$ out of the numerator and cancel with the denominator.
  5. Set $h = 0$ in what remains.

IROC, $x\to a$ form

  1. Compute the number $f(a)$.
  2. Write $\dfrac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$ with the actual expression and number.
  3. Polynomial: factor the numerator; $(x-a)$ will be a factor. Root: multiply by the conjugate.
  4. Cancel $(x-a)$, then substitute $x = a$.

Identify $f$ and $a$

  1. Which template? $h\to 0$ with $h$ downstairs, or $x\to a$ with $(x-a)$ downstairs.
  2. $h$-form: $a$ is the number added to $h$ inside $f$. $x$-form: $a$ comes from the denominator (sign flips: $x+3 \Rightarrow a=-3$).
  3. $f(x)$: replace $(a+h)$ with $x$, or read the $x$-expression directly.
  4. Verify $f(a)$ equals the subtracted constant. If the constant "is missing," it may have been combined into the expression.

Traps to Watch

Subtracting negatives. On $[-1, 3]$ the width is $3 - (-1) = 4$. In $f(3) - f(-1)$, compute $f(-1)$ with parentheses: $(-1)^2 = 1$, not $-1$.

Sign of the common-denominator numerator. $\dfrac{1}{a+h} - \dfrac{1}{a}$ has numerator $a - (a+h) = -h$. Forgetting the minus flips the sign of your final IROC.

Reading $a$ from $x + c$. $\lim_{x\to -3}$ pairs with a denominator of $x + 3$, because $x - (-3) = x + 3$. Do not report $a = 3$.

Constant multiples. For $f(x) = 7\sqrt{x}$ or $f(x) = 3/x$, factor the constant out of the whole limit first: $7\cdot\lim\dfrac{\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{2}}{x-2}$. Less algebra, fewer errors.

Radians. $\sin 5$ means 5 radians. Check the calculator mode before touching a trig AROC.

Symbolic interval. Find the average rate of change of $f(x) = x^2$ on $[a,\ a+h]$ in terms of $a$ and $h$. Then say what happens to your answer as $h \to 0$, and what that number represents.

IROC · $x\to a$ · common denominator + conjugate

$f(4) = \dfrac12$. Two moves are needed: combine the fractions, then kill the root.

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{2 - \sqrt{x}}{2\sqrt{x}}$$ $$\frac{2-\sqrt{x}}{2\sqrt{x}\,(x-4)}\cdot\frac{2+\sqrt{x}}{2+\sqrt{x}} = \frac{4 - x}{2\sqrt{x}\,(x-4)(2+\sqrt{x})} = \frac{-1}{2\sqrt{x}\,(2+\sqrt{x})}$$ $$\lim_{x\to 4}\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{x}(2+\sqrt{x})} = \frac{-1}{2\cdot 2\cdot 4} = -\frac{1}{16}$$

IROC $= -\dfrac{1}{16}$

Choose your form. Find the IROC of $f(x) = x^2 - 4x$ at $x = a$ (leave the answer in terms of $a$), using whichever form you prefer. Then find the $x$-value where the IROC equals $0$.

IROC · general $a$

$$f(a+h) - f(a) = (a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - 4a - 4h) - (a^2 - 4a) = 2ah + h^2 - 4h = h(2a + h - 4)$$ $$\lim_{h\to 0}(2a + h - 4) = 2a - 4$$

Set $2a - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow a = 2$. That is the vertex of the parabola, where the tangent is horizontal.

IROC at $a$ is $2a - 4$; it is zero at $x = 2$

Identify · $h$-form · trig constant

$f(x) = \sin x$, instantaneous rate at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$   (value: $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$)

9

Identify $f(x)$ and the $x$-value, then evaluate the limit. $$\lim_{x\to 16}\frac{\sqrt{x} - 4}{x - 16}$$

Identify + evaluate · conjugate

$a = 16$, $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, $f(16) = 4$ ✓. To evaluate, multiply by the conjugate:

$$\lim_{x\to 16}\frac{\sqrt{x}-4}{x-16}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x}+4}{\sqrt{x}+4} = \lim_{x\to 16}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+4} = \frac{1}{8}$$

$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $x = 16$; the limit equals $\dfrac18$

10

Two right answers. Find two different valid pairs $(f, a)$ for which this limit is the IROC of $f$ at $a$. $$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{(2+h)^3 - 8}{h}$$

Identify · non-uniqueness

Reading 1: $2 + h$ is $a + h$ with $a = 2$, so $f(x) = x^3$; check $f(2) = 8$ ✓.

Reading 2: take $a = 0$, so $a + h = h$, and $f(x) = (x + 2)^3$; then $f(0+h) - f(0) = (2+h)^3 - 8$ ✓.

Both are correct because $(x+2)^3$ is just $x^3$ shifted left by 2; the tangent slope at $x=2$ on one graph equals the tangent slope at $x=0$ on the other. The intended answer on a quiz is Reading 1 (the "obvious" one), but the limit itself does not care.

$f(x) = x^3,\ a = 2$  or  $f(x) = (x+2)^3,\ a = 0$; the limit's value is $12$ either way

12

Concept. Let $f(x) = x^2 - 4x$. Show that the AROC on $[0, 4]$ is $0$, then find the IROC at $x = 1$. Does an average rate of change of zero mean the function "didn't change" on the interval?

AROC vs IROC · interpretation

$f(4) = 16 - 16 = 0$ and $f(0) = 0$, so AROC $= \dfrac{0 - 0}{4} = 0$.

IROC at $1$ (from problem 7, $2a - 4$ with $a = 1$): $2(1) - 4 = -2$. Or directly: $f(1+h) - f(1) = (1 + 2h + h^2 - 4 - 4h) - (-3) = -2h + h^2$, so the quotient is $-2 + h \to -2$.

No. AROC $= 0$ only says the function ended where it started. In between, it dropped to $f(2) = -4$ and climbed back. The secant through the endpoints is flat, but the tangent at $x=1$ has slope $-2$: the function was falling there.

AROC on $[0,4]$ is $0$; IROC at $x=1$ is $-2$; zero average change does not mean no change

13

Concept (preview of a later theorem). For $f(x) = x^2$, find the AROC on $[1, 5]$. Then find the value $c$ in $(1, 5)$ where the IROC at $x = c$ equals that AROC. (Use $2c$ for the IROC of $x^2$ at $c$, from problem 3.)

AROC = IROC · solve for $c$

$$\text{AROC} = \frac{f(5)-f(1)}{5-1} = \frac{25 - 1}{4} = 6$$

Set the IROC equal to it: $2c = 6 \Rightarrow c = 3$, which is inside $(1,5)$.

Geometrically: somewhere on the arc from $(1,1)$ to $(5,25)$ the tangent line is parallel to the secant. For a parabola that spot is always the midpoint of the interval. (This is the Mean Value Theorem, coming later in the course.)

AROC $= 6$; $c = 3$
