Rates of change and the definition

Average rate of change (slope of a secant line) Review #1b

$$\text{average rate of change on }[a,b]=\frac{\color{#BC6C26}{f(b)-f(a)}}{\color{#21442B}{b-a}}=\frac{\color{#BC6C26}{\text{rise}}}{\color{#21442B}{\text{run}}}$$

Key$a,b$ = the interval's endpoints (inputs). $f(a), f(b)$ = the outputs there. Units: output units per input unit (feet per second, etc.).

Plain EnglishOn average, how much did the output change for each 1 unit of input across the whole interval? It is just the slope between two points on the graph.

hover to animate

Derivative at a point, the "h" form (usually Definition 1) Review #2

$$f'(a)=\lim_{\color{#BC6C26}{h}\to 0}\frac{f(a+\color{#BC6C26}{h})-f(a)}{\color{#BC6C26}{h}}$$

Key$a$ = the x-value you want the slope at (a number). $h$ = the horizontal gap from $a$ to a second point $Q$. The fraction is the secant slope from $P=(a,f(a))$ to $Q=(a+h,f(a+h))$.

Plain EnglishFind a secant slope, then slide the second point onto the first by shrinking the gap $h$ to zero. The secant becomes the tangent, and its slope is $f'(a)$.

hover to animate

Derivative at a point, the "x → a" form (usually Definition 2) Review #3

$$f'(a)=\lim_{\color{#BC6C26}{x}\to a}\frac{f(\color{#BC6C26}{x})-f(a)}{\color{#BC6C26}{x}-a}$$

Key$a$ = the fixed x-value (a number). $x$ = a moving x-value sliding toward $a$. Same secant slope as the h form, with $x$ playing the role of $a+h$.

Plain EnglishSame idea, different bookkeeping: let the second point $x$ walk toward $a$. You usually finish by factoring out $(x-a)$ and cancelling it.

hover to animate

The derivative function $f'(x)$ Review #6, #9a

$$f'(x)=\frac{dy}{dx}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(\color{#21442B}{x}+h)-f(\color{#21442B}{x})}{h}$$

Key$x$ stays a letter, so the answer is a formula, not a number. Notation: $f'(x)$, $y'$, $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, $\dfrac{dg}{dx}$ all mean the same thing.

Plain EnglishA slope-machine: plug in any x and it tells you the slope of $f$ there. Plot those slopes and you get a brand new graph, the graph of $f'$.

Square roots in a limit: multiply by the conjugate Review #4

$$\frac{\sqrt{9+h}-3}{h}\cdot\frac{\color{#DDA25E}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}}{\color{#DDA25E}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}}=\frac{h}{h(\sqrt{9+h}+3)}\;\to\;\frac{1}{6}$$

KeyConjugate of $\sqrt{A}-B$ is $\sqrt{A}+B$. Their product is $A-B^2$, which kills the root so the $h$ can cancel.

Plain EnglishPlugging in $h=0$ gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, so you have to do algebra first. With a square root, the algebra is "multiply top and bottom by the conjugate." The answer $\tfrac16$ is the slope of $y=\sqrt{x}$ at $x=9$.

Reading a limit backwards: which $f$, which point? Review #6, #7

$$\lim_{x\to \color{#BC6C26}{5}}\frac{\color{#21442B}{\sqrt{6x-14}}-\color{#DDA25E}{4}}{x-\color{#BC6C26}{5}}\;=\;f'(\color{#BC6C26}{5})\ \text{ for }\ f(x)=\color{#21442B}{\sqrt{6x-14}}$$

KeySee $(x+h)$ with a letter $x$ → it is $f'(x)$. See $(\text{number}+h)$ or $x\to\text{number}$ → it is $f'(\text{that number})$. Check: the subtracted constant must equal $f(a)$ (here $\sqrt{16}=4$).

Plain EnglishMatch the limit to the template. Whatever is being plugged into is $f$; whatever $h$ is added to (or $x$ approaches) is the point.

Tangent line at $x=a$ Review #5, #9b

$$y-\color{#21442B}{f(a)}=\color{#DDA25E}{f'(a)}\,(x-\color{#21442B}{a})$$

KeyPoint $(a, f(a))$ comes from $f$. Slope $m=f'(a)$ comes from $f'$. Point-slope form: $y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$.

Plain English$f'(a)$ IS the slope of the tangent line, the line that just kisses the curve at $x=a$. So "$f'(3)=11$" means the tangent at $x=3$ rises 11 for every 1 to the right.

Normal line at $x=a$ (perpendicular to the tangent) Review #9c

$$y-f(a)=\color{#BC6C26}{-\frac{1}{f'(a)}}\,(x-a)$$

KeyNormal slope = negative reciprocal of the tangent slope. Same point $(a,f(a))$. If $f'(a)=0$ the normal is the vertical line $x=a$.

Plain EnglishSame point, but turned 90°. Flip the slope upside down and change its sign: tangent slope $-7$ gives normal slope $\tfrac17$.

The sign of $f'$ tells you the direction of $f$ Review #10

$$\color{#39864E}{f'(x)>0}\Rightarrow f\text{ increasing}\qquad \color{#BC6C26}{f'(x)<0}\Rightarrow f\text{ decreasing}\qquad f'(x)=0\Rightarrow\text{horizontal tangent}$$

Key"Graph of $f'$ is above the x-axis" means $f'>0$. It says nothing about whether $f$ itself is positive.

Plain EnglishPositive slopes mean the graph of $f$ is going uphill (left to right). Negative slopes mean downhill.

Position and velocity Review #1

$$\color{#DDA25E}{\text{average velocity}=\frac{s(t_2)-s(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}}\qquad \color{#21442B}{v(t)=s'(t)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{s(t+h)-s(t)}{h}}$$

Key$s(t)$ = position (feet), $t$ = time (seconds). Average velocity = secant slope; instantaneous velocity = tangent slope. Units: ft/s.

Plain EnglishAverage velocity is "total distance change over total time." Instantaneous velocity is the speedometer reading at one exact moment, which is a derivative.

Derivative shortcuts for checking slopes beyond the review C&D #3, #4

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(\text{const})=0$$

Key$n$ = the exponent. Use these to get each piece's slope in the piecewise problems (e.g. $x^4-2x^2\to 4x^3-4x$). When a problem says "use the definition," you still have to do the limit.

Plain EnglishShortcuts that give the same answer as the limit. The picture: slide along $\sin x$ and the slopes trace out $\cos x$.

Continuity and differentiability
hover to animate

Continuous at $x=c$: the three conditions C&D #1, #3

$$\text{(1) } f(c)\text{ is defined}\quad \text{(2) }\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)\quad \text{(3) }\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)$$

Key$c$ = the x-value being tested. Any one condition failing gives a discontinuity: hole (1 or 3 fails), jump (2 fails), vertical asymptote (limit is infinite).

Plain EnglishYou can draw through $x=c$ without lifting your pencil: there is a point there, both sides head to the same height, and the point sits exactly at that height.

hover to animate

Differentiable at $x=c$ C&D #1, #3

$$f\text{ continuous at }c\quad\text{AND}\quad \color{#21442B}{\text{left slope}}=\color{#BC6C26}{\text{right slope}}\;\;\big(\lim_{x\to c^-}f'(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f'(x)\big)$$

Key"Left slope" = slope of the piece coming in from the left; "right slope" = from the right. Both must be the same finite number.

Plain EnglishThe graph is smooth at $c$: zoom in far enough and it looks like one straight line. No breaks, no sharp turns, no vertical tangent.

hover to animate

Where a derivative fails to exist C&D #1, #2

$$\underbrace{\text{corner}\quad\text{cusp}\quad\text{vertical tangent}}_{\text{continuous, NOT differentiable}}\qquad \underbrace{\text{hole}\quad\text{jump}\quad\text{asymptote}}_{\text{neither}}$$

KeyCorner: two different slopes ($|x|$). Cusp: slopes go to $+\infty$ and $-\infty$ ($x^{2/3}$). Vertical tangent: slope is infinite ($\sqrt[3]{x}$). Any discontinuity: no derivative at all.

Plain EnglishIf you can't lay one non-vertical ruler on the graph at that spot, there is no derivative there. Watch the ruler rock back and forth.

hover to animate

Differentiable ⇒ continuous (but not the other way) C&D #1

$$\text{differentiable at }c\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{continuous at }c\qquad\quad \text{NOT continuous}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{NOT differentiable}$$

KeySo of the four answers to "continuous, differentiable, both or neither?" only three can happen: both, continuous only, or neither. "Differentiable only" is impossible.

Plain EnglishSmooth implies connected. Connected does not imply smooth ($|x|$ is connected but has a corner).

hover to animate

Piecewise function at the break point $x=c$ C&D #3, #4

$$\text{Step 1: } \color{#DDA25E}{L(c)=R(c)}\ \ (\text{continuous})\qquad \text{Step 2: } \color{#21442B}{L'(c)=R'(c)}\ \ (\text{differentiable})$$

Key$L$ = the left piece's formula, $R$ = the right piece's formula, $c$ = the break point. To find unknowns $a$ and $b$, these two equations are your system.

Plain EnglishFirst make the pieces meet (same height), then make them meet smoothly (same slope). Fail step 1 and you are done: neither.

Facts these problems lean on.

  • $f(a+h)$ means replace every $x$ with $(a+h)$: $f(4+h)=(4+h)^2-3(4+h)+1$, not $f(4)+h$.
  • $(a+h)^2=a^2+2ah+h^2$  ·  $(x+h)^3=x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3$.
  • Perpendicular slopes multiply to $-1$: $m_{\text{tan}}\cdot m_{\text{norm}}=-1$.
  • $\sin 0=0$, $\cos 0=1$, $\cos\!\left(-\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)=-\tfrac12$, $\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}=1$.
  • Units always: rate = (output unit)/(input unit), e.g. ft/s.

From Average Rate of Change to the Derivative

Every derivative on this test starts as a plain slope between two points (a secant line). Push the second point onto the first and that slope becomes the slope of the tangent line, which is what $f'(a)$ means.

Play first

Secant Explorer: $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$ at $a=4$ Review #2, #3

Givens
  • $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$
  • $a=4$, so $P=(4,f(4))=(4,5)$
  • $h$ = gap to the second point $Q=(4+h,\,f(4+h))$

Drag $h$ toward 0 from both sides. The secant slope is always exactly $5+h$, so the slopes close in on 5 from above and below. That is $f'(4)=5$.

Average rate of change Review #1b

$$\text{average rate of change on }[a,b]=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$$

Key: $a,b$ = endpoints of the interval; $f(a),f(b)$ = outputs at those endpoints. Units: output units per input unit.

Example (Review #1b). Givens: $s(t)=16t^2$ feet, $t$ in seconds, interval $[0,2]$. Then $\dfrac{s(2)-s(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac{64-0}{2}=32$ ft/s. Always attach units; "32" alone loses points.

Instantaneous rate of change = the derivative at a point

An average rate needs two points. An instantaneous rate (velocity at exactly $t=2$, slope at exactly $x=4$) only has one point, so we take the average rate over a tiny interval and let the interval shrink to zero. That limit is the derivative. There are two ways to write it:

Which one is "Definition 1"? Most classes call the h form Definition 1 and the x → a form Definition 2. Check your notes. On the test, write the formula you are using as your first line, so the grader sees which definition you mean. Either one gives the same number.

To find $f'(a)$ by the definition Review #1c, #2, #3, #9a

Trigger words: "using the definition," "using a limit," "Definition 1 / Definition 2," "instantaneous velocity," "show your work."

Steps (h form)
  1. Write the template with your $f$ and $a$: $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$.
  2. Find $f(a)$ on the side. It is just a number.
  3. Build $f(a+h)$: put $(a+h)$ in parentheses everywhere $x$ appears, then expand.
  4. Subtract. Every term without an $h$ should cancel. If one doesn't, there is an arithmetic slip.
  5. Factor out $h$ and cancel it with the denominator.
  6. Only now plug in $h=0$. Keep writing "$\lim$" on every line until this step.
x → a form changes

Step 3 becomes "write $f(x)-f(a)$," and step 5 becomes "factor the numerator so an $(x-a)$ appears, then cancel." Example: $x^2-3x-4=(x-4)(x+1)$.

The three algebra moves that get rid of $\tfrac{0}{0}$

1. Expand and cancel $h$ (polynomials)

$\dfrac{(4+h)^2-3(4+h)+1-5}{h}=\dfrac{h^2+5h}{h}=h+5\to 5$.

2. Factor and cancel $(x-a)$ (x → a form)

$\dfrac{x^2-3x-4}{x-4}=\dfrac{(x-4)(x+1)}{x-4}=x+1\to 5$.

3. Multiply by the conjugate (square roots)

$\dfrac{\sqrt{9+h}-3}{h}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{9+h}+3}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}=\dfrac{(9+h)-9}{h(\sqrt{9+h}+3)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}\to\dfrac16$. Leave the bottom unmultiplied; you want the $h$ to cancel.

Notation: all of these mean "the derivative"

$f'(x)$  ·  $y'$  ·  $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$  ·  $\dfrac{dg}{dx}$ (when the function is named $g$)  ·  $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[f(x)\big]$. With a number in it, $f'(4)$ is the slope at $x=4$ only. Review #9a's "Find $\dfrac{dg}{dx}$" wants the whole formula $g'(x)$.

Common exam traps: (1) Writing $f(4+h)$ as $f(4)+h$. It means plug $4+h$ into every $x$. (2) Dropping parentheses: $-(4x^2-5x)$ distributes the minus to both terms. (3) Plugging in $h=0$ before cancelling, which gives $\tfrac00$ and earns nothing. (4) Dropping "$\lim_{h\to0}$" halfway through; teachers take points for that. (5) No units on a rate.

Exam framing. "Find $f'(4)$ using Definition 1" is graded on the setup as much as the answer. A full-credit answer shows: the definition with $f$ and $4$ filled in, $f(4)=5$ computed, $f(4+h)$ expanded, the $h$ factored and cancelled, then the limit evaluated: $f'(4)=5$. Same answer by Definition 2 is a great self-check.

Using the Derivative

Once you have $f'(a)$, it does three jobs: it is the slope of the tangent line, it builds the tangent and normal lines, and its sign tells you whether $f$ is going up or down. This tab also covers reading a limit backwards to see which derivative it is.

Givens

  • $g'(x)=6x+5$ (found by the definition in tab 4, #9a)
  • $x_0$ = the x-value of the point you choose

The view zooms around your point with equal x and y scales so the 90° angle looks like 90°. Slide to $x_0=-\tfrac56$ to see a horizontal tangent (and a vertical normal).

What $f'(a)$ means Review #5

Graphically

$f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(a,f(a))$.

As a rate

$f'(a)$ is the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $x=a$: how fast the output is changing per unit of input right at that moment.

For motion

If $s(t)$ is position, $s'(t)$ is instantaneous velocity, in distance units per time unit.

Model answer, "$f'(3)=11$, explain graphically": "The tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $x=3$ has slope 11, so near $x=3$ the graph is increasing and rising about 11 units for every 1 unit to the right."

To write a tangent or normal line at $x=a$ Review #9b, #9c

Trigger words: "line tangent to," "tangent line at," "line normal to," "perpendicular to the tangent."

  1. Point: $y_1=f(a)$. Use the original function.
  2. Tangent slope: $m=f'(a)$. Find $f'(x)$, then plug in $a$.
  3. Normal slope (only if asked): $m_\perp=-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$.
  4. Point-slope: $y-y_1=m(x-a)$. Leaving it in point-slope form is fine unless told otherwise.

If $f'(a)=0$: tangent is horizontal $y=f(a)$, normal is vertical $x=a$.

The sign of $f'$ Review #10

If on an interval…then the graph of $f$…
$f'(x)>0$ (graph of $f'$ above the x-axis)is increasing (going uphill left to right)
$f'(x)<0$ (graph of $f'$ below the x-axis)is decreasing
$f'(x)=0$ at a pointhas a horizontal tangent there

Model answer, Review #10: "Since $f'(x)>0$ for $-3 \lt x \lt 2$, every tangent line to $f$ on that interval has positive slope, so $f$ is increasing on $(-3,2)$."

Position, height, and velocity Review #1

Review #1 gives $s(t)=16t^2$ as distance fallen, not height. The gumball starts 220 ft up, so its height is $H(t)=220-16t^2$.

  • Height after 2 s: $H(2)=220-64=156$ ft (not 64; 64 ft is how far it has fallen).
  • Using $s$, velocities come out positive (32, 64 ft/s, meaning "downward"). Using $H$, they come out negative ($-32$, $-64$ ft/s). Both are right if you say which one you used.

Common exam traps: (1) Normal slope is the negative reciprocal, not just the negative: $-7\to\tfrac17$, not $7$. (2) Using the formula $f'(x)$ as the slope instead of the number $f'(a)$. (3) Using $f'(a)$ as the y-value of the point; the point uses $f(a)$. (4) Reading "graph of $f'$ above the x-axis" as "$f$ is positive." It means $f$ is increasing. (5) In a "do NOT evaluate" table, evaluating anyway wastes time and may not get credit.

Exam framing. Explanation questions (#5, #10) want three things: name the derivative fact ("slope of the tangent line" or "$f'>0$"), connect it to the graph of $f$ ("increasing"), and give the interval or point ("on $(-3,2)$", "at $x=3$"). One sentence with all three is full credit.

Continuity & Differentiability

Continuous means no breaks. Differentiable means no breaks and no sharp turns or vertical spots, so there is one clear tangent slope. Every question on the homework is "which of these is true at $x=c$?"

Graph 1 of 10

Pick an answer. Open dot = point missing; filled dot = the actual value of $f(c)$.
Score: 0 / 0

Continuity at $x=c$: three conditions C&D #1, #3

1. $f(c)$ is defined

There is a filled-in point at $x=c$. An open circle with nothing else above or below it fails here.

2. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)$ exists

Left-hand and right-hand limits are the same finite number. A jump fails here; so does a vertical asymptote.

3. $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=f(c)$

The point sits exactly where the curve is heading. A hole with the dot moved somewhere else fails here.

Differentiability at $x=c$, and the ways it fails C&D #1, #2

$f$ is differentiable at $c$ when $f'(c)$ exists: the function is continuous at $c$ and the slope coming in from the left equals the slope coming in from the right (and that slope is not infinite).

Feature at $c$Continuous?Differentiable?Why / example
Smooth curveYesYesone tangent slope, e.g. $x^2$
CornerYesNoleft slope $\ne$ right slope, e.g. $|x|$ at 0
CuspYesNoslopes blow up to $\pm\infty$ in opposite directions, e.g. $x^{2/3}$
Vertical tangentYesNoslope is infinite, e.g. $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at 0
Hole (removable)NoNo$f(c)$ missing or misplaced
JumpNoNoleft limit $\ne$ right limit
Vertical asymptoteNoNofunction blows up, e.g. $\frac1x$ at 0

Differentiable ⇒ continuous. Not continuous ⇒ not differentiable. But continuous does NOT guarantee differentiable. So "differentiable only" never happens.

Try it

Make the pieces meet, then meet smoothly C&D #4 (same idea, new numbers)

Givens $$f(x)=\begin{cases}ax^2+1, & x\ge 1\\ 4x+b, & x<1\end{cases}$$
  • Break point $c=1$; right piece $R(x)=ax^2+1$; left piece $L(x)=4x+b$
$$\begin{aligned}\text{slopes: } R'(1)&=L'(1) & 2a(1)&=4 &\Rightarrow a&=2\\ \text{values: } R(1)&=L(1) & a+1&=4+b &\Rightarrow b&=-1\end{aligned}$$

Solve the slope equation first when it has only one unknown.

To check a piecewise function at its break point C&D #3, #4

Trigger words: "numerically," "piecewise," "find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ is differentiable / continuous."

  1. Values: plug $c$ into both formulas (ignore which side gets the "$\le$"). Equal → continuous. Not equal → stop: neither.
  2. Slopes: take the derivative of each formula, then plug in $c$. Equal → both. Not equal → continuous only (a corner).
  3. Unknowns $a,b$: "differentiable" means use both equations as a system. "Continuous" only gives the values equation.

Write it as a mini table so the grader sees your evidence: $L(c)=\_\_$, $R(c)=\_\_$, $L'(c)=\_\_$, $R'(c)=\_\_$, conclusion.

To sketch a function with a given feature C&D #2

Start from the parent shape and slide it so the feature lands on the given point $(h,k)$: a corner comes from $y=|x-h|+k$; a cusp from $y=(x-h)^{2/3}+k$; a vertical tangent from $y=\sqrt[3]{x-h}+k$. Then label the point on your sketch.

Common exam traps: (1) Checking slopes before values. In C&D #3b the values are 3 and 4, so it is neither no matter what the slopes do. (2) Calling a smooth-looking curve with an open circle at $c$ "both"; the hole makes it neither. (3) Calling a cusp or vertical tangent "neither"; the graph is still connected, so it is continuous only. (4) Plugging $c$ into the original pieces to compare slopes; you must differentiate first.

Exam framing. "Numerically" means show numbers, not a picture: "$\lim_{x\to0^-}f(x)=0=\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x)=f(0)$, so $f$ is continuous at 0. Left slope $=1$, right slope $=0$, so $f$ is not differentiable at 0." Conclusion word last: continuous, not differentiable.

#1 The falling gumball Review #1

An object falling from rest has displacement $s(t)=16t^2$ (feet, seconds). A gumball is dropped from the Golden Gate Bridge, 220 ft tall. (a) Height after 2 s? (b) Average rate of change of position on $[0,2]$? (c) Instantaneous velocity at $t=2$?

Givens
  • $s(t)=16t^2$ = feet fallen after $t$ seconds
  • Starting height 220 ft

(a) $s(2)=16(2)^2=64$ ft fallen, so height $=220-64=\boxed{156\text{ ft}}$.

(b) $\dfrac{s(2)-s(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac{64-0}{2}=\boxed{32\text{ ft/s}}$ (downward).

(c) $$\begin{aligned}v(2)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{16(2+h)^2-64}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{64+64h+16h^2-64}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h(64+16h)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}(64+16h)=\boxed{64\text{ ft/s}}\end{aligned}$$

If you model height as $H(t)=220-16t^2$, (b) and (c) come out $-32$ and $-64$ ft/s; the sign just means "down."

#2 $f'(4)$ by Definition 1 Review #2

If $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$, find $f'(4)$ using Definition 1 of the derivative at a point.

Givens
  • $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$, $a=4$
  • $f(4)=16-12+1=5$
$$\begin{aligned}f'(4)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(4+h)-f(4)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(4+h)^2-3(4+h)+1-5}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{16+8h+h^2-12-3h+1-5}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h^2+5h}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}(h+5)=\boxed{5}\end{aligned}$$

If your class calls the x → a form "Definition 1," use the #3 solution here instead. Same answer.

#3 $f'(4)$ by Definition 2 Review #3

If $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$, find $f'(4)$ using Definition 2 of the derivative at a point.

Givens
  • $f(x)=x^2-3x+1$, $a=4$, $f(4)=5$
$$\begin{aligned}f'(4)&=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{f(x)-f(4)}{x-4}=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{x^2-3x+1-5}{x-4}=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{x^2-3x-4}{x-4}\\&=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{(x-4)(x+1)}{x-4}=\lim_{x\to4}(x+1)=\boxed{5}\end{aligned}$$

#4 A limit with a square root Review #4

Find $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sqrt{9+h}-3}{h}$. What does your answer represent?

Givens
  • Direct substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, so do algebra first
  • Conjugate of $\sqrt{9+h}-3$ is $\sqrt{9+h}+3$
$$\begin{aligned}\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sqrt{9+h}-3}{h}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{9+h}+3}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(9+h)-9}{h(\sqrt{9+h}+3)}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{9+h}+3}=\frac{1}{3+3}=\boxed{\tfrac16}\end{aligned}$$

Represents: $f'(9)$ for $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, the slope of the tangent line to $y=\sqrt{x}$ at $x=9$ (the point $(9,3)$).

#5 Interpreting $f'(3)=11$ Review #5

Suppose $f'(3)=11$. Explain what this means graphically.

The line tangent to the graph of $f$ at $x=3$ has slope 11. So at $x=3$ the graph is increasing steeply: right there, $y$ rises about 11 units for each 1 unit increase in $x$.

#7 Write the limit for $f'(\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ Review #7

Write a limit expression that gives $f'(\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ when $f(x)=\sin x$. Do not evaluate.

$$f'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+h\right)-\sin\frac{\pi}{2}}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+h\right)-1}{h}\quad\text{or}\quad\lim_{x\to\pi/2}\frac{\sin x-1}{x-\frac{\pi}{2}}$$

#8 Two points with the same tangent slope Review #8

Find two points on the graph of $y=x^2+6x-7$ where the tangent lines at those points have the same slope.

Givens
  • $y=x^2+6x-7$, a parabola with vertex at $x=-3$
$$y'=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^2+6(x+h)-7-(x^2+6x-7)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{2xh+h^2+6h}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h+6)=2x+6$$

As written, this has no answer. $y'=2x+6$ is a line, so each slope happens at exactly one x-value. No two different points on a parabola share a tangent slope. Ask your teacher what was intended.

Most likely intent: two points whose tangents are equally steep in opposite directions. Any pair symmetric about the vertex $x=-3$ works, e.g. $x=-1$: point $(-1,-12)$, slope $4$; and $x=-5$: point $(-5,-12)$, slope $-4$. The skill being tested is: find $y'$, set up the slope condition, solve for $x$, then plug into $y$ for the points.

#9 Derivative, tangent line, normal line Review #9

Let $g(x)=3x^2+5x$. (a) Find $\dfrac{dg}{dx}$. (b) Find the line tangent to $g$ at $x=-2$. (c) Find the line normal to $g$ at $x=-2$.

Givens
  • $g(x)=3x^2+5x$, $a=-2$
  • $g(-2)=3(4)+5(-2)=2$, so the point is $(-2,2)$

(a) $$\begin{aligned}g'(x)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{3(x+h)^2+5(x+h)-(3x^2+5x)}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{6xh+3h^2+5h}{h}\\&=\lim_{h\to0}(6x+3h+5)=\boxed{6x+5}\end{aligned}$$

(b) Slope $g'(-2)=6(-2)+5=-7$. Tangent: $\boxed{y-2=-7(x+2)}$, i.e. $y=-7x-12$.

(c) Normal slope $=-\dfrac{1}{-7}=\dfrac17$. Normal: $\boxed{y-2=\tfrac17(x+2)}$, i.e. $y=\tfrac17x+\tfrac{16}{7}$.

#10 Graph of $f'$ above the x-axis Review #10

The graph of $y=f'(x)$ is above the x-axis for $-3 \lt x \lt 2$. What does this tell us about the graph of $f$?

$f'(x)>0$ on $(-3,2)$, so every tangent line to $f$ there has positive slope: $f$ is increasing on $(-3,2)$. (It does not tell us whether $f$ itself is positive.)

#4 Find $a$ and $b$ C&D #4

Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f(x)=\begin{cases}ax^2+10, & x\ge2\\ x^2-6x+b, & x<2\end{cases}$ is differentiable.

Givens
  • Break point $c=2$
  • Right piece $R(x)=ax^2+10$, so $R'(x)=2ax$
  • Left piece $L(x)=x^2-6x+b$, so $L'(x)=2x-6$
$$\begin{aligned}\text{Slopes match: } R'(2)&=L'(2) &\;\; 4a&=-2 &\Rightarrow\; a&=-\tfrac12\\ \text{Values match: } R(2)&=L(2) &\;\; 4a+10&=4-12+b &\Rightarrow\; 8&=b-8 \;\Rightarrow\; b=16\end{aligned}$$

$\boxed{a=-\tfrac12,\; b=16}$. Check: both pieces give $f(2)=8$ and slope $-2$ at $x=2$.
