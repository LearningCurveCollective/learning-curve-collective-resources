Where We Left Off

Last session we built every standard differentiation rule from the limit definition. Before we extend them, here's the full toolkit on one card — everything below is assumed knowledge today.

The Rules

Power Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$$

Product Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}[fg] = f'g + fg'$$

Chain Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$$

Today's launch point — we push this much further.

Quotient Rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[\frac{f}{g}\right]=\frac{f'g-fg'}{g^2}$$

The Toolkit Derivatives

Trig

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x,\quad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2 x$$

Exponential

$$\frac{d}{dx}e^x=e^x,\quad \frac{d}{dx}a^x=a^x\ln a$$

Logarithm

$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac{1}{x}$$

Applications

Tangent & normal lines, linear approximation, particle motion ($v=s'$, $a=v'$).

Today's Big Idea: Differentiate Anything

So far you can differentiate anything written as $y = f(x)$ with $x$ alone on the right. Today we remove that restriction four ways:

Nested chain rule
Functions buried three or four layers deep — apply the chain rule repeatedly, working outside-in.
Implicit differentiation
When $y$ is tangled up with $x$ (like $x^2+y^2=25$) and you can't solve for it — differentiate anyway, treating $y$ as a function of $x$.
Inverse functions
Derivatives of $\arcsin$ and $\arctan$, recovered from the functions they undo.
Logarithmic differentiation
A trick using $\ln$ to tame nasty products, quotients, and powers like $x^x$.

The common thread: every one of these is the chain rule wearing a different hat. Keep that in mind and nothing today is really new — just the same idea applied with more nerve.

Nested / Repeated Chain Rule

Concept

When a function is composed several layers deep, apply the chain rule once per layer, peeling from the outside in. Each layer contributes a factor; you multiply them all together.

$$\frac{d}{dx}f(g(h(x))) = f'(g(h(x)))\cdot g'(h(x))\cdot h'(x)$$
Worked Example

Differentiate $y=\sin^2(3x)$, i.e. $\big(\sin(3x)\big)^2$.

1

Identify the three layers. Outermost: $(\;\cdot\;)^2$. Middle: $\sin(\;\cdot\;)$. Inner: $3x$.

2

Outside-in, one factor per layer. Power layer: $2\sin(3x)$. Sine layer: $\cos(3x)$. Inner: $3$.

$$y' = 2\sin(3x)\cdot\cos(3x)\cdot 3 = 6\sin(3x)\cos(3x)$$
Practice

Differentiate $y=e^{\sin(x^2)}$.

Layers: $e^{(\cdot)}$, then $\sin(\cdot)$, then $x^2$. Factors: $e^{\sin(x^2)}$, $\cos(x^2)$, $2x$.

$$y' = e^{\sin(x^2)}\cdot\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x = 2x\cos(x^2)\,e^{\sin(x^2)}$$
✎

Implicit Differentiation

Concept

Sometimes $y$ can't be isolated — like on the circle $x^2+y^2=25$. No problem: differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a hidden function of $x$. Every time you differentiate a $y$, the chain rule tacks on a $\frac{dy}{dx}$. Then solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

The key reflex: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[y^n\big] = n y^{n-1}\dfrac{dy}{dx}$. That trailing $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is just the chain rule — $y$ is the “inside” function.

Worked Example

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2+y^2=25$, then the slope at $(3,4)$.

1

Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$. The $y^2$ picks up a $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$$2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$

2

Solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$$
3

Plug in the point $(3,4)$.

$$\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(3,4)} = -\frac{3}{4}$$
Practice

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$.

Differentiate term by term; the $xy$ needs the product rule: $\frac{d}{dx}(xy)=y+x\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$$2x + \left(y + x\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$$

Collect the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms: $(x+2y)\frac{dy}{dx} = -(2x+y)$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{2x+y}{x+2y}$$
✎

More Practice — Implicit Differentiation

Work these by hand first, then check. Each has a full worked solution.

1

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^3+y^3=6xy$ (folium of Descartes).

Differentiate; $6xy$ needs the product rule: $3x^2+3y^2\frac{dy}{dx}=6y+6x\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Collect: $(3y^2-6x)\frac{dy}{dx}=6y-3x^2$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}$$
2

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $xy+y^2=4$.

Product rule on $xy$: $y+x\frac{dy}{dx}+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$.

$(x+2y)\frac{dy}{dx}=-y$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{y}{x+2y}$$
3

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2y^3=8$.

Product rule: $2xy^3 + x^2\cdot 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx}=0$.

$3x^2y^2\frac{dy}{dx}=-2xy^3$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{2y}{3x}$$
4

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $\sin y + x = y$.

Differentiate: $\cos y\,\frac{dy}{dx}+1=\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$(\cos y-1)\frac{dy}{dx}=-1$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1}{1-\cos y}$$
5

Find the slope of $x^2+y^2=25$ at the point $(3,-4)$.

From the worked example, $\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{x}{y}$. At $(3,-4)$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{3}{-4}=\frac{3}{4}$$

Derivatives of Inverse Functions

Concept

You don't have to memorize these blindly — they fall out of implicit differentiation on the function each one undoes. The two you need for AB/BC:

Arcsine

$$\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$

Arctangent

$$\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$$

The General Rule

There's a single rule behind all inverse-function derivatives — and it comes straight from the chain rule. If $g=f^{-1}$ is the inverse of $f$, then:

$$\frac{d}{dx}f^{-1}(x) = \frac{1}{f'\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)}$$

In words: the slope of the inverse at a point is the reciprocal of the slope of the original function at the matching point. (Geometrically, the inverse is the graph reflected over $y=x$, which swaps rise and run — flipping the slope.)

0

The plan. By definition, an inverse undoes its function: $f\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)=x$. Differentiate both sides — the left side needs the chain rule — and solve for the inverse's derivative.

1

Start from the defining identity.

$$f\big(f^{-1}(x)\big) = x$$

2

Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$. The left side is a composition — chain rule: derivative of the outside $f'$ evaluated at the inside, times the derivative of the inside $f^{-1}$.

$$f'\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\cdot \frac{d}{dx}f^{-1}(x) = 1$$

3

Solve for the inverse's derivative. Just divide.

$$\frac{d}{dx}f^{-1}(x) = \frac{1}{f'\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)}$$

This is the source of the arcsin/arctan formulas. Take $f=\tan$, so $f^{-1}=\arctan$ and $f'=\sec^2$. The rule gives $\frac{1}{\sec^2(\arctan x)}$; using $\sec^2=1+\tan^2$ and $\tan(\arctan x)=x$, that's $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$ — exactly the arctangent formula above. The implicit-differentiation method in the next box is really just this rule in disguise.

Worked Example — no formula needed

Suppose $f$ is invertible with $f(2)=5$ and $f'(2)=3$. If $g=f^{-1}$, find $g'(5)$.

The rule says $g'(b)=\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$ where $b=f(a)$. Here $f(2)=5$ means $a=2,\ b=5$:

$$g'(5)=\frac{1}{f'(2)}=\frac{1}{3}$$

No formula for $f$ required — just the matching point and the reciprocal slope.

0

The plan. Let $y=\arctan x$, flip it to $\tan y = x$, differentiate implicitly, then convert back using a right triangle. Same idea works for $\arcsin$.

1

Rewrite without the inverse. $y=\arctan x \iff \tan y = x$.

2

Differentiate both sides implicitly. Recall $\frac{d}{dx}\tan y = \sec^2 y\,\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$$\sec^2 y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sec^2 y}$$

3

Convert back to $x$. Using the identity $\sec^2 y = 1+\tan^2 y$ and $\tan y = x$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{1+\tan^2 y} = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$$
Worked Example

Differentiate $y=\arcsin(x^2)$ — inverse trig plus the chain rule.

Outside $\arcsin(\cdot)$ gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(\cdot)^2}}$ with inside untouched; multiply by the inside's derivative $2x$.

$$y' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-(x^2)^2}}\cdot 2x = \frac{2x}{\sqrt{1-x^4}}$$
Practice

Differentiate $y=\arctan(3x)$.

$\frac{1}{1+(3x)^2}$ times the inside derivative $3$.

$$y' = \frac{3}{1+9x^2}$$

$f'(x)=5x^4+1$, so $f'(1)=6$. With $f(1)=2$, $a=1$:

$$\big(f^{-1}\big)'(2)=\frac{1}{f'(1)}=\frac{1}{6}$$

Logarithmic Differentiation

Concept

Two situations defeat the ordinary rules: a variable raised to a variable power like $x^x$ (neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies), and giant products/quotients that would be brutal by product and quotient rules. The fix: take $\ln$ of both sides first. Logs turn products into sums and powers into multipliers, and then you just differentiate implicitly.

Why it's legal: it's implicit differentiation on $\ln y$. Since $\frac{d}{dx}\ln y = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}$, once you've differentiated you simply multiply back by $y$ to isolate $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Worked Example

Differentiate $y=x^x$.

1

Take $\ln$ of both sides. The power drops down front.

$$\ln y = \ln(x^x) = x\ln x$$

2

Differentiate both sides w.r.t. $x$. Left side uses the chain rule; right side uses the product rule.

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \ln x + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1$$

3

Multiply back by $y=x^x$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = x^x(\ln x + 1)$$
Practice

Use logarithmic differentiation on $y=(2x+1)^3(x-4)^2$.

$\ln y = 3\ln(2x+1) + 2\ln(x-4)$. Differentiate:

$$\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3\cdot 2}{2x+1} + \frac{2}{x-4} = \frac{6}{2x+1} + \frac{2}{x-4}$$

Multiply back by $y=(2x+1)^3(x-4)^2$:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = (2x+1)^3(x-4)^2\left[\frac{6}{2x+1}+\frac{2}{x-4}\right] = 2(x-4)(2x+1)^2(5x-11)$$

(The factored form is what you'd get after simplifying — either form is correct.)

Related Rates

Concept

When two or more quantities are linked by an equation and they all change over time, their rates of change are linked too. Related rates problems give you one rate and ask for another. The engine is the chain rule: differentiate the equation with respect to time $t$, and every variable picks up a $\frac{d}{dt}$ factor.

The reliable recipe: (1) draw it and name the variables; (2) write an equation relating them; (3) differentiate both sides with respect to $t$; (4) plug in the known values and rates last, and solve for the unknown rate.

The #1 mistake: plugging in numbers before differentiating. Keep the variables symbolic until after you've taken $\frac{d}{dt}$ — otherwise a changing quantity becomes a frozen constant and its rate wrongly disappears.

Worked Example — the sliding ladder

A 10 ft ladder leans against a wall. The bottom slides away from the wall at $\frac{dx}{dt}=1$ ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the bottom is 6 ft from the wall?

1

Relate the variables. Let $x$ = distance of the bottom from the wall, $y$ = height of the top. By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$x^2 + y^2 = 10^2 = 100$$

2

Differentiate with respect to $t$. Each term uses the chain rule:

$$2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0$$

3

Find the missing value. When $x=6$: $y=\sqrt{100-36}=8$.

4

Plug in and solve for $\frac{dy}{dt}$. With $x=6,\ y=8,\ \frac{dx}{dt}=1$:

$$2(6)(1) + 2(8)\frac{dy}{dt}=0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{12}{16} = -0.75 \text{ ft/s}$$

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = -0.75 \text{ ft/s}$$

The negative sign means the top is moving down — exactly what we expect.

Worked Example — the inflating balloon

Air is pumped into a spherical balloon at $\frac{dV}{dt}=100$ cm³/s. How fast is the radius growing when $r=5$ cm?

Relate: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. Differentiate w.r.t. $t$ (chain rule on $r^3$): $\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}$.

Solve for $\frac{dr}{dt}$ and plug in $r=5$, $\frac{dV}{dt}=100$:

$$\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{100}{4\pi(25)} = \frac{1}{\pi} \approx 0.318 \text{ cm/s}$$
Practice

A circular oil slick expands so its radius grows at $\frac{dr}{dt}=2$ m/min. How fast is the area growing when $r=3$ m? (Area $A=\pi r^2$.)

Differentiate $A=\pi r^2$ w.r.t. $t$: $\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r\frac{dr}{dt}$.

Plug in $r=3$, $\frac{dr}{dt}=2$:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi(3)(2) = 12\pi \approx 37.7 \text{ m}^2/\text{min}$$
✎

Linear Approximations & Differentials

Concept

Near a point, a curve and its tangent line are nearly indistinguishable. So the tangent line is a quick stand-in for the function — the linear approximation (or linearization):

$$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \quad\approx\quad f(x) \text{ for } x \text{ near } a$$

Drag the sliders below: the green tangent line hugs the curve near the touch point and drifts away as you move out. That gap is your approximation error.

Curve $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, tangent $L(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)$. Error stays tiny near $a$ and grows as $x$ moves away.

Worked Example

Use a linear approximation at $a=16$ to estimate $\sqrt{16.5}$.

$f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$. At $a=16$: $f(16)=4$, $f'(16)=\frac{1}{8}$.

$$L(x)=4+\tfrac18(x-16) \;\Longrightarrow\; L(16.5)=4+\tfrac18(0.5)=4.0625$$

$$\sqrt{16.5}\approx 4.0625 \quad(\text{actual }\approx 4.0620)$$

Since $\sqrt{x}$ is concave down ($f''<0$), the tangent sits above the curve — a slight over-estimate. The sign of $f''$ tells you the error's direction for free.

Differentials

The same idea in “differential” notation. If $y=f(x)$, a small input change $dx$ produces an approximate output change:

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx \quad\approx\quad \Delta y \text{ (the true change)}$$

It's the linear approximation rewritten: $dy$ is the rise along the tangent line for a run of $dx$, a stand-in for the true change $\Delta y$ along the curve.

Practice

Use differentials to estimate the change in $y=x^2$ when $x$ goes from $5$ to $5.1$. Compare to the true change.

$dy=f'(x)\,dx = 2x\,dx$. At $x=5$, $dx=0.1$: $dy = 2(5)(0.1)=1.0$.

True change: $\Delta y = 5.1^2 - 5^2 = 26.01 - 25 = 1.01$. The differential $dy=1.0$ is a close estimate — off by just $0.01$.

$$dy = 1.0, \qquad \Delta y = 1.01$$
✎

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $\sin(xy)=x$.

Implicit + chain + product

Differentiate: $\cos(xy)\cdot\frac{d}{dx}(xy)=1$, and $\frac{d}{dx}(xy)=y+x\frac{dy}{dx}$.

$$\cos(xy)\left(y+x\frac{dy}{dx}\right)=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{1-y\cos(xy)}{x\cos(xy)}$$

Differentiate $y=x\arctan x$.

Product + inverse trig

Product rule with $f=x$, $g=\arctan x$ ($g'=\frac{1}{1+x^2}$).

$$y' = \arctan x + \frac{x}{1+x^2}$$

Differentiate $y=x^{\sin x}$.

Logarithmic

$\ln y = \sin x\,\ln x$. Differentiate (product rule on right): $\frac{1}{y}y' = \cos x\,\ln x + \frac{\sin x}{x}$.

$$y' = x^{\sin x}\left(\cos x\,\ln x + \frac{\sin x}{x}\right)$$

5

Tangent line via implicit differentiation. Find the equation of the tangent line to the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at the point $(3, 4)$.

Implicit + tangent line

From implicit differentiation, $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$, so at $(3,4)$ the slope is $-\frac{3}{4}$. Point-slope:

$$y = -\tfrac{3}{4}(x - 3) + 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = -\tfrac{3}{4}x + \tfrac{25}{4}$$
6

Slope on a curve you can't solve. The folium of Descartes is $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$. Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$, and the slope of the tangent at $(3, 3)$.

Implicit + product rule

Differentiate both sides; the $6xy$ needs the product rule: $3x^2 + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Collect: $(3y^2 - 6x)\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y - 3x^2$, so

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6y - 3x^2}{3y^2 - 6x} = \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}, \qquad \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{(3,3)} = -1$$
7

Derivative of an inverse function (conceptual). Let $g$ be the inverse of $f$, where $f$ is differentiable with $f(2) = 5$ and $f'(2) = 3$. Find $g'(5)$. Explain why you don't need a formula for $f$.

Inverse function theorem

The inverse-function rule says $g'(b) = \dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$ where $b = f(a)$. Here $f(2) = 5$ means $a = 2,\ b = 5$, so:

$$g'(5) = \frac{1}{f'(2)} = \frac{1}{3}$$

You never need a formula because an inverse just swaps inputs and outputs — its graph is $f$'s graph reflected over $y = x$, which flips rise and run, so the slope at the mirrored point is the reciprocal. This is exactly how the $\arcsin$/$\arctan$ derivatives were born.

8

Why log differentiation? A student tries to differentiate $y = x^x$ using the power rule, getting $x\cdot x^{x-1} = x^x$. Explain why this is wrong, and give the correct derivative.

Conceptual: which rule applies

The power rule $\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$ requires the exponent $n$ to be a constant. Here the exponent is $x$ — it varies — so the rule simply doesn't apply. (Likewise the exponential rule $\frac{d}{dx}a^x$ needs a constant base.) Since $x^x$ has a variable in both spots, neither shortcut works, and you must take $\ln$ first:

$$y' = x^x(\ln x + 1)$$

Sanity check: at $x = 1$ this gives $1\cdot(0 + 1) = 1$, whereas the wrong answer gives $1^1 = 1$ too — they happen to agree at $x=1$ but disagree everywhere else (e.g. at $x=2$: correct $\approx 6.77$, wrong $= 4$).

9

Triple-nested chain. Differentiate $y = \sqrt{1 + \tan(2x)}$.

Nested chain (3 layers)

Layers: $\sqrt{\;\cdot\;}$, then $1 + \tan(\cdot)$, then $2x$. Factors: $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\tan(2x)}}$, then $\sec^2(2x)$, then $2$.

$$y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\tan(2x)}}\cdot \sec^2(2x)\cdot 2 = \frac{\sec^2(2x)}{\sqrt{1+\tan(2x)}}$$
10

Clean surprise. Show that $y = x\arcsin x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ has derivative $y' = \arcsin x$.

Product + inverse trig + chain

First term by product rule: $\frac{d}{dx}[x\arcsin x] = \arcsin x + \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.

Second term by chain rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{1-x^2} = \dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.

Add them — the two fraction terms cancel exactly:

$$y' = \arcsin x + \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} - \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \arcsin x$$
