The rules at a glance

Everything on this sheet comes down to a short list of rules. Know each one cold, know when it applies, and know the algebra that gets a messy expression into a shape the rule can handle. Tap any study-guide tab for the full story behind a rule; use the flashcards at the bottom of this page to drill them.

Power rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{n}\right] = n\,x^{\,n-1}\quad\text{for any real } n$$

Works for negative and fractional exponents too: $x^{-3}\to -3x^{-4}$, $x^{1/2}\to \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$.

Constants, constant multiples, sums

$$\frac{d}{dx}[c]=0 \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[c\,f]=c\,f' \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[f\pm g]=f'\pm g'$$

Differentiate term by term. A lone constant disappears; a constant coefficient rides along.

Product rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[u\,v\right] = u'\,v + u\,v'$$

"First times derivative of second, plus second times derivative of first." Order of the two terms does not matter.

Quotient rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u}{v}\right] = \frac{u'\,v - u\,v'}{v^{2}}$$

"Lo d-hi minus hi d-lo, over lo squared." Here the order in the numerator does matter.

Sine and cosine

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x]=\cos x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\cos x]=-\sin x$$

Derivatives of $\sin x$ cycle every four steps: $\sin\to\cos\to-\sin\to-\cos\to\sin$.

Tangent and secant

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x]=\sec^{2}x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\sec x]=\sec x\,\tan x$$

Both follow from the quotient rule applied to $\tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$ and $\tfrac{1}{\cos x}$.

Cotangent and cosecant

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\cot x]=-\csc^{2}x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\csc x]=-\csc x\,\cot x$$

Every "co" function picks up a minus sign. Swap $\sin\leftrightarrow\cos$, $\tan\leftrightarrow\cot$, $\sec\leftrightarrow\csc$ and negate.

Derivative at a point (recognize the limit)

$$f'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

If a limit has this shape, do not evaluate it; identify $f$ and $a$ and write down $f'(a)$.

Tangent line at $x=a$

$$y - f(a) = f'(a)\,(x-a)$$

Slope of the tangent $=$ derivative $=$ instantaneous rate of change. Three names for one number.

Differentiable at $x=a$ (piecewise)

$$f_{L}(a) = f_{R}(a) \qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{L}'(a) = f_{R}'(a)$$

Left piece and right piece must agree in value (continuity) and in derivative (matching slopes). Two equations, usually two unknowns. Differentiable implies continuous; the reverse is false.

Which rule do I use?

Decision tree: read the expression before you differentiate

Can it be rewritten as a sum of terms $c\,x^{n}$? Radicals, fractions with a monomial denominator, products you can expand, a single factor on top of $x^{k}$.

Yes: rewrite, then power rule term by term. This is the fastest route and the one most students skip.

Two non-constant pieces multiplied, and expanding is ugly or impossible? $(\sqrt{x}+1)(x^{3}-2x)$, $x^{2}\sin x$, $f(x)\,g(x)$ from a table.

Product rule: $u'v+uv'$.

A fraction whose denominator is not a monomial? $\dfrac{4x+1}{2x-3}$, $\dfrac{\cos x}{1+\sin x}$, $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)+1}$.

Quotient rule: $\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^{2}}$. Check first whether the fraction simplifies (node 1).

Trig functions present?

Use the six trig derivatives as the "$u'$" and "$v'$" inside whichever rule you are already in. $\tan x$, $\sec x$, $\cot x$, $\csc x$ are single functions with their own derivatives; do not rewrite them as quotients unless you are proving the formula.

Asked for a number, not a formula? "$f'(4)$", "slope at $x=2$", "rate of change at $t=3$", table or graph values.

Differentiate in general first (or set up the rule with the given values), then substitute. Never substitute before differentiating.

A function inside another function? $(2x+3)^{-1}$, $\sin(2x)$, $(x+1)^{3}$.

That is chain rule territory (the next chapter). For now, expand or split it so a product or quotient rule applies: $(x+1)^{3}=(x+1)^{2}(x+1)$, $\sin 2x=2\sin x\cos x$.

Watch out: the five classic errors

1. Differentiating each factor and multiplying. $\frac{d}{dx}[x\cdot x]$ is $2x$, not $1\cdot 1 = 1$. Products need the product rule (or expand first).

2. Flipping the quotient-rule numerator. It is $u'v - uv'$: the derivative of the top comes first. Reversing the order flips the sign of your whole answer.

3. Substituting before differentiating. If $f(x)=x^{2}$, then $f(3)=9$ and $\frac{d}{dx}[9]=0$, but $f'(3)=6$. Differentiate the function, then plug in.

4. Dropping the sign on co-functions. $\frac{d}{dx}[\cos x]=-\sin x$, $\frac{d}{dx}[\cot x]=-\csc^{2}x$, $\frac{d}{dx}[\csc x]=-\csc x\cot x$.

5. Power rule on $\sqrt{x}$ without converting. You cannot "bring down" a root. Write $x^{1/2}$ and then apply $n x^{n-1}$.

The power rule and its helpers

Power rule

Concept

For any real exponent $n$, the derivative of $x^{n}$ is found by bringing the exponent down as a coefficient and reducing the exponent by one.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{n}\right] = n\,x^{\,n-1}$$

Three helper rules let you handle any polynomial-style expression one term at a time:

$$\frac{d}{dx}[c] = 0 \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[c\,f(x)\right] = c\,f'(x) \qquad\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[f(x)\pm g(x)\right] = f'(x)\pm g'(x)$$

Why does a constant have derivative zero? A derivative measures how fast the output changes, and a constant never changes. Why does a coefficient ride along? Scaling a function by $c$ scales every slope by $c$.

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = -2x^{3} - x^{2} + 4x - 7$.

Differentiate term by term. The constant $-7$ disappears, and $4x = 4x^{1}$ becomes $4\cdot 1\cdot x^{0} = 4$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -2\cdot 3x^{2} - 2x + 4 = \boxed{-6x^{2} - 2x + 4}$$
Practice

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = 4x^{3} - 7x^{2} + 2x - 11$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 12x^{2} - 14x + 2$$

Rewrite first, differentiate second

Radicals and fractions are powers in disguise

Concept

The power rule only recognizes the form $x^{n}$. Before differentiating, translate every root into a fractional exponent and every reciprocal into a negative exponent:

$$\sqrt[k]{x^{m}} = x^{m/k} \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{x^{m}} = x^{-m} \qquad\qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt[k]{x^{m}}} = x^{-m/k}$$

After differentiating, you may leave negative and fractional exponents as they are, or convert back to radical form. Multiple-choice answers are usually written in whichever form is cleanest, so be comfortable reading both.

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = 2x^{4} - \dfrac{3}{x} + \sqrt[5]{x} - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}}$.

Step 1. Rewrite each term as a power of $x$.

$$y = 2x^{4} - 3x^{-1} + x^{1/5} - x^{-2/3}$$

Step 2. Apply the power rule to each term. Watch the signs: $-3x^{-1}$ becomes $-3\cdot(-1)x^{-2} = +3x^{-2}$, and $-x^{-2/3}$ becomes $-\left(-\tfrac23\right)x^{-5/3} = +\tfrac23 x^{-5/3}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x^{3} + 3x^{-2} + \tfrac15 x^{-4/5} + \tfrac23 x^{-5/3}$$

Step 3 (optional). Convert back.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x^{3} + \frac{3}{x^{2}} + \frac{1}{5\sqrt[5]{x^{4}}} + \frac{2}{3\sqrt[3]{x^{5}}}$$
Practice

(a) Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = x^{4} + \dfrac{5}{x} - \sqrt{x} + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[4]{x}}$.

(b) Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = -\dfrac{2}{x^{4}} + \dfrac{3}{\sqrt[3]{x}} - \sqrt[5]{x^{3}}$.

(a) Rewrite: $y = x^{4} + 5x^{-1} - x^{1/2} + x^{-1/4}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 4x^{3} - 5x^{-2} - \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} - \tfrac14 x^{-5/4}$$

(b) Rewrite: $y = -2x^{-4} + 3x^{-1/3} - x^{3/5}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 8x^{-5} - x^{-4/3} - \tfrac35 x^{-2/5}$$

Simplify before you differentiate

Concept

A product on top of a monomial looks like it needs the quotient rule. It usually does not. Expand the numerator, split the fraction into separate terms, and the whole thing collapses to the power rule.

Test: is the denominator a single term like $x^{3}$? Then divide it into each term of the numerator. If the denominator is a sum like $x^{2}+1$, you are stuck with the quotient rule (Tab 2).

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{(x+2)\left(x^{2}-2x+4\right)}{x^{2}}$.

Step 1. Expand the numerator. This is the sum-of-cubes pattern $(a+b)(a^{2}-ab+b^{2}) = a^{3}+b^{3}$ with $a=x$, $b=2$.

$$(x+2)(x^{2}-2x+4) = x^{3} + 8$$

Step 2. Divide each term by $x^{2}$.

$$y = \frac{x^{3}}{x^{2}} + \frac{8}{x^{2}} = x + 8x^{-2}$$

Step 3. Power rule.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 - 16x^{-3} = \boxed{1 - \frac{16}{x^{3}}}$$

Compare that with running the quotient rule on the original: the same answer, after three lines of extra algebra and three extra chances to drop a sign.

Practice

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{(x^{2}-4)(x^{2}+4)}{x^{3}}$.

Difference of squares: $(x^{2}-4)(x^{2}+4) = x^{4}-16$, so $y = x - 16x^{-3}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 + 48x^{-4} = 1 + \frac{48}{x^{4}}$$

Derivatives at a point: slope and rate of change

One number, three names

Concept

The value $f'(a)$ is simultaneously the slope of the tangent line to $y=f(x)$ at $x=a$, the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $a$, and the limit of average rates of change on shrinking intervals around $a$. Test questions rotate among these phrasings; the computation is identical.

Procedure. (1) Rewrite if needed. (2) Differentiate to get $f'(x)$ in general. (3) Only then substitute $x=a$.

Substituting first always produces $0$, because $f(a)$ is a constant. If your "derivative at a point" came out to zero suspiciously fast, this is probably why.

Worked Example

If $f(x) = \sqrt{x} - \dfrac{4}{\sqrt{x}}$, find $f'(9)$.

Step 1. Rewrite. $f(x) = x^{1/2} - 4x^{-1/2}$.

Step 2. Differentiate.

$$f'(x) = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} + 2x^{-3/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} + \frac{2}{x\sqrt{x}}$$

Step 3. Substitute $x = 9$. Since $\sqrt{9}=3$ and $9\sqrt{9}=27$:

$$f'(9) = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{2}{27} = \frac{9}{54} + \frac{4}{54} = \boxed{\frac{13}{54}}$$
Worked Example

A particle's position is $s(t) = 3t^{2} - \dfrac{8}{t}$ for $t>0$. What is the instantaneous rate of change of position at $t = 2$?

"Instantaneous rate of change at $t=2$" means $s'(2)$. Rewrite $s(t) = 3t^{2} - 8t^{-1}$, then

$$s'(t) = 6t + 8t^{-2} = 6t + \frac{8}{t^{2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s'(2) = 12 + \frac{8}{4} = \boxed{14}$$
Practice

(a) If $f(x) = 9\sqrt{x} + 3x + \dfrac{8}{x} + 2$, find $f'(4)$.

(b) If $s(t) = t^{3} - \dfrac{12}{t^{2}}$, find the instantaneous rate of change of $s$ at $t = 2$.

(a) $f'(x) = \tfrac92 x^{-1/2} + 3 - 8x^{-2}$, so $f'(4) = \tfrac94 + 3 - \tfrac12 = \dfrac{19}{4}$.

(b) $s'(t) = 3t^{2} + 24t^{-3}$, so $s'(2) = 12 + 3 = 15$.

Higher-order derivatives

Differentiate again (and again)

Concept

The derivative of a derivative is the second derivative, written $f''(x)$, $y''$, or $\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}$. Keep going for $f'''(x)$, and from the fourth derivative on, use the compact notation $f^{(4)}(x)$, $f^{(n)}(x)$.

For a polynomial of degree $d$, each derivative lowers the degree by one, so $f^{(d)}(x)$ is a constant and $f^{(d+1)}(x) = 0$.

Worked Example

If $f(x) = x^{5} - 2x^{3}$, find $f'''(x)$.

$$f'(x) = 5x^{4} - 6x^{2} \qquad f''(x) = 20x^{3} - 12x \qquad f'''(x) = \boxed{60x^{2} - 12}$$
Practice

If $f(x) = 2x^{6} - x^{4} + 3x$, find $f^{(4)}(x)$.

$$f' = 12x^{5} - 4x^{3} + 3,\quad f'' = 60x^{4} - 12x^{2},\quad f''' = 240x^{3} - 24x,\quad f^{(4)}(x) = 720x^{2} - 24$$

The product rule

Derivative of a product

Concept

If $u$ and $v$ are both differentiable functions of $x$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[u\,v\right] = u'\,v + u\,v'$$

Read it as "derivative of the first, times the second, plus the first, times derivative of the second." Because addition commutes, you can write the two pieces in either order; what matters is that each piece contains exactly one derivative.

The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Check with $x\cdot x = x^{2}$: the product rule gives $1\cdot x + x\cdot 1 = 2x$, which is right. Multiplying the derivatives gives $1\cdot 1 = 1$, which is wrong.

When to expand instead. If both factors are polynomials, expanding and using the power rule is often just as fast and gives a cleaner answer. Use the product rule when one factor is a radical, a trig function, or an unknown function $f(x)$ you only know through a table.

Worked Example

Find $f'(x)$ if $f(x) = (3x+2)\left(x^{3}-5\right)$. Then confirm by expanding.

Step 1. Name the pieces. $u = 3x+2$, $u' = 3$; $v = x^{3}-5$, $v' = 3x^{2}$.

Step 2. Product rule.

$$f'(x) = (3)\left(x^{3}-5\right) + (3x+2)\left(3x^{2}\right)$$

Step 3. Simplify.

$$f'(x) = 3x^{3} - 15 + 9x^{3} + 6x^{2} = \boxed{12x^{3} + 6x^{2} - 15}$$

Check by expanding first. $f(x) = 3x^{4} + 2x^{3} - 15x - 10$, so $f'(x) = 12x^{3} + 6x^{2} - 15$. Same answer.

Worked Example

Find $f'(x)$ if $f(x) = \left(\sqrt{x}-3\right)\left(2x^{3}+x\right)$.

Here expanding would create $x^{7/2}$ and $x^{3/2}$ terms; the product rule is tidier. $u = x^{1/2}-3$, so $u' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$; $v = 2x^{3}+x$, so $v' = 6x^{2}+1$.

$$f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\left(2x^{3}+x\right) + \left(\sqrt{x}-3\right)\left(6x^{2}+1\right)$$

On a multiple-choice test, this unsimplified form is usually enough to match an answer choice. On free response, leave it unless the problem asks you to simplify.

Practice

(a) $f(x) = \left(x^{2}+3x\right)(2x-1)$. Find $f'(x)$ two ways: product rule, then expand-and-differentiate.

(b) $f(x) = \left(\sqrt{x}+1\right)\left(x^{3}-2x\right)$. Find $f'(x)$.

(a) Product rule: $(2x+3)(2x-1) + \left(x^{2}+3x\right)(2) = 4x^{2}+4x-3 + 2x^{2}+6x = 6x^{2}+10x-3$. Expanding: $f(x) = 2x^{3}+5x^{2}-3x$, so $f'(x) = 6x^{2}+10x-3$.

(b) $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\left(x^{3}-2x\right) + \left(\sqrt{x}+1\right)\left(3x^{2}-2\right)$.

The quotient rule

Derivative of a quotient

Concept

If $u$ and $v$ are differentiable and $v\neq 0$, then

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u}{v}\right] = \frac{u'\,v - u\,v'}{v^{2}}$$

Mnemonic: "lo d-hi minus hi d-lo, over lo squared." Unlike the product rule, the subtraction makes the order essential: the derivative of the numerator comes first.

Special case worth memorizing. When the numerator is a constant, $u' = 0$ and the rule collapses:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{v}\right] = \frac{0\cdot v - 1\cdot v'}{v^{2}} = -\frac{v'}{v^{2}}$$
Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{4x+1}{2x-3}$.

Step 1. Name the pieces. $u = 4x+1$, $u' = 4$; $v = 2x-3$, $v' = 2$.

Step 2. Quotient rule.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(4)(2x-3) - (4x+1)(2)}{(2x-3)^{2}}$$

Step 3. Simplify the numerator only. Leave the denominator factored.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{8x - 12 - 8x - 2}{(2x-3)^{2}} = \boxed{\frac{-14}{(2x-3)^{2}}}$$

The $x$ terms cancel in the numerator. That always happens for a ratio of two linear functions: the derivative is a constant over the denominator squared.

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{x^{2}}{1+x^{3}}$.

$u = x^{2}$, $u' = 2x$; $v = 1+x^{3}$, $v' = 3x^{2}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x\left(1+x^{3}\right) - x^{2}\left(3x^{2}\right)}{\left(1+x^{3}\right)^{2}} = \frac{2x + 2x^{4} - 3x^{4}}{\left(1+x^{3}\right)^{2}} = \boxed{\frac{2x - x^{4}}{\left(1+x^{3}\right)^{2}}}$$
Worked Example

What is the slope of the line tangent to $f(x) = \dfrac{x^{2}-5}{x+1}$ at $x = 2$?

Differentiate in general, then substitute.

$$f'(x) = \frac{2x\,(x+1) - \left(x^{2}-5\right)(1)}{(x+1)^{2}}$$

At $x = 2$: $2x(x+1) = 4\cdot 3 = 12$, $x^{2}-5 = -1$, $(x+1)^{2} = 9$.

$$f'(2) = \frac{12 - (-1)}{9} = \boxed{\frac{13}{9}}$$

Tip: once you know you only need $f'(2)$, you can substitute $x=2$ into each piece of the quotient-rule template rather than simplifying the general formula. Just make sure every piece is a derivative-or-value after differentiating.

Practice

(a) $y = \dfrac{5x-2}{3x+4}$    (b) $y = \dfrac{x^{3}}{2-x^{2}}$    (c) $y = \dfrac{(x+3)(x-3)}{(x+1)(x-1)}$ (hint: multiply out top and bottom first)

(a) $\dfrac{5(3x+4) - (5x-2)(3)}{(3x+4)^{2}} = \dfrac{26}{(3x+4)^{2}}$

(b) $\dfrac{3x^{2}\left(2-x^{2}\right) - x^{3}(-2x)}{\left(2-x^{2}\right)^{2}} = \dfrac{6x^{2} - x^{4}}{\left(2-x^{2}\right)^{2}}$

(c) $y = \dfrac{x^{2}-9}{x^{2}-1}$, so $y' = \dfrac{2x\left(x^{2}-1\right) - \left(x^{2}-9\right)(2x)}{\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{2}} = \dfrac{16x}{\left(x^{2}-1\right)^{2}}$

Product and quotient rules from a table

Unknown functions, known values

Concept

A table gives you $f(a)$, $f'(a)$, $g(a)$, $g'(a)$ for a specific $a$. You never see the formulas for $f$ and $g$, and you do not need them. Write the rule symbolically, then substitute the four numbers.

If $h = f\cdot g$:   $h'(a) = f'(a)\,g(a) + f(a)\,g'(a)$

If $k = \dfrac{f}{g}$:   $k'(a) = \dfrac{f'(a)\,g(a) - f(a)\,g'(a)}{\left[g(a)\right]^{2}}$

Only use values from the row matching the $x$ in the question. A table with rows for $x=1$ and $x=3$ is a classic trap: the $x = 1$ row is there to be ignored when the question asks about $x=3$.

Worked Example

Using the table, find (a) $h'(2)$ if $h(x) = f(x)\,g(x)$; (b) $k'(2)$ if $k(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$; (c) $m'(2)$ if $m(x) = \left(3f(x)+1\right)\left(2-g(x)\right)$; (d) $p'(2)$ if $p(x) = x^{2}f(x)$.

(a) $h'(2) = f'(2)g(2) + f(2)g'(2) = (-1)(-2) + (4)(3) = 2 + 12 = \boxed{14}$

(b) $k'(2) = \dfrac{f'(2)g(2) - f(2)g'(2)}{[g(2)]^{2}} = \dfrac{(-1)(-2) - (4)(3)}{(-2)^{2}} = \dfrac{2-12}{4} = \boxed{-\dfrac52}$

Preview: proving "chain rule" results the long way

A power of a binomial, by the product rule

Concept

Expressions like $(x+1)^{3}$ or $(2x+3)^{-1}$ have a function sitting inside a power. The chain rule (next chapter) handles these in one line. Until then, you can still prove the results using only what you know: split the power into a product, or treat the negative power as a quotient.

Worked Example

Prove that $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[(x+1)^{3}\right] = 3(x+1)^{2}$ using the product rule.

0

The plan. We will first find the derivative of $(x+1)^{2}$ by writing it as $(x+1)(x+1)$. Then we will write $(x+1)^{3}$ as $(x+1)^{2}\cdot(x+1)$ and apply the product rule a second time, reusing the first result.

1

Derivative of the square. By the product rule with $u = v = x+1$ and $u' = v' = 1$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x+1)^{2}\right] = (1)(x+1) + (x+1)(1) = 2(x+1)$$

2

Split the cube into a product. $(x+1)^{3} = (x+1)^{2}\cdot(x+1)$. Take $u = (x+1)^{2}$, so $u' = 2(x+1)$ by Step 1, and $v = x+1$, so $v' = 1$.

3

Product rule, then factor.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x+1)^{3}\right] = 2(x+1)\cdot(x+1) + (x+1)^{2}\cdot 1 = 2(x+1)^{2} + (x+1)^{2}$$

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(x+1)^{3}\right] = \boxed{3(x+1)^{2}}$$

Notice the shape of the answer: the exponent came down, the exponent dropped by one, and the inside $(x+1)$ stayed intact. That is the power rule applied to the inside expression, which is exactly what the chain rule will formalize.

The six trig derivatives

Two of these come from the limit definition and must be memorized. The other four are consequences of those two plus the quotient rule, and this tab proves every one of them.

Memorize

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\sin x] = \cos x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\cos x] = -\sin x$$

Derive (then memorize)

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \sec^{2}x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\sec x] = \sec x\tan x$$ $$\frac{d}{dx}[\cot x] = -\csc^{2}x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[\csc x] = -\csc x\cot x$$

The co-function pattern. Take any formula in the left column of a pair, swap every function for its co-function, and put a minus sign in front: $\sin\to\cos$ gives $\cos\to-\sin$; $\tan\to\sec^{2}$ gives $\cot\to-\csc^{2}$; $\sec\to\sec\tan$ gives $\csc\to-\csc\cot$. Three formulas to learn, three to flip.

These formulas assume $x$ is measured in radians. In degrees the derivative of $\sin x$ would carry an extra factor of $\tfrac{\pi}{180}$, which is why calculus lives in radians.

Derivative of tangent

Concept

Since $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$, the quotient rule applies with $u = \sin x$ and $v = \cos x$. The Pythagorean identity $\sin^{2}x + \cos^{2}x = 1$ cleans up the numerator.

Worked Example

Prove that $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \sec^{2}x$.

0

The plan. Write tangent as sine over cosine, apply the quotient rule, simplify the numerator with the Pythagorean identity, and recognize the result as $\sec^{2}x$.

1

Rewrite and name the pieces. $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$, so $u = \sin x$, $u' = \cos x$, $v = \cos x$, $v' = -\sin x$.

2

Quotient rule.

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \frac{(\cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^{2}x} = \frac{\cos^{2}x + \sin^{2}x}{\cos^{2}x}$$

3

Pythagorean identity. By $\sin^{2}x + \cos^{2}x = 1$, the numerator is $1$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \frac{1}{\cos^{2}x}$$

Recognize secant. Since $\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, we have $\dfrac{1}{\cos^{2}x} = \sec^{2}x$.

$$\frac{d}{dx}[\tan x] = \boxed{\sec^{2}x}$$

Derivative of secant

Concept

Since $\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, this is a quotient with a constant numerator, so $u' = 0$ and only one term survives.

Worked Example

Prove that $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sec x] = \sec x\tan x$.

Rewrite and name the pieces. $u = 1$, $u' = 0$; $v = \cos x$, $v' = -\sin x$.

Trig functions inside products and quotients

Same rules, trig pieces

Concept

Nothing new here: identify $u$ and $v$, write down $u'$ and $v'$ using the trig table, and run the product or quotient rule. Simplify with identities only when it is clearly worth it.

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = x^{4}\tan x$.

$u = x^{4}$, $u' = 4x^{3}$; $v = \tan x$, $v' = \sec^{2}x$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 4x^{3}\tan x + x^{4}\sec^{2}x$$
Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{\cos x}{5x}$.

$u = \cos x$, $u' = -\sin x$; $v = 5x$, $v' = 5$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(-\sin x)(5x) - (\cos x)(5)}{(5x)^{2}} = \frac{-5x\sin x - 5\cos x}{25x^{2}} = \frac{-x\sin x - \cos x}{5x^{2}}$$

Alternative: pull the constant out first, $y = \tfrac15\cdot\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$, and run the quotient rule on $\dfrac{\cos x}{x}$. Same answer, smaller numbers.

Worked Example

If $y = \sin x\cos x$, find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ at $x = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$.

Product rule: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = (\cos x)(\cos x) + (\sin x)(-\sin x) = \cos^{2}x - \sin^{2}x$.

At $x = \tfrac{\pi}{6}$: $\cos^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{6} - \sin^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{6} = \left(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^{2} - \left(\tfrac12\right)^{2} = \tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \boxed{\tfrac12}$

Shortcut: $\cos^{2}x - \sin^{2}x = \cos 2x$, and $\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac12$. Either route works; the identity route is faster if you see it.

Worked Example

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \sec x\tan x$.

Two trig shortcuts tests love

Shortcut 1: the four-step cycle

Concept

Differentiating $\sin x$ repeatedly cycles through four functions and then repeats. The same is true for $\cos x$, starting one step along.

$$\sin x \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dx\;}\; \cos x \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dx\;}\; -\sin x \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dx\;}\; -\cos x \;\xrightarrow{\;d/dx\;}\; \sin x$$

To find $f^{(n)}(x)$, divide $n$ by $4$ and keep the remainder $r$. Then $f^{(n)} = f^{(r)}$ (with $f^{(0)} = f$).

Worked Example

If $g(x) = \cos x$, find $g^{(99)}(x)$.

$99 = 4\cdot 24 + 3$, so $g^{(99)} = g'''$. The cosine cycle is $\cos x\to -\sin x\to -\cos x\to \sin x$, so $g''' (x) = \boxed{\sin x}$.

Practice

If $f(x) = \sin x$, find $f^{(50)}(x)$.

$50 = 4\cdot 12 + 2$, so $f^{(50)} = f'' = -\sin x$.

Shortcut 2: recognize a derivative hiding in a limit

Concept

The definition of the derivative at a point has two common forms. If a limit matches either shape, do not try to compute it directly. Identify $f$ and $a$, then write $f'(a)$.

$$\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = f'(a) \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x-a} = f'(a)$$
Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sec\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}+h\right) - \sec\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)}{h}$.

This matches the first form with $f(x) = \sec x$ and $a = \tfrac{\pi}{4}$, so the limit equals $f'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$.

$$f'(x) = \sec x\tan x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \sec\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot\tan\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \sqrt2\cdot 1 = \boxed{\sqrt2}$$
Practice

(a) $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}+h\right) - \sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right)}{h}$     (b) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to \pi/4}\frac{\tan x - 1}{x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}}$

(a) $f(x) = \sin x$, $a = \tfrac{\pi}{3}$: the limit is $\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac12$.

(b) Second form with $f(x) = \tan x$, $a = \tfrac{\pi}{4}$, and $f(a) = \tan\tfrac{\pi}{4} = 1$: the limit is $\sec^{2}\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \left(\sqrt2\right)^{2} = 2$.

Tangent lines

Equation of the tangent line at $x = a$

Concept

The tangent line at $x = a$ is the straight line that just grazes the curve at $\left(a, f(a)\right)$ and has the same steepness as the curve there. Any line is pinned down by two pieces of information, and the picture shows where each one comes from:

The point $\left(a, f(a)\right)$ tells you where the line touches the curve; the slope $f'(a)$ tells you how tilted it is. Change either one and you get a different line.

So the recipe is always the same: plug $a$ into $f$ to get the point, plug $a$ into $f'$ to get the slope, and write point-slope form:

$$y - f(a) = f'(a)\,(x - a)$$

Why both are necessary. Knowing only the slope $f'(a)$ gives you an entire family of parallel lines; knowing only the point $\left(a, f(a)\right)$ gives you every line through that point, a whole pinwheel of them. Only the pair picks out the one line that both passes through the point and matches the curve's direction there.

The normal line at the same point is perpendicular to the tangent, so its slope is $-\dfrac{1}{f'(a)}$ (when $f'(a)\neq 0$).

Worked Example

Find an equation of the line tangent to $y = \dfrac{x^{2}+3}{x-1}$ at $x = 2$.

Step 1. The point. $y(2) = \dfrac{4+3}{2-1} = 7$, so the point is $(2, 7)$.

Step 2. The slope. Quotient rule with $u = x^{2}+3$, $v = x-1$:

$$y' = \frac{2x\,(x-1) - \left(x^{2}+3\right)(1)}{(x-1)^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y'(2) = \frac{4\cdot 1 - 7}{1} = -3$$

Step 3. Point-slope.

$$y - 7 = -3(x - 2) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \boxed{y = -3x + 13}$$
Practice

Find an equation of the line tangent to $y = \dfrac{3x}{x+2}$ at $x = 1$.

Point: $y(1) = 1$. Slope: $y' = \dfrac{3(x+2) - 3x}{(x+2)^{2}} = \dfrac{6}{(x+2)^{2}}$, so $y'(1) = \dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac23$.

$$y - 1 = \tfrac23(x - 1)$$

Where does the tangent have a given slope?

Set the derivative equal to the target slope

Concept

This reverses the usual question. Instead of "what is the slope at $x = a$?" you are asked "at which $x$ is the slope equal to $m$?" Differentiate, set $f'(x) = m$, solve for $x$, and then compute the corresponding $y$ values, since the answer is a set of points.

Horizontal tangent means $m = 0$: solve $f'(x) = 0$. For a quotient, the derivative is zero exactly when its numerator is zero (and the denominator is not).

Worked Example

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x+3}$. Find all points on the graph of $f$ where the tangent line has slope $\dfrac13$.

Step 1. Differentiate.

$$f'(x) = \frac{(1)(x+3) - (x)(1)}{(x+3)^{2}} = \frac{3}{(x+3)^{2}}$$

Step 2. Set equal to the slope and solve.

$$\frac{3}{(x+3)^{2}} = \frac13 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x+3)^{2} = 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; x+3 = \pm 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 0 \text{ or } x = -6$$

Step 3. Find the $y$-coordinates. $f(0) = 0$ and $f(-6) = \dfrac{-6}{-3} = 2$.

The points are $\boxed{(0,0)\text{ and }(-6, 2)}$. Forgetting the negative square root loses half the answer, and that missing half is always one of the multiple-choice options.

Worked Example

Find all points where the graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^{2}}{x-2}$ has a horizontal tangent line.

$$f'(x) = \frac{2x\,(x-2) - x^{2}(1)}{(x-2)^{2}} = \frac{x^{2} - 4x}{(x-2)^{2}} = \frac{x(x-4)}{(x-2)^{2}}$$

Numerator zero: $x = 0$ or $x = 4$ (neither makes the denominator zero). Then $f(0) = 0$ and $f(4) = \dfrac{16}{2} = 8$.

Horizontal tangents at $\boxed{(0,0)\text{ and }(4,8)}$.

Practice

(a) Find all points on $f(x) = \dfrac{x-1}{x+1}$ where the tangent has slope $\dfrac12$.

(b) Find all points where $f(x) = \dfrac{x^{2}+4}{x}$ has a horizontal tangent. (Simplify first.)

(a) $f'(x) = \dfrac{(x+1) - (x-1)}{(x+1)^{2}} = \dfrac{2}{(x+1)^{2}} = \dfrac12 \Rightarrow (x+1)^{2} = 4 \Rightarrow x = 1$ or $x = -3$. Points: $(1, 0)$ and $(-3, 2)$.

(b) $f(x) = x + 4x^{-1}$, so $f'(x) = 1 - \dfrac{4}{x^{2}} = 0 \Rightarrow x = \pm 2$. Points: $(2, 4)$ and $(-2, -4)$.

Reading derivatives off a graph

Piecewise-linear graphs give you values and slopes

Concept

When $f$ and $g$ are drawn as straight segments, you can read two things at any $x$: the height (the function value) and the slope of the segment (the derivative). Those four numbers feed the product or quotient rule exactly as a table would.

At a corner (where two segments of different slope meet), the derivative does not exist. If a product-rule question asks for $h'(x)$ at a corner of $g$, the correct response is "nonexistent," no matter how nice $f$ is there.

Worked Example

$f$ is linear; $g$ is piecewise linear with a corner at $x=2$.

The graphs of $f$ and $g$ are shown above. Let $h(x) = f(x)\,g(x)$. Find (a) $h'(4)$, (b) $h'(1)$, and (c) $h'(2)$.

Read the graph. $f$ runs from $(0,6)$ to $(6,0)$, so $f'(x) = -1$ everywhere and $f(x) = 6 - x$. The second piece of $g$ runs from $(2,3)$ to $(6,5)$: slope $\dfrac{5-3}{6-2} = \dfrac12$. The first piece runs from $(0,1)$ to $(2,3)$: slope $1$.

(a) At $x=4$: $f(4) = 2$, $f'(4) = -1$, $g(4) = 4$, $g'(4) = \tfrac12$.

$$h'(4) = f'(4)\,g(4) + f(4)\,g'(4) = (-1)(4) + (2)\left(\tfrac12\right) = \boxed{-3}$$

(b) At $x=1$: $f(1) = 5$, $f'(1) = -1$, $g(1) = 2$, $g'(1) = 1$.

$$h'(1) = (-1)(2) + (5)(1) = \boxed{3}$$

(c) At $x=2$: $g$ has a corner, so $g'(2)$ does not exist, and therefore $h'(2)$ is nonexistent.

Practice

Using the same graph: (a) if $k(x) = \dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$, find $k'(4)$; (b) find $h'(5)$ where $h = f\cdot g$.

(a) $k'(4) = \dfrac{f'(4)g(4) - f(4)g'(4)}{[g(4)]^{2}} = \dfrac{(-1)(4) - (2)\left(\tfrac12\right)}{16} = \dfrac{-5}{16}$

(b) $f(5) = 1$, $f'(5) = -1$, $g(5) = 4.5$, $g'(5) = \tfrac12$: $h'(5) = (-1)(4.5) + (1)\left(\tfrac12\right) = -4$

Differentiability and piecewise functions

When does a derivative fail to exist?

Concept

A function is differentiable at $a$ when the limit defining $f'(a)$ exists: the slopes approaching from the left and from the right agree and are finite. Four pictures break that:

Corner

Left and right slopes are both finite but different, like $|x|$ at $0$ or any piecewise-linear graph where segments meet.

Cusp

The slopes approach $+\infty$ from one side and $-\infty$ from the other, like $x^{2/3}$ at $0$.

Vertical tangent

The slope blows up to $\pm\infty$ on both sides in the same direction, like $\sqrt[3]{x}$ at $0$. The tangent line exists but its slope does not.

Discontinuity

No continuity, no derivative. Differentiability implies continuity, so a jump or hole rules out a derivative immediately.

Piecewise with parameters. To make $f$ differentiable at the seam $x = c$ you need two equations:

$$\underbrace{\text{left piece}(c) = \text{right piece}(c)}_{\text{continuity}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\text{left derivative}(c) = \text{right derivative}(c)}_{\text{matching slopes}}$$
Worked Example

Find the values of $a$ and $b$ that make $f$ both continuous and differentiable everywhere, where

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} a x^{2}, & x \le 1 \\[4pt] x^{3} + b, & x > 1 \end{cases}$$

Step 1. Continuity at $x = 1$. The two pieces must give the same value: $a(1)^{2} = (1)^{3} + b$, so $a = 1 + b$.

Step 2. Matching derivatives at $x = 1$. Differentiate each piece: $2ax$ and $3x^{2}$. At $x = 1$: $2a = 3$, so $a = \dfrac32$.

Step 3. Solve the system. $b = a - 1 = \dfrac12$.

$$\boxed{a = \tfrac32,\quad b = \tfrac12}$$

Away from $x = 1$ each piece is a polynomial, which is differentiable on its own, so the seam is the only place that needed checking.

Practice

Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f(x) = \begin{cases} 2x^{2} + a, & x \le 3 \\[4pt] bx - 5, & x > 3 \end{cases}$ is differentiable everywhere.

Derivatives: $4x$ and $b$. At $x = 3$: $12 = b$. Continuity: $2(9) + a = 3b - 5 = 31$, so $a = 13$. Answer: $a = 13$, $b = 12$.

1

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = 3x^{5} - \dfrac{2}{x^{2}} + \sqrt[3]{x} - 9$.

Power rule after rewriting

Rewrite: $y = 3x^{5} - 2x^{-2} + x^{1/3} - 9$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = 15x^{4} + 4x^{-3} + \tfrac13 x^{-2/3} = 15x^{4} + \frac{4}{x^{3}} + \frac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^{2}}}$$
2

Two ways. Let $y = \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(x^{3}-4x\right)$. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ using the product rule, then confirm by expanding first.

Product rule

Product rule: $2x\left(x^{3}-4x\right) + \left(x^{2}+1\right)\left(3x^{2}-4\right) = 2x^{4} - 8x^{2} + 3x^{4} - 4x^{2} + 3x^{2} - 4 = 5x^{4} - 9x^{2} - 4$.

Expanding: $y = x^{5} - 4x^{3} + x^{3} - 4x = x^{5} - 3x^{3} - 4x$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5x^{4} - 9x^{2} - 4$. They agree.

3

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{3x-2}{x^{2}+1}$. Simplify the numerator.

Quotient rule

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{3\left(x^{2}+1\right) - (3x-2)(2x)}{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}} = \frac{3x^{2} + 3 - 6x^{2} + 4x}{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}} = \frac{-3x^{2} + 4x + 3}{\left(x^{2}+1\right)^{2}}$$
4

Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ if $y = \dfrac{(x-3)\left(x^{2}+3x+9\right)}{x^{4}}$. Choose your method wisely.

Simplify first, then power rule

Difference of cubes: $(x-3)\left(x^{2}+3x+9\right) = x^{3} - 27$. Then $y = \dfrac{x^{3}}{x^{4}} - \dfrac{27}{x^{4}} = x^{-1} - 27x^{-4}$.

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -x^{-2} + 108x^{-5} = -\frac{1}{x^{2}} + \frac{108}{x^{5}}$$
5

Trig. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ for (a) $y = x^{3}\csc x$ and (b) $y = \dfrac{\sin x}{1+\cos x}$, simplifying (b) completely.

Trig with product and quotient rules

(a) $3x^{2}\csc x + x^{3}(-\csc x\cot x) = 3x^{2}\csc x - x^{3}\csc x\cot x$

(b) $\dfrac{\cos x\,(1+\cos x) - \sin x\,(-\sin x)}{(1+\cos x)^{2}} = \dfrac{\cos x + \cos^{2}x + \sin^{2}x}{(1+\cos x)^{2}} = \dfrac{1 + \cos x}{(1+\cos x)^{2}} = \dfrac{1}{1+\cos x}$

6

Values at a point. Suppose $v$ and $w$ are differentiable at $x = 1$ with $v(1) = -2$, $v'(1) = 4$, $w(1) = 3$, $w'(1) = -1$. Find the following derivatives at $x = 1$:

(a) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(vw)$    (b) $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{v}{w}\right)$    (c) $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{w}{v}\right)$    (d) $\dfrac{d}{dx}(5w - 3v)$    (e) $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac{1}{w}\right)$

Rules with given values

(a) $v'w + vw' = (4)(3) + (-2)(-1) = 14$

(b) $\dfrac{v'w - vw'}{w^{2}} = \dfrac{12 - 2}{9} = \dfrac{10}{9}$

(c) $\dfrac{w'v - wv'}{v^{2}} = \dfrac{(-1)(-2) - (3)(4)}{4} = \dfrac{2 - 12}{4} = -\dfrac52$

(d) $5w' - 3v' = -5 - 12 = -17$

(e) $-\dfrac{w'}{w^{2}} = -\dfrac{-1}{9} = \dfrac19$

7

Tangent lines. (a) Find an equation of the line tangent to $y = \dfrac{2x+1}{x-2}$ at $x = 3$. (b) Find all points on the graph of $f(x) = \dfrac{x^{2}}{x+1}$ where the tangent line is horizontal.

Tangent lines

(a) Point: $y(3) = \dfrac{7}{1} = 7$. Slope: $y' = \dfrac{2(x-2) - (2x+1)}{(x-2)^{2}} = \dfrac{-5}{(x-2)^{2}}$, so $y'(3) = -5$. Tangent: $y - 7 = -5(x-3)$, i.e. $y = -5x + 22$.

(b) $f'(x) = \dfrac{2x(x+1) - x^{2}}{(x+1)^{2}} = \dfrac{x^{2} + 2x}{(x+1)^{2}} = \dfrac{x(x+2)}{(x+1)^{2}}$. Zero when $x = 0$ or $x = -2$. Points: $(0, 0)$ and $(-2, -4)$.

8

Prove it. Using the quotient rule and $\csc x = \dfrac{1}{\sin x}$, prove that $\dfrac{d}{dx}[\csc x] = -\csc x\cot x$.

Quotient-rule proof

With $u = 1$, $u' = 0$, $v = \sin x$, $v' = \cos x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{\sin x}\right] = \frac{(0)(\sin x) - (1)(\cos x)}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{\cos x}{\sin^{2}x} = -\frac{1}{\sin x}\cdot\frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = -\csc x\cot x$$
9

Differentiability. Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f(x) = \begin{cases} ax^{2} + b, & x \le 1 \\[4pt] \dfrac{4}{x}, & x > 1 \end{cases}$ is differentiable at $x = 1$.

Piecewise differentiability

Right piece: $4x^{-1}$ has derivative $-4x^{-2}$, which is $-4$ at $x = 1$; its value at $1$ is $4$.

Matching slopes: $2a(1) = -4$, so $a = -2$. Continuity: $a + b = 4$, so $b = 6$.

$$a = -2,\qquad b = 6$$
10

Two shortcuts. (a) If $f(x) = \sin x$, find $f^{(123)}(x)$. (b) Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}+h\right) - \cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)}{h}$.

Derivative cycle and limit recognition

(a) $123 = 4\cdot 30 + 3$, so $f^{(123)} = f''' = -\cos x$.

(b) This is $f'\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\right)$ for $f(x) = \cos x$: $-\sin\tfrac{\pi}{6} = -\dfrac12$.
