The Definite Integral

The definite integral is defined as the limit of Riemann sums as the number of rectangles approaches infinity:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;=\; \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$

Geometrically, this says: chop $[a,b]$ into $n$ thin strips of width $\Delta x$, approximate each strip's area by a rectangle of height $f(x_i^*)$, sum them up, and take the limit. As $n \to \infty$ (equivalently, $\Delta x \to 0$), the approximation becomes exact.

$n$
Number of subintervals (rectangles) we divide $[a,b]$ into.
$\Delta x = \dfrac{b - a}{n}$
Width of each subinterval; as $n$ grows, $\Delta x$ shrinks.
$x_i^*$
A sample point chosen in the $i$-th subinterval (left, right, or midpoint).
$F(x)$
An antiderivative of $f(x)$, meaning $F'(x) = f(x)$.

Approximation Methods

Every approximation method chops $[a,b]$ into $n$ equal subintervals of width $\Delta x = \dfrac{b-a}{n}$. What differs is how we estimate the area of each strip.

Left Riemann Sum

$$L_n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

Uses the left endpoint of each subinterval: $x_i = a + i\,\Delta x$.

Right Riemann Sum

$$R_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

Uses the right endpoint of each subinterval: $x_i = a + i\,\Delta x$.

Midpoint Rule

$$M_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f\!\left(\frac{x_{i-1}+x_i}{2}\right)\Delta x$$

Uses the midpoint $\bar{x}_i = a + \left(i - \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta x$. Typically the most accurate of the four for the same $n$.

Trapezoidal Rule

$$T_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{f(x_{i-1}) + f(x_i)}{2}\,\Delta x$$

Averages left and right endpoints; equivalently, $T_n = \tfrac{1}{2}(L_n + R_n)$. Draws a straight line across each strip instead of a flat top.

Quick shortcut: You can always compute $T_n$ from $L_n$ and $R_n$: $$T_n = \frac{L_n + R_n}{2}$$ This is worth memorizing; it saves a lot of time.

The Definite Integral

The definite integral connects the idea of summing infinitely many thin slices to a single, exact number. Here is its formal definition and geometric meaning.

Formal Definition

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;=\; \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x$$

This limit exists whenever $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ (or bounded with finitely many discontinuities). The choice of sample point $x_i^*$ does not matter: left, right, midpoint all converge to the same value.

Geometric Meaning

When $f(x) \ge 0$, the integral equals the area under the curve. When $f$ dips below the $x$-axis, that region counts as negative area. The integral gives the net signed area.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The FTC is the bridge between differentiation and integration. It has two parts, and both are essential.

FTC Part I (Derivative of an Integral)

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$$

If $f$ is continuous, $G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is an antiderivative of $f$. Differentiation undoes integration.

FTC Part II (Evaluation Theorem)

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$$

If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a,b]$, the definite integral equals $F(b)-F(a)$. Often written $F(x)\Big|_a^b$.

Chain Rule Extension (upper limit is $g(x)$)

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\!\bigl(g(x)\bigr) \cdot g'(x)$$

Evaluate $f$ at the upper bound, then multiply by the derivative of that bound.

Both Bounds Variable

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{h(x)}^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\!\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x) - f\!\bigl(h(x)\bigr)\,h'(x)$$

Split at any constant $c$ and differentiate each piece.

Watch out: FTC Part II requires $f$ to be continuous on $[a,b]$. If $f$ has a discontinuity inside the interval (e.g., $\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$), you cannot blindly plug in bounds: the integral is improper and may diverge.

Worked Examples

FTC Part I: Find $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt$

Step 1: Identify the form: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ with $f(t) = \cos(t^2)$.

Step 2: Apply FTC Part I directly: the derivative of the integral is the integrand evaluated at the upper bound:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt = \cos(x^2)$$

No antiderivative needed! That's the power of FTC I: it works even when you can't find a closed-form antiderivative.

FTC Part II: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^4 \left(3\sqrt{x} - \frac{1}{x^2}\right)dx$

Step 1: Rewrite with power-rule-friendly exponents:

$$\int_1^4 \left(3x^{1/2} - x^{-2}\right)dx$$

Step 2: Find the antiderivative $F(x)$:

$$F(x) = 3\cdot\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} - \frac{x^{-1}}{-1} = 2x^{3/2} + \frac{1}{x}$$

Step 3: Apply FTC II: $F(4) - F(1)$:

$$F(4) = 2(4)^{3/2} + \frac{1}{4} = 2(8) + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{65}{4}$$ $$F(1) = 2(1)^{3/2} + \frac{1}{1} = 3$$ $$\int_1^4 \left(3\sqrt{x}-\frac{1}{x^2}\right)dx = \frac{65}{4} - 3 = \frac{53}{4}$$

Chain Rule Extension: Find $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^{x^3} \sqrt{t^2+1}\,dt$

Step 1: Identify the form: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt$ where $f(t) = \sqrt{t^2+1}$ and $g(x) = x^3$.

Step 2: Apply the chain rule extension: evaluate $f$ at the upper bound, then multiply by $g'(x)$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^{x^3}\sqrt{t^2+1}\,dt = \sqrt{(x^3)^2+1}\cdot 3x^2 = 3x^2\sqrt{x^6+1}$$

Key Properties of Integrals

The definite integral (like the derivative) is a linear operator: it respects addition and scalar multiplication. Several of the properties below follow directly from this linearity; the rest are structural facts about how integrals behave over intervals.

Linearity linear operator

$$\int_a^b [\alpha f + \beta g]\,dx = \alpha\!\int_a^b f\,dx + \beta\!\int_a^b g\,dx$$

Constant Multiple from linearity

$$\int_a^b c\,f(x)\,dx = c\!\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

Sum / Difference from linearity

$$\int_a^b [f \pm g]\,dx = \int_a^b f\,dx \pm \int_a^b g\,dx$$

Additivity over Intervals

$$\int_a^b f\,dx + \int_b^c f\,dx = \int_a^c f\,dx$$

Reversing Bounds

$$\int_b^a f(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

Zero-Width Integral

$$\int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0$$

Comparison

If $f(x)\le g(x)$ on $[a,b]$: $\;\displaystyle\int_a^b f\,dx \le \int_a^b g\,dx$

Even & Odd Symmetry on $[-a,a]$

Even ($f(-x)=f(x)$): $\;\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a} f\,dx = 2\!\int_0^a f\,dx$
Odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$): $\;\displaystyle\int_{-a}^{a} f\,dx = 0$

Over- & Underestimate Analysis

A classic question: "Is the approximation an overestimate or an underestimate? Justify." The answer depends on whether $f$ is increasing/decreasing and whether it is concave up/concave down.

Left & Right: Monotonicity

If $f$ is increasing:

$$L_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le R_n$$

If $f$ is decreasing:

$$R_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le L_n$$

Why?

On an increasing function, the left endpoint is always the smallest value on each subinterval (underestimate), and the right endpoint is the largest (overestimate). Decreasing reverses the picture.

Trapezoid & Midpoint: Concavity

If $f$ is concave up ($f'' > 0$):

$$M_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le T_n$$

If $f$ is concave down ($f'' < 0$):

$$T_n \le \int_a^b f\,dx \le M_n$$

Why?

When concave up, the curve bows below the secant line (trapezoid overshoots) but above the tangent at the midpoint (midpoint undershoots). Concave down reverses both.

How to justify: Always state your reasoning in two steps: (1) identify whether $f$ is increasing/decreasing or concave up/down on the interval (cite the table or graph), then (2) state the corresponding inequality. This two-step structure makes your argument airtight.
Mixed behavior: If $f$ changes from increasing to decreasing (or changes concavity) within $[a,b]$, you cannot make a blanket over/underestimate claim for the whole interval. You would need to analyze each subinterval where the behavior is consistent.

Power Rule & Basic Antiderivatives

Every integration technique ultimately reduces to these building blocks. The power rule reverses the process of differentiating $x^n$, and the rest are antiderivatives you'll want to recognize on sight.

The Power Rule

Concept

If you differentiate $x^{n+1}$, you get $(n+1)x^n$. Reversing that process gives the power rule for integration. It works for every exponent except $n = -1$ (which gives $\ln|x|$ instead).

$$\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \qquad (n \neq -1)$$

This includes negative and fractional exponents: write $\frac{1}{x^3}$ as $x^{-3}$ and $\sqrt{x}$ as $x^{1/2}$ first, then apply the rule.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \left(3x^4 - \frac{2}{x^3} + \sqrt{x}\right)dx$

Step 1: Rewrite each term with power-rule-friendly exponents:

$$= \int \left(3x^4 - 2x^{-3} + x^{1/2}\right)dx$$

Step 2: Apply the power rule term by term (add 1 to the exponent, divide by the new exponent):

$$= 3\cdot\frac{x^5}{5} - 2\cdot\frac{x^{-2}}{-2} + \frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} + C$$

Step 3: Simplify:

$$= \frac{3x^5}{5} + \frac{1}{x^2} + \frac{2}{3}x^{3/2} + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \left(5x^3 + \frac{4}{\sqrt{x}} - 7\right)dx$

Rewrite: $\int (5x^3 + 4x^{-1/2} - 7)\,dx$

$$= \frac{5x^4}{4} + 4\cdot\frac{x^{1/2}}{1/2} - 7x + C = \frac{5x^4}{4} + 8\sqrt{x} - 7x + C$$

Algebraic Manipulation

Before reaching for a fancy technique, always ask: "Can I simplify this first?" Many intimidating integrals become straightforward once you do a little algebra.

Split the Fraction

Concept

When the numerator has multiple terms added together, you can split the fraction into separate terms and simplify each one individually. This often turns a single complicated fraction into several easy power-rule integrals.

$$\int \frac{A + B + C}{\text{denominator}}\,dx = \int \frac{A}{\text{denom}}\,dx + \int \frac{B}{\text{denom}}\,dx + \int \frac{C}{\text{denom}}\,dx$$
Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^3 + 2x - 5}{x^2}\,dx$

Step 1: Split each term over the denominator:

$$= \int \left(\frac{x^3}{x^2} + \frac{2x}{x^2} - \frac{5}{x^2}\right)dx = \int \left(x + \frac{2}{x} - 5x^{-2}\right)dx$$

Step 2: Integrate each term:

$$= \frac{x^2}{2} + 2\ln|x| + \frac{5}{x} + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{4x^2 - 6x + 3}{2x}\,dx$

Split: $\int \left(2x - 3 + \frac{3}{2x}\right)dx$

$$= x^2 - 3x + \frac{3}{2}\ln|x| + C$$

$u$-Substitution

The chain rule in reverse, and the single most frequently used technique. If only one method sticks, make it this one. (The name "substitution" refers to replacing a complicated expression with a single variable $u$, transforming the integral into a simpler one.)

The Method

Concept

When an integrand looks like $f\!\bigl(g(x)\bigr) \cdot g'(x)$, you can substitute $u = g(x)$ to simplify the integral. The derivative $g'(x)\,dx$ becomes $du$, and the whole integral rewrites in terms of $u$.

$$\int f\!\bigl(g(x)\bigr)\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du \quad\text{where } u = g(x),\;\; du = g'(x)\,dx$$

How to apply it:

1. Identify the inner function $u = g(x)$. Look for a function inside another function, or a piece whose derivative appears elsewhere in the integrand.

2. Compute $du = g'(x)\,dx$. If it differs from what's in the integral by only a constant, adjust freely (constants pull in and out of integrals).

3. Rewrite the entire integral in terms of $u$ and $du$. No $x$'s should remain.

4. Integrate in $u$, then substitute back $u = g(x)$ to return to the original variable. (For definite integrals, convert the bounds to $u$-values instead of substituting back.)

Worked Example: Indefinite

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx$

Step 1: Identify the composition: $e^{(\;\cdot\;)}$ applied to $x^2$. Let $u = x^2$.

Step 2: Compute $du$: $\;du = 2x\,dx$. This matches what's left!

Step 3: Substitute and integrate:

$$\int 2x\,e^{x^2}\,dx = \int e^u\,du = e^u + C$$

Step 4: Substitute back: $= e^{x^2} + C$

Worked Example: Definite (change the bounds!)

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2 x\sqrt{x^2+1}\,dx$

Step 1: Let $u = x^2+1$, so $du = 2x\,dx$, meaning $x\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}\,du$.

Step 2: Convert the bounds: when $x=0$, $u=1$; when $x=2$, $u=5$.

Step 3: Substitute and integrate:

$$= \frac{1}{2}\int_1^5 u^{1/2}\,du = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\Big|_1^5 = \frac{1}{3}\!\left(5\sqrt{5}-1\right)$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}\,dx$

Let $u = \ln x$, $du = \frac{1}{x}\,dx$.

$$\int \frac{\cos(\ln x)}{x}\,dx = \int \cos u\,du = \sin u + C = \sin(\ln x) + C$$

Integration by Parts

The product rule in reverse. Use this when the integrand is a product of two different "types" of function and $u$-sub doesn't fit. (The name "by parts" refers to splitting the integrand into two parts: one you differentiate and one you integrate.) Note: Integration by Parts is a BC-only topic; it does not appear on the AP Calculus AB exam.

The Formula & LIATE

Concept

From the product rule $d(uv) = u\,dv + v\,du$, we rearrange to get:

$$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$$

How to apply it:

1. Choose $u$ from the integrand (use LIATE below). Everything else is $dv$.

2. Differentiate $u$ to get $du$. Integrate $dv$ to get $v$.

3. Plug into the formula: $uv - \int v\,du$. The new integral $\int v\,du$ should be simpler than the original.

The LIATE heuristic tells you which factor makes a good $u$. Pick from the top of this list:

LLogarithmic: $\ln x$, $\log x$ IInverse trig: $\arctan x$, $\arcsin x$ AAlgebraic: $x^2$, $x$, $\sqrt{x}$ TTrigonometric: $\sin x$, $\cos x$ EExponential: $e^x$, $2^x$

Functions earlier in the list simplify when differentiated, so they make better choices for $u$.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x\,e^x\,dx$

Step 1: Identify the two factors: $x$ (Algebraic) and $e^x$ (Exponential). LIATE says let $u = x$.

Step 2: Set up: $u = x \Rightarrow du = dx$,   $dv = e^x\,dx \Rightarrow v = e^x$.

Step 3: Apply the formula:

$$\int x\,e^x\,dx = x\,e^x - \int e^x\,dx = x\,e^x - e^x + C = e^x(x-1) + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x\cos x\,dx$

Let $u = x$, $dv = \cos x\,dx$. Then $du = dx$, $v = \sin x$.

$$= x\sin x - \int \sin x\,dx = x\sin x + \cos x + C$$

Long Division & Completing the Square

Additional algebraic techniques that frequently appear before applying other methods.

Long Division & Completing the Square

Concept

Long division: When the degree of the numerator is $\ge$ the degree of the denominator, divide first. This produces a polynomial plus a simpler remainder fraction.

Completing the square: Rewrites $ax^2 + bx + c$ in the form $(x-h)^2 + k$, which lets you match an $\arctan$ or $\arcsin$ pattern.

Worked Example: Long Division

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2 + 1}{x + 1}\,dx$

Step 1: Divide $x^2 + 1$ by $x+1$:

$$\frac{x^2+1}{x+1} = x - 1 + \frac{2}{x+1}$$

Step 2: Integrate:

$$= \frac{x^2}{2} - x + 2\ln|x+1| + C$$
Worked Example: Completing the Square

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x^2+6x+13}\,dx$

Step 1: Complete the square in the denominator:

$$x^2+6x+13 = (x^2+6x+9)+4 = (x+3)^2+4$$

Step 2: Match the $\arctan$ form $\displaystyle\int\frac{1}{u^2+a^2}\,du = \frac{1}{a}\arctan\!\left(\frac{u}{a}\right)$:

$$= \frac{1}{2}\arctan\!\left(\frac{x+3}{2}\right) + C$$
Practice: Long Division

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2}{x-1}\,dx$

Long division: $\frac{x^2}{x-1} = x + 1 + \frac{1}{x-1}$

$$= \frac{x^2}{2} + x + \ln|x-1| + C$$
Practice: Completing the Square

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x^2 - 4x + 8}\,dx$

Step 1: Complete the square in the denominator:

$$x^2 - 4x + 8 = (x^2 - 4x + 4) + 4 = (x-2)^2 + 4$$

Step 2: Match the $\arctan$ form with $u = x-2$ and $a = 2$:

$$\int \frac{1}{(x-2)^2 + 4}\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\arctan\!\left(\frac{x-2}{2}\right) + C$$

Partial Fraction Decomposition

When you have a rational function (polynomial ÷ polynomial), you can break it into simpler fractions that are each easy to integrate. Always do long division first if the degree on top $\ge$ the degree on the bottom.

Distinct Linear Factors

Concept

When the denominator factors into distinct linear terms like $(x-a)(x-b)$, set up one fraction for each factor:

$$\frac{P(x)}{(x-a)(x-b)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{x-b}$$

How to apply it:

1. Factor the denominator completely into linear terms $(x - a)(x - b)\cdots$

2. Write one partial fraction $\dfrac{A}{x-a} + \dfrac{B}{x-b} + \cdots$ for each factor.

3. Find each constant using the cover-up method: to find $A$, "cover up" the $(x-a)$ factor in the original fraction and plug in $x = a$. Repeat for each constant.

4. Integrate the pieces separately: $\displaystyle\int \frac{A}{x-a}\,dx + \int \frac{B}{x-b}\,dx = A\ln|x-a| + B\ln|x-b| + C$.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{5x+1}{(x+1)(x-2)}\,dx$

Step 1: Set up the decomposition: $\dfrac{5x+1}{(x+1)(x-2)} = \dfrac{A}{x+1} + \dfrac{B}{x-2}$

Step 2: Cover-up for $A$: cover the $(x+1)$ factor, plug in $x = -1$:

$$A = \frac{5(-1)+1}{(-1)-2} = \frac{-4}{-3} = \frac{4}{3}$$

Step 3: Cover-up for $B$: cover the $(x-2)$ factor, plug in $x = 2$:

$$B = \frac{5(2)+1}{(2)+1} = \frac{11}{3}$$

Step 4: Integrate each piece:

$$\int \frac{4/3}{x+1}\,dx + \int \frac{11/3}{x-2}\,dx = \frac{4}{3}\ln|x+1| + \frac{11}{3}\ln|x-2| + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{3x-1}{(x-1)(x+2)}\,dx$

Cover-up: $A = \frac{3(1)-1}{(1)+2} = \frac{2}{3}$,   $B = \frac{3(-2)-1}{(-2)-1} = \frac{-7}{-3} = \frac{7}{3}$

$$= \frac{2}{3}\ln|x-1| + \frac{7}{3}\ln|x+2| + C$$

Trig Identities

Trig identities let you rewrite powers of trig functions into integrable forms.

Power-Reducing & Pythagorean Identities

Concept

Even powers of $\sin$ and $\cos$ don't have direct antiderivatives. Use power-reducing identities to convert them:

$$\sin^2 x = \frac{1-\cos 2x}{2} \qquad \cos^2 x = \frac{1+\cos 2x}{2}$$

For odd powers, peel off one factor, convert the rest using $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, then $u$-sub:

$$\int \cos^3 x\,dx = \int (1-\sin^2 x)\cos x\,dx \quad\longrightarrow\quad u = \sin x$$
Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \sin^2 x\,dx$

Step 1: Apply the power-reducing identity: $\sin^2 x = \frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}$

Step 2: Integrate:

$$= \int \frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}\,dx = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \cos^3 x\,dx$  (Hint: peel off one $\cos x$, use $\cos^2 x = 1-\sin^2 x$, then $u = \sin x$.)

$= \int(1-\sin^2 x)\cos x\,dx$. Let $u = \sin x$:

$$= \int(1-u^2)\,du = u - \frac{u^3}{3} + C = \sin x - \frac{\sin^3 x}{3} + C$$

Trig Substitution

When you see a square root of a quadratic expression, a trig substitution can eliminate the radical using a Pythagorean identity.

The Method

Concept

Match the form under the root and apply the corresponding substitution:

$\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$

let $x = a\sin\theta$

$\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$

let $x = a\tan\theta$

$\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$

let $x = a\sec\theta$

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\,dx$

Step 1: Match the form $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ with $a = 2$. Let $x = 2\sin\theta$, $dx = 2\cos\theta\,d\theta$.

Step 2: Simplify the radical:

$$\sqrt{4-x^2} = \sqrt{4-4\sin^2\theta} = 2\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta} = 2\cos\theta$$

Step 3: Substitute everything:

$$\int \frac{2\cos\theta}{2\cos\theta}\,d\theta = \int 1\,d\theta = \theta + C$$

Step 4: Convert back: since $x = 2\sin\theta$, we have $\theta = \arcsin(x/2)$.

$$= \arcsin\!\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\,dx$

Let $x = 3\sin\theta$, $dx = 3\cos\theta\,d\theta$, $\sqrt{9-x^2} = 3\cos\theta$.

$$= \int \frac{9\sin^2\theta \cdot 3\cos\theta}{3\cos\theta}\,d\theta = 9\int \sin^2\theta\,d\theta$$ $$= 9\left(\frac{\theta}{2} - \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right) + C = \frac{9}{2}\arcsin\!\left(\frac{x}{3}\right) - \frac{x\sqrt{9-x^2}}{2} + C$$

Improper Integrals

When the interval is infinite or the integrand blows up at a point, the integral is improper. Replace the trouble spot with a limit and check whether the result converges to a finite number.

Infinite Intervals

Concept

Replace the infinite bound with a variable and take a limit:

$$\int_a^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$$

If the limit exists and is finite, the integral converges. Otherwise it diverges.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$

Step 1: Replace $\infty$ with $t$:

$$\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_1^t x^{-2}\,dx$$

Step 2: Integrate:

$$= \lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_1^t = \lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{t}+1\right) = 0 + 1 = 1$$

The integral converges to $1$.

Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}\,dx$. Does it converge or diverge?

$$= \lim_{t\to\infty}[\ln|x|]_1^t = \lim_{t\to\infty}(\ln t - 0) = \infty$$

The integral diverges. This is the famous $p = 1$ borderline case.

Discontinuous Integrands & the $p$-Test

Concept

If $f(x)$ blows up at $x = a$, replace that bound with a limit approaching from the correct side:

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to a^+}\int_t^b f(x)\,dx$$

The $p$-test is a quick convergence check for $\frac{1}{x^p}$ integrals:

$$\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^p}\,dx \;\begin{cases} \text{converges} & \text{if } p > 1 \\ \text{diverges} & \text{if } p \le 1 \end{cases}$$

Comparison test: If $0 \le f(x) \le g(x)$ for all $x$ in the interval and $\int g$ converges, then $\int f$ converges too. If $\int f$ diverges, so does $\int g$.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$

Step 1: The integrand $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}} = x^{-1/2}$ blows up at $x = 0$. Replace with a limit:

$$= \lim_{t\to 0^+}\int_t^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx$$

Step 2: Integrate and evaluate:

$$= \lim_{t\to 0^+}\left[2\sqrt{x}\right]_t^1 = \lim_{t\to 0^+}(2 - 2\sqrt{t}) = 2$$

The integral converges to $2$.

Practice

Does $\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^2+1}\,dx$ converge? If so, find its value.

Comparison: $\frac{1}{x^2+1} \le \frac{1}{x^2}$ and $\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ converges ($p=2>1$), so this converges too.

$$= \lim_{t\to\infty}[\arctan x]_1^t = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{4}$$

Which Technique Should I Try?

Work through this decision tree from top to bottom. Stop at the first "yes."

1

Can you simplify first?

Expand, split fractions, factor, rewrite radicals as powers → Algebraic Manipulation (AB tab)

2

Is there a composition $f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)$?

Inner function whose derivative is lurking nearby → $u$-Substitution (AB tab)

3

Is it a product of two different types?

e.g. $x \cdot e^x$, $x^2 \sin x$, $\ln x$ alone → §1 Integration by Parts (use LIATE)

4

Is it a rational function $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$?

Degree on top $\ge$ bottom? Long divide first (§2), then → §3 Partial Fractions

5

Powers of trig functions?

$\sin^n x$, $\cos^n x$, or mixed → §4 Trig Identities (power-reduce or peel off one)

6

$\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$, or $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$?

Square root of a quadratic → §5 Trig Substitution

7

Infinite bounds or integrand blows up?

Replace trouble spot with a limit → §6 Improper Integrals

Tip: In practice, most AP problems need steps 1–3 (AB tab). Always try simplifying and $u$-sub before the BC techniques.

These techniques go beyond the standard AP curriculum but appear in competition math, college courses, and can sometimes provide elegant shortcuts. They're ordered from most commonly useful to most specialized.

1

Tabular Integration

When $u$ is a polynomial (so repeated differentiation eventually hits 0), you can avoid writing out integration by parts over and over. Make two columns: differentiate $u$ going down, integrate $dv$ going down, then alternate signs $+, -, +, -, \ldots$ and multiply diagonally.

The Method

Concept

Tabular method (or "DI method") is a shortcut for repeated integration by parts when one factor is a polynomial.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2\sin x\,dx$

Build the table. Differentiate $x^2$ on the left, integrate $\sin x$ on the right:

Sign Differentiate Integrate
$+$$x^2$$\sin x$
$-$$2x$$-\cos x$
$+$$2$$-\sin x$
$-$$0$$\cos x$

Read off the answer. Multiply each "Differentiate" entry with the "Integrate" entry one row below, using the sign column:

$$= (+)x^2(-\cos x) + (-)2x(-\sin x) + (+)2(\cos x) + C$$ $$= -x^2\cos x + 2x\sin x + 2\cos x + C$$
Practice

Use the tabular method to evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2 e^x\,dx$

Table: differentiate $x^2$, integrate $e^x$ (which stays $e^x$).

$$= x^2 e^x - 2x\,e^x + 2e^x + C = e^x(x^2 - 2x + 2) + C$$
2

Repeated Factors & Irreducible Quadratics

When partial fraction decomposition involves repeated linear factors or irreducible quadratic factors in the denominator, the setup requires additional terms.

Repeated Factors & Irreducible Quadratics

Concept

Repeated linear factors: If $(x-a)$ appears $k$ times, you need $k$ fractions with increasing powers in the denominator:

$$\frac{P(x)}{(x-a)^3} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{(x-a)^2} + \frac{C}{(x-a)^3}$$

Irreducible quadratic factor: A quadratic that doesn't factor over the reals needs a linear numerator $Bx + C$:

$$\frac{P(x)}{(x-a)(x^2+bx+c)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$$

The $Bx+C$ piece often requires completing the square and splitting into a $\ln$ part and an $\arctan$ part.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{2x}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}\,dx$

Step 1: Set up: $\dfrac{2x}{(x-1)(x^2+1)} = \dfrac{A}{x-1} + \dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}$

Step 2: Cover-up for $A$: plug in $x=1$:   $A = \dfrac{2(1)}{1^2+1} = 1$

Step 3: Multiply through and match: $2x = (x^2+1) + (Bx+C)(x-1)$. Setting $x = 0$: $0 = 1 - C$, so $C=1$. Setting $x=2$: $4 = 5 + (2B+1) = 6 + 2B$, so $B = -1$.

Step 4: Integrate: $\int \dfrac{1}{x-1}\,dx + \int \dfrac{-x+1}{x^2+1}\,dx = \ln|x-1| - \frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2+1) + \arctan x + C$

Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{6}{(x-1)^2}\,dx$

$$\int 6(x-1)^{-2}\,dx = \frac{6(x-1)^{-1}}{-1} + C = \frac{-6}{x-1} + C$$
3

Switching to Polar Coordinates

When integrating over circular or radial regions, converting from Cartesian $(x,y)$ to polar $(r,\theta)$ can simplify the integral dramatically.

The Conversion

Concept

In polar coordinates, points are described by distance $r$ from the origin and angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$-axis.

$$x = r\cos\theta$$
$$y = r\sin\theta$$
$$x^2+y^2 = r^2$$

The crucial formula for converting area elements is:

$$dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$$
Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2 \le 4} (x^2+y^2)\,dA$ by converting to polar coordinates.

Step 1: The region is a disk of radius 2 centered at the origin. In polar: $0 \le r \le 2$, $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$.

Step 2: Replace $x^2+y^2$ with $r^2$ and $dA$ with $r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$\iint (x^2+y^2)\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 r^2 \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 r^3\,dr\,d\theta$$

Step 3: Integrate with respect to $r$:

$$= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^2\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{16}{4}\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} 4\,d\theta$$

Step 4: Integrate with respect to $\theta$:

$$= 4\theta\Big|_0^{2\pi} = 8\pi$$
Practice

Find the area of the region inside the circle $r = 3\cos\theta$.

The region is traced as $\theta$ goes from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$ and $r$ from $0$ to $3\cos\theta$.

$$A = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_0^{3\cos\theta} r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{9\cos^2\theta}{2}\,d\theta$$ $$= \frac{9}{2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta = \frac{9}{4}\left[\theta + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{2}\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{9\pi}{4}$$
4

Trig Power Reduction (Wallis-Type Integrals)

For integrals of the form $\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^n x\,dx$ or $\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^n x\,dx$, there's a recursive reduction formula (Wallis). For $\int \sin^m x \cos^n x\,dx$ with mixed powers, strategy depends on whether exponents are odd or even.

Wallis Formula and Reduction

Concept

The Wallis integral formula provides a closed form for definite integrals of even powers of sine and cosine on $[0, \pi/2]$.

$$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx = \frac{(n-1)!!}{n!!} \cdot \begin{cases}\frac{\pi}{2} & n\text{ even}\\1 & n\text{ odd}\end{cases}$$

where $n!! = n(n-2)(n-4)\cdots$ is the double factorial.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 x\,dx$

Step 1: Here $n = 4$ (even), so we use the Wallis formula:

$$= \frac{(4-1)!!}{4!!} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3!!}{4!!} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}$$

Step 2: Compute the double factorials: $3!! = 3 \cdot 1 = 3$ and $4!! = 4 \cdot 2 = 8$.

Step 3: Substitute:

$$= \frac{3}{8} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{16}$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^5 x\,dx$

For $n = 5$ (odd): $\frac{(5-1)!!}{5!!} \cdot 1 = \frac{4!!}{5!!} = \frac{4 \cdot 2}{5 \cdot 3 \cdot 1} = \frac{8}{15}$

5

Reduction Formulas

A reduction formula expresses $\int f_n(x)\,dx$ in terms of $\int f_{n-k}(x)\,dx$, reducing the complexity step by step. Useful for powers of trig, powers of secant, etc.

Common Reduction Formulas

Concept

Reduction formulas allow you to systematically reduce the power of a function, eventually reaching an easy base case.

$$\int \sin^n x\,dx = -\frac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\int \sin^{n-2}x\,dx$$
$$\int \sec^n x\,dx = \frac{\sec^{n-2}x\tan x}{n-1} + \frac{n-2}{n-1}\int \sec^{n-2}x\,dx$$
Worked Example

Find $\displaystyle\int \sin^3 x\,dx$ using the reduction formula.

Step 1: Apply the reduction formula with $n = 3$:

$$\int \sin^3 x\,dx = -\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3} + \frac{2}{3}\int \sin x\,dx$$

Step 2: Integrate the remaining $\sin x$:

$$= -\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3} + \frac{2}{3}(-\cos x) + C$$ $$= -\frac{\sin^2 x\cos x}{3} - \frac{2}{3}\cos x + C$$

Step 3: Optionally factor:

$$= -\cos x\left(\frac{\sin^2 x}{3} + \frac{2}{3}\right) + C = -\frac{\cos x(1 + 2\cos^2 x)}{3} + C$$
Practice

Use the reduction formula to find $\displaystyle\int \sec^3 x\,dx$.

Apply the formula with $n=3$:

$$\int \sec^3 x\,dx = \frac{\sec x\tan x}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\int \sec x\,dx = \frac{\sec x\tan x}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C$$
6

Hyperbolic Substitution

Similar to trig substitution but uses $\sinh$, $\cosh$, $\tanh$. Useful for $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}$ and $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ forms. Key identity: $\cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$.

The Substitutions

Concept

The hyperbolic functions provide an alternative to trig substitution, often leading to simpler final forms.

$$\sqrt{x^2+a^2} \to x = a\sinh t$$
$$\sqrt{x^2-a^2} \to x = a\cosh t$$

The key hyperbolic identity is $\cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$, analogous to $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.

Worked Example

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}\,dx$ using $x = \sinh t$.

Step 1: Let $x = \sinh t$, so $dx = \cosh t\,dt$ and $\sqrt{x^2+1} = \sqrt{\sinh^2 t + 1} = \cosh t$.

Step 2: Substitute:

$$\int \frac{\cosh t}{\cosh t}\,dt = \int 1\,dt = t + C$$

Step 3: Convert back using $t = \sinh^{-1}(x) = \ln(x + \sqrt{x^2+1})$:

$$= \sinh^{-1}(x) + C = \ln(x + \sqrt{x^2+1}) + C$$
Practice

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \sqrt{x^2+4}\,dx$ using $x = 2\sinh t$.

Let $x = 2\sinh t$, $dx = 2\cosh t\,dt$, $\sqrt{x^2+4} = 2\cosh t$.

$$= \int 2\cosh t \cdot 2\cosh t\,dt = 4\int \cosh^2 t\,dt = 4\int \frac{\cosh 2t + 1}{2}\,dt$$ $$= 2\left(\frac{\sinh 2t}{2} + t\right) + C = \sinh 2t + 2t + C$$

Converting back: $\sinh 2t = 2\sinh t\cosh t = \frac{x}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{2}$, and $t = \sinh^{-1}(x/2)$:

$$= \frac{x\sqrt{x^2+4}}{2} + 2\ln\left(\frac{x}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{2}\right) + C = \frac{x\sqrt{x^2+4}}{2} + 2\ln\left(x+\sqrt{x^2+4}\right) + C'$$

Already tried everything in AB & BC? These clues point you to the right advanced method.

1

IBP with a polynomial factor?

$x^n \cdot e^x$, $x^n \cdot \sin x$, etc.: repeated IBP gets tedious → §1 Tabular Integration

2

Repeated or irreducible quadratic factors in denominator?

$(x-a)^k$ or $(x^2+bx+c)$ that won't factor → §2 Repeated Factors & Irreducible Quadratics

3

Integrating over a circular or radial region?

$x^2+y^2$ terms, disk/annulus region → §3 Polar Coordinates

4

$\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx$ or $\cos^n x\,dx$?

Definite integral with pure trig powers → §4 Wallis / Trig Power Reduction

5

Need $\int \sin^n x\,dx$ or $\int \sec^n x\,dx$ for general $n$?

Express $I_n$ in terms of $I_{n-2}$ → §5 Reduction Formulas

6

$\sqrt{x^2 \pm a^2}$ but want a cleaner result than trig sub?

Avoids back-substitution with triangles → §6 Hyperbolic Substitution

These are tools for your toolbox, not a strict sequence. With experience you'll recognize which fits at a glance.

1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{3x^2}{(x^3+1)^4}\,dx$

U-SUBSTITUTION

Let $u = x^3+1$, so $du = 3x^2\,dx$. Then: $$\int \frac{3x^2}{(x^3+1)^4}\,dx = \int u^{-4}\,du = \frac{u^{-3}}{-3} + C = \frac{-1}{3(x^3+1)^3} + C$$
2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^4 - 3x^2 + 1}{x^2}\,dx$

ALGEBRAIC MANIPULATION

Divide each term by $x^2$: $$\int \frac{x^4 - 3x^2 + 1}{x^2}\,dx = \int \left(x^2 - 3 + x^{-2}\right)\,dx = \frac{x^3}{3} - 3x - \frac{1}{x} + C$$
3

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \left(4x^3 - \frac{6}{\sqrt[3]{x}}\right)dx$

POWER RULE

Rewrite using exponents: $\int (4x^3 - 6x^{-1/3})\,dx$. Apply the power rule: $$= 4 \cdot \frac{x^4}{4} - 6 \cdot \frac{x^{2/3}}{2/3} + C = x^4 - 9x^{2/3} + C$$
4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \tan x\,dx$

U-SUBSTITUTION

Rewrite as $\int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx$. Let $u = \cos x$, so $du = -\sin x\,dx$: $$\int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx = -\int \frac{du}{u} = -\ln|u| + C = -\ln|\cos x| + C = \ln|\sec x| + C$$
5

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \left(\frac{3}{x^4} + 2\sqrt{x^3}\right)dx$

POWER RULE

Rewrite using exponents: $\int(3x^{-4} + 2x^{3/2})\,dx$. Apply the power rule: $$= 3 \cdot \frac{x^{-3}}{-3} + 2 \cdot \frac{x^{5/2}}{5/2} + C = -\frac{1}{x^3} + \frac{4x^{5/2}}{5} + C$$
6

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{(x+1)^2}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$

ALGEBRAIC MANIPULATION

Expand the numerator and divide by $x^{1/2}$: $$\frac{(x+1)^2}{x^{1/2}} = \frac{x^2+2x+1}{x^{1/2}} = x^{3/2}+2x^{1/2}+x^{-1/2}$$ Integrate each term: $$\int \left(x^{3/2}+2x^{1/2}+x^{-1/2}\right)dx = \frac{2x^{5/2}}{5} + \frac{4x^{3/2}}{3} + 2x^{1/2} + C$$
7

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/4} \sec^2 x\,dx$

FTC PART II

Step 1: Recognize the antiderivative: $\int \sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x$.

Step 2: Apply FTC II: evaluate at the bounds:

$$\int_0^{\pi/4}\sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x\Big|_0^{\pi/4} = \tan\frac{\pi}{4} - \tan 0 = 1 - 0 = 1$$
8

Find $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\,dt$

FTC PART I (CHAIN RULE)

Step 1: Identify the form: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt$ where $f(t) = e^{-t^2}$ and $g(x) = x^2$.

Step 2: Apply FTC I with the chain rule: evaluate $f$ at the upper bound and multiply by $g'(x) = 2x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\,dt = e^{-(x^2)^2}\cdot 2x = 2x\,e^{-x^4}$$

Note: $e^{-t^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, but FTC I doesn't require one!

1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2 \ln x\,dx$

INTEGRATION BY PARTS

LIATE says $u = \ln x$ (Logarithmic beats Algebraic). Then $dv = x^2\,dx$, $du = \frac{1}{x}\,dx$, and $v = \frac{x^3}{3}$. $$\int x^2 \ln x\,dx = \frac{x^3 \ln x}{3} - \int \frac{x^3}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \frac{x^3 \ln x}{3} - \int \frac{x^2}{3}\,dx = \frac{x^3 \ln x}{3} - \frac{x^3}{9} + C$$
2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \sin^2 x \cos^2 x\,dx$

TRIG IDENTITIES

Use the identity $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x = \frac{\sin^2 2x}{4}$ and $\sin^2 2x = \frac{1-\cos 4x}{2}$: $$\sin^2 x\cos^2 x = \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{1-\cos 4x}{2} = \frac{1-\cos 4x}{8}$$ Integrate: $$\int \frac{1-\cos 4x}{8}\,dx = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{\sin 4x}{32} + C$$
3

Does this improper integral converge? $\displaystyle\int_1^{\infty} \frac{3}{x^4}\,dx$

IMPROPER INTEGRALS

Set up the limit: $$\int_1^{\infty} \frac{3}{x^4}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_1^t 3x^{-4}\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-x^{-3}\right]_1^t$$ Evaluate: $$= \lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-t^{-3}+1\right) = 0 + 1 = 1$$ The integral converges to 1.
4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{4}{x^2-4}\,dx$

PARTIAL FRACTIONS

Factor: $x^2-4 = (x-2)(x+2)$. Decompose: $$\frac{4}{(x-2)(x+2)} = \frac{1}{x-2} - \frac{1}{x+2}$$ Integrate: $$\int \left(\frac{1}{x-2} - \frac{1}{x+2}\right)dx = \ln|x-2| - \ln|x+2| + C = \ln\left|\frac{x-2}{x+2}\right| + C$$
5

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{9+x^2}}\,dx$

TRIG SUBSTITUTION

Let $x = 3\tan\theta$, so $dx = 3\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{9+x^2} = 3\sec\theta$: $$\int\frac{3\sec^2\theta}{3\sec\theta}\,d\theta = \int\sec\theta\,d\theta = \ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C$$ Back-substitute: $\tan\theta = \frac{x}{3}$ and $\sec\theta = \frac{\sqrt{9+x^2}}{3}$: $$= \ln\left|\frac{\sqrt{9+x^2}}{3}+\frac{x}{3}\right|+C = \ln|x+\sqrt{9+x^2}|+C'$$
6

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^3+2}{x+1}\,dx$

LONG DIVISION

Divide $x^3+2$ by $x+1$ to get: $$\frac{x^3+2}{x+1} = x^2 - x + 1 + \frac{1}{x+1}$$ Integrate each term: $$\int \left(x^2 - x + 1 + \frac{1}{x+1}\right)dx = \frac{x^3}{3} - \frac{x^2}{2} + x + \ln|x+1| + C$$
7

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \cos^4 x\,dx$

TRIG IDENTITIES

Use $\cos^2 x = \frac{1+\cos 2x}{2}$: $$\cos^4 x = \left(\frac{1+\cos 2x}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{1+2\cos 2x+\cos^2 2x}{4}$$ Apply the identity again to $\cos^2 2x$: $$= \frac{1+2\cos 2x+\frac{1+\cos 4x}{2}}{4} = \frac{3}{8}+\frac{\cos 2x}{2}+\frac{\cos 4x}{8}$$ Integrate: $$\int\left(\frac{3}{8}+\frac{\cos 2x}{2}+\frac{\cos 4x}{8}\right)dx = \frac{3x}{8}+\frac{\sin 2x}{4}+\frac{\sin 4x}{32}+C$$
8

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{x^2+4x+5}\,dx$

COMPLETING THE SQUARE

Complete the square: $$x^2+4x+5 = (x+2)^2+1$$ This matches the arctangent pattern $\int\frac{1}{u^2+1}\,du = \arctan u$. Let $u = x+2$: $$\int \frac{1}{(x+2)^2+1}\,dx = \arctan(x+2) + C$$
9

Does this improper integral converge? $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^{2/3}}\,dx$

IMPROPER INTEGRALS

The integrand blows up at $x=0$. Set up the limit: $$\int_0^1 \frac{1}{x^{2/3}}\,dx = \int_0^1 x^{-2/3}\,dx = \lim_{t\to 0^+}\int_t^1 x^{-2/3}\,dx = \lim_{t\to 0^+}\left[3x^{1/3}\right]_t^1$$ Evaluate: $$= 3(1) - 3\lim_{t\to 0^+}t^{1/3} = 3 - 0 = 3$$ The integral converges to 3.
10

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x+7}{(x+1)(x+3)}\,dx$

PARTIAL FRACTIONS

Set $\frac{x+7}{(x+1)(x+3)} = \frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{x+3}$. Using cover-up method: $$A = \frac{-1+7}{-1+3} = \frac{6}{2} = 3, \quad B = \frac{-3+7}{-3+1} = \frac{4}{-2} = -2$$ Integrate: $$\int\left(\frac{3}{x+1}+\frac{-2}{x+3}\right)dx = 3\ln|x+1| - 2\ln|x+3| + C$$
11

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{\sqrt{x^2-16}}{x}\,dx$

TRIG SUBSTITUTION

Let $x = 4\sec\theta$, so $dx = 4\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2-16} = 4\tan\theta$: $$\int\frac{4\tan\theta}{4\sec\theta}\cdot 4\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta = 4\int\tan^2\theta\,d\theta = 4\int(\sec^2\theta-1)\,d\theta$$ $$= 4\tan\theta - 4\theta + C$$ Back-substitute: $\tan\theta = \frac{\sqrt{x^2-16}}{4}$ and $\theta = \sec^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x}{4}\right)$: $$= \sqrt{x^2-16} - 4\sec^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x}{4}\right) + C$$
12

★ Challenge: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int e^x \sin x\,dx$

INTEGRATION BY PARTS (TWICE, CYCLIC)

Let $I = \int e^x\sin x\,dx$. First IBP with $u=\sin x$, $dv=e^x\,dx$: $$I = e^x\sin x - \int e^x\cos x\,dx$$ Apply IBP again with $u=\cos x$, $dv=e^x\,dx$: $$I = e^x\sin x - \left(e^x\cos x - \int e^x\sin x\,dx\right) = e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - I$$ Solve for $I$: $2I = e^x(\sin x - \cos x)$, so: $$I = \frac{e^x(\sin x - \cos x)}{2} + C$$

This "solve for $I$" trick is a classic BC move whenever IBP cycles back to the original integral.

Test your skills! These problems are in random order; part of the challenge is identifying which technique to use. Each solution reveals the method.

1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2\le 9} \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dA$ by converting to polar coordinates.

POLAR COORDINATES

In polar: $\sqrt{x^2+y^2} = r$ and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$. The region $x^2+y^2 \le 9$ becomes $0\le r\le 3$, $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$: $$\iint_{x^2+y^2\le 9} \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 r^2\,dr\,d\theta$$ $$= \int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^3\!d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}9\,d\theta = 18\pi$$
2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^3 e^{2x}\,dx$

TABULAR INTEGRATION

Set up a table with alternating derivatives and integrals. Differentiate $x^3$ and integrate $e^{2x}$:

Row 1: $(+)x^3 \cdot \frac{e^{2x}}{2}$
Row 2: $(-) 3x^2 \cdot \frac{e^{2x}}{4}$
Row 3: $(+) 6x \cdot \frac{e^{2x}}{8}$
Row 4: $(-) 6 \cdot \frac{e^{2x}}{16}$

Combine the rows: $$\int x^3 e^{2x}\,dx = e^{2x}\left(\frac{x^3}{2}-\frac{3x^2}{4}+\frac{3x}{4}-\frac{3}{8}\right)+C$$
3

Use the reduction formula to evaluate $\displaystyle\int \sin^4 x\,dx$

REDUCTION FORMULAS

For $I_n = \int \sin^n x\,dx$, the reduction formula is: $$I_n = -\frac{\sin^{n-1} x\cos x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}I_{n-2}$$ For $I_4 = \int \sin^4 x\,dx$: $$I_4 = -\frac{\sin^3 x\cos x}{4}+\frac{3}{4}I_2$$ And $I_2 = \int \sin^2 x\,dx = -\frac{\sin x\cos x}{2}+\frac{1}{2}x$. Combining: $$I_4 = -\frac{\sin^3 x\cos x}{4}+\frac{3}{4}\left(-\frac{\sin x\cos x}{2}+\frac{x}{2}\right)+C$$ $$= -\frac{\sin^3 x\cos x}{4}-\frac{3\sin x\cos x}{8}+\frac{3x}{8}+C$$
4

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}\,dx$ using hyperbolic substitution.

HYPERBOLIC SUBSTITUTION

Let $x = 3\sinh t$, so $dx = 3\cosh t\,dt$ and $\sqrt{x^2+9} = 3\cosh t$ (using $\cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$): $$\int\frac{3\cosh t}{3\cosh t}\,dt = \int 1\,dt = t + C = \sinh^{-1}\!\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)+C$$ Using the formula $\sinh^{-1}(u) = \ln(u+\sqrt{u^2+1})$: $$= \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{3}+\sqrt{\frac{x^2}{9}+1}\right)+C = \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{3}+\frac{\sqrt{x^2+9}}{3}\right)+C$$ $$= \ln(x+\sqrt{x^2+9})+C'$$
5

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x+3}{(x+1)^2}\,dx$

REPEATED FACTORS (PARTIAL FRACTIONS)

Decompose with a repeated factor: $$\frac{x+3}{(x+1)^2} = \frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{B}{(x+1)^2}$$ Multiply both sides by $(x+1)^2$: $x+3 = A(x+1)+B$.
Set $x=-1$: $2=B$.
Compare $x$ coefficients: $1=A$.
Therefore: $$\int \left(\frac{1}{x+1}+\frac{2}{(x+1)^2}\right)dx = \ln|x+1|-\frac{2}{x+1}+C$$
6

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^6 x\,dx$

WALLIS FORMULA

For even powers, use the Wallis formula: $$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2n} x\,dx = \frac{(2n-1)!!}{(2n)!!} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2}$$ For $n=3$ (so $\sin^6 x$): $$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^6 x\,dx = \frac{5!!}{6!!}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{5\cdot 3\cdot 1}{6\cdot 4\cdot 2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{15}{48}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{5\pi}{32}$$
