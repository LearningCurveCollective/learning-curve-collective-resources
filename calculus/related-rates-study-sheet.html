One-Page Cheat Sheet

Related rates in one place: the four-step process, the geometry toolbox, the sign rules, and the moves that rescue you when a rate seems to be missing. Everything here is unpacked in the study tabs and backed by six fully worked examples. If you can explain every row below in your own words, you are ready for the quiz.

The four-step process Tab 1

Calc1 Gather givens (rates with signs, snapshot values, constants). 2 Write one simple equation. 3 Differentiate with respect to $t$. 4 Plug in the snapshot values, solve, interpret.

Plain EnglishWrite down everything you know, find one formula that connects it all, take the derivative, and only then feed in the numbers for "that instant."

Translate words into rates Tab 1

CalcEvery "at a rate of" phrase is a derivative, and the units tell you which one: ft/hr is $\frac{dh}{dt}$, ft³/hr is $\frac{dV}{dt}$, ft²/min is $\frac{dA}{dt}$, mph is a distance rate.

Plain EnglishThe problem hands you one rate and asks for another. Spot both before doing anything else; the units are the name tag.

Signs: which way is it changing? Tab 3

CalcFalling, draining, shrinking, deflating, and moving toward a point all mean the variable is decreasing: the rate is negative. Rising, filling, growing, and moving away are positive.

Plain English"At 1/2 ft/hr" is a speed; the story around it decides the sign. Drop the negative and the answer key will have a trap waiting (think $+4$ vs $-4$).

Cones hide a similar triangle Tab 2

CalcThe water cone is always similar to the tank, so $\frac{r}{h}=\frac{R}{H}=c$ (a constant). Tank 18 ft deep with top radius 6 ft: $r=\frac{h}{3}$. Tank 20 ft deep, radius 10: $r=\frac{h}{2}$.

Plain EnglishThe tank's big numbers ($H$, $R$) only matter through this ratio. They are never the values you plug in at the end; the water's snapshot depth is.

Substitute before you differentiate Tab 2

CalcUse the constraint to get down to one variable first: with $r=\frac{h}{2}$, $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^2 h=\frac{\pi h^3}{12}$, so $\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt}$. No product rule, no unknown $\frac{dr}{dt}$.

Plain EnglishIf you product-rule first, an unknown rate appears and you are stuck. Substituting first makes the problem two lines long. (Product rule still works if you also use $r'=\frac{1}{2}h'$; same answer, more arithmetic.)

Boxes: freeze the constant dimension Tab 2

Calc$V=\ell w h$; if $h=10$ never changes, write $V=10\,\ell w$ and product-rule: $\frac{dV}{dt}=10\!\left[\frac{d\ell}{dt}w+\ell\frac{dw}{dt}\right]$. The constant multiplies the whole bracket.

Plain EnglishA fixed dimension is just a number: bake it in before differentiating. Applying it to only one term turns $10(12-32)=-200$ into the wrong $120-32=88$.

Distance problems: Pythagoras Tab 3

CalcLet $x$ and $y$ be each mover's distance from the corner at the asked time; then $x^2+y^2=z^2$ and $\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{x\,x'+y\,y'}{z}$. Moving toward the corner makes that rate negative.

Plain EnglishFirst figure out where everyone actually is at the moment in question (distance = rate × time), then build the triangle. Expect a 3-4-5 family: $(3,4,5)$, $(6,8,10)$, $(30,40,50)$, $(5,12,13)$, $(7,24,25)$.

Two rates given? Divide the equations Tab 3

CalcCube with $\frac{dV}{dt}$ and $\frac{dA}{dt}$ both known: differentiate $V=s^3$ and $A=6s^2$, then divide. $\frac{dV/dt}{dA/dt}=\frac{3s^2\,s'}{12s\,s'}=\frac{s}{4}$, so the unknown rate $s'$ cancels and $s$ pops out.

Plain EnglishTwo given rates and no snapshot usually means you are solving for a dimension. Treat $s'$ as a variable, write both derivative equations, and eliminate it (dividing is fastest; solve-and-substitute also works).

Snapshot values go in last Tab 1

Calc"When the water is 8 ft deep" is a snapshot, not a constant. Substitute $h=8$ into $V$ before differentiating and $V$ becomes a fixed number whose derivative is $0$.

Plain EnglishTrue constants (the tank's size, a ladder's length, a frozen height) can go in early. Values that hold only "at that instant" wait until after the derivative exists.

Constant fill, slowing rise Tab 3

CalcConstant $\frac{dV}{dt}=k$ into a vertex-down cone gives $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi c^2 h^2}$: positive, decreasing, nonlinear. A cylinder, by contrast, fills at a constant $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi r^2}$.

Plain EnglishA cup of water poured into the skinny tip raises the level a lot; the same cup near the wide top barely moves it (pour it into a lake and nothing happens). To prove non-linearity, differentiate again and show the second derivative is not zero.

The shape formula bank Tab 2

CalcCone $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ · Cylinder $V=\pi r^2 h$ · Sphere $V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, $A=4\pi r^2$ · Cube $V=s^3$, $A=6s^2$ · Box $V=\ell wh$, $A=2(\ell w+\ell h+wh)$ · Circle $A=\pi r^2$, $C=2\pi r$ · Right triangle $x^2+y^2=z^2$.

Plain EnglishStep 2 of the process is only fast if these are automatic. The units of the rate you want point to the right formula: a ft³/hr question wants a volume formula.

Interpret the answer Tab 1

CalcA negative $\frac{dV}{dt}$ means the volume is decreasing; "the rate at which water is leaving" is the magnitude. Always attach units: ft³/hr, in³/sec, mph, ft/s.

Plain EnglishOn a multiple-choice question the sign is the trap ($-200$ and $+200$ will both be listed). On a free-response question, the closing sentence with units is its own point.

Numbers worth memorizing. Right-triangle families: (3, 4, 5) and its multiples (6, 8, 10), (30, 40, 50), (60, 80, 100), plus (5, 12, 13) and (7, 24, 25). Cone volume carries a 1/3; sphere volume a 4/3; cube division gives $s=4\cdot\frac{dV/dt}{dA/dt}$. Worked-example answers to sanity-check yourself against: Ex. 1 gives $-2\pi$, Ex. 2 gives $-40$, Ex. 3 gives $-28$, Ex. 4 gives $s=9$, Ex. 5 gives $-\frac{5}{6}$.

How free-response graders score these. Related-rates FRQs typically award separate points for (1) the equation relating the variables, (2) the derivative with correct chain-rule factors, (3) the plug-in and solve, and (4) the answer with units and the right sign or direction. Writing the four-step process down in order earns the points in order.

1

The Four-Step Process

Related-rates problems feel wordy because they are: the calculus is usually two lines, buried in a paragraph of story. The fix is a fixed process. Walk through a full problem below, one step at a time, before reading the details.

Play first: one problem, four steps

Step through the draining-tank problem (this is Worked Example 1). Watch what each step adds, and notice when the snapshot value $h=6$ finally gets used.

The process, step by step

Step 1: gather your givens

Read the whole problem once, then write down everything you know before touching any calculus. In particular, pay attention to the rate they give you: every related-rates problem hands you at least one derivative, disguised as a phrase like "the surface is falling at $\frac{1}{2}$ ft/hr" ($\frac{dh}{dt}=-\frac{1}{2}$). Record signs now, list the snapshot values ("when the water is 6 ft deep") separately, and write what you want: "find $\frac{dV}{dt}$."

Step 2: come up with a simple equation

You need one formula that connects the variable whose rate you want to the variable whose rate you have. The units of the target rate point to it: a ft³/hr question wants a volume formula; mph between two cars wants $x^2+y^2=z^2$. If the formula has an extra variable, hunt for a constraint that removes it (Tab 2).

Step 3: differentiate with respect to $t$

Differentiate both sides implicitly. Every changing variable picks up its own rate by the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dt}(h^3)=3h^2\frac{dh}{dt}$, and a product of two changing variables needs the product rule: $\frac{d}{dt}(\ell w)=\frac{d\ell}{dt}w+\ell\frac{dw}{dt}$. Plain constants (like a frozen height of 10) just ride along in front.

Step 4: plug in last, then interpret

Only now do the snapshot values enter. Substitute, solve for the unknown rate, and close with a sentence: sign (increasing or decreasing?), magnitude, and units. "$\frac{dV}{dt}=-2\pi$, so water is leaving the tank at $2\pi$ ft³/hr."

Sorting the givens: three kinds of numbers

KindHow to spot itWhen it entersExample

Common exam trap: plugging in too early. Substituting a snapshot value like $h=8$ into $V=\frac{\pi h^3}{12}$ before differentiating freezes $h$: $V$ becomes the fixed number $\frac{128\pi}{3}$, its derivative is $0$, and the rate you were solving for has vanished. The "when" values describe one instant of an ongoing motion, so the motion (the derivative) must be written down first.

Exam framing. A typical free-response part reads: "Water drains from the tank described above. At the instant the depth is 6 ft, the depth is decreasing at $\frac{1}{2}$ ft/hr. Find the rate at which the volume of water is changing at that instant. Indicate units of measure." A full-credit answer shows the one-variable equation, the differentiated equation, the substitution, and the sentence $\frac{dV}{dt}=-2\pi$ ft³/hr with "decreasing" stated. That is the four steps, in order, worth roughly one point each.

2

The Geometry Toolbox

Step 2 of the process ("come up with a simple equation") is where most problems are won or lost. Each shape hides one extra fact that turns a stuck two-variable formula into a clean one-variable one. Play with the cone below to see the most important fact of all.

Play first: the similar triangle inside every cone

Drag the water level up and down. The water's radius $r$ and depth $h$ both change, but their ratio never does: the water cone stays similar to the tank (here $H=18$, $R=6$).

Water depth

A. Cones: similar triangles, then substitute Ex. 1

The move

$V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ has two changing variables, and the problem only gives you $\frac{dh}{dt}$. The rescue is the cross-section: the water always forms a triangle similar to the tank's, so $\frac{r}{h}=\frac{R}{H}=c$, a constant. Solve for whichever variable you want to eliminate: you were given $\frac{dh}{dt}$, so keep $h$ and set $r=ch$.

$$r=\frac{h}{3}\;\Rightarrow\; V=\frac{1}{3}\pi\Big(\frac{h}{3}\Big)^2 h=\frac{\pi h^3}{27}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

One variable before differentiating means one clean chain rule and no product rule at all.

The alternative route

Product-ruling $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$ directly is not wrong, but it produces a $\frac{dr}{dt}$ the problem never gave you. You can still finish by differentiating the constraint ($r=ch\Rightarrow \frac{dr}{dt}=c\,\frac{dh}{dt}$) and substituting both $r=ch$ and that rate; the answer matches. Substituting first is simply fewer chances to slip.

B. Boxes: freeze the constant, product-rule the rest Ex. 2

The move

All 3-D shapes track three dimensions; a box tracks them separately as $V=\ell wh$. When one dimension is fixed ("the height is 10 in"), it is a true constant: write it as a number immediately, $V=10\,\ell w$, and product-rule the two that actually change.

$$\frac{dV}{dt}=10\left[\frac{d\ell}{dt}\,w+\ell\,\frac{dw}{dt}\right]$$

The 10 sits outside the bracket and multiplies both product-rule terms. Compute the bracket first, then scale.

C. One-variable shapes: spheres, circles, cylinders

The move

Some shapes need no constraint because one variable already runs the show. A sphere's size is all radius: $V=\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ gives $\frac{dV}{dt}=4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}$ in one line. A ripple is a circle: $A=\pi r^2$ gives $\frac{dA}{dt}=2\pi r\frac{dr}{dt}$. A cylinder with fixed radius is effectively one-variable too: $V=\pi r^2h$ with $r$ frozen gives $\frac{dV}{dt}=\pi r^2\frac{dh}{dt}$.

Common exam trap: distributing the constant to only one term. With $\frac{dV}{dt}=10[\,\ell'w+\ell w'\,]$ and a bracket of $12-32=-20$, the answer is $10(-20)=-200$. Applying the 10 to only the first term gives $120-32=88$, which will not be among the answer choices, and on a grid-in it is simply wrong. Finish the bracket, then multiply.

Memory hook. Match the complaint to the cure: "two variables, one rate" → similar triangles (cone). "Three variables" → one of them is probably frozen (box). "No rate for $r$" → eliminate $r$ before differentiating. "Two rates, no snapshot" → divide the equations (cube). "Two moving objects" → Pythagoras with positions at the asked time.

Signs, Snapshots & Two-Rate Problems

The calculus in a related-rates problem is rarely what costs points; the bookkeeping is. This tab is the bookkeeping: signs, where everything is at the asked moment, and the special problems that hand you two rates or no numbers at all.

Play first: two cars and a shrinking hypotenuse

Car A drives north at 60 mph and reaches the intersection at noon. Car B is 160 mi east at noon, driving toward the intersection at 80 mph. Drag time forward and watch $\frac{dz}{dt}$: the cars close in, then the sign flips.

Time after noon

Sign conventions: the phrase decides

The problem saysYou writeWhy
"the surface is falling at $\frac{1}{2}$ ft/hr"$\frac{dh}{dt}=-\frac{1}{2}$depth is decreasing
"moving toward the intersection at 80 mph"$\frac{dx}{dt}=-80$distance from the corner is shrinking
"the width decreases at 4 in/sec"$\frac{dw}{dt}=-4$decreasing is negative, even inside a product rule
"air leaks from the balloon at $36\pi$ cm³/min"$\frac{dV}{dt}=-36\pi$volume is going down
"sand is added at 10 ft³/min" / "driving away at 55 mph"$\frac{dV}{dt}=+10$ / $\frac{dx}{dt}=+55$growing and receding are positive

And on the way back out: a negative result means decreasing. $\frac{dz}{dt}=-14$ mph is read "the cars are getting closer at 14 mph."

Where is everything at the asked time? Ex. 3

The move

Distance problems describe a starting arrangement ("at noon, car A reaches the intersection; car B is 160 mi away") but ask about a later instant ("at 1 PM"). Before building the triangle, advance every object: distance = rate × time. At 1 PM, A is $60(1)=60$ mi north; B has covered $80$ of its $160$ mi, so it is $160-80=80$ mi east. Then $z=\sqrt{60^2+80^2}=100$ (a scaled 3-4-5), and

$$z\,\frac{dz}{dt}=x\,\frac{dx}{dt}+y\,\frac{dy}{dt}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{(80)(-80)+(60)(60)}{100}=\frac{-6400+3600}{100}=-28\ \text{mph}$$

Note the $-80$: car B moves toward the corner. Forget that sign and you get $+100$ mph, a classic wrong answer choice.

A side note the widget shows: the moment $\frac{dz}{dt}=0$ is the cars' closest approach; after it, the distance grows again. Setting $x\,x'+y\,y'=0$ finds that time.

Two rates, no snapshot: solve for the dimension Ex. 4

The move

A cube's volume grows at $\frac{dV}{dt}=36$ in³/min while its surface area grows at $\frac{dA}{dt}=16$ in²/min; find the edge. Nothing to plug in? Then the dimension itself is the unknown. Differentiate both formulas and treat $\frac{ds}{dt}$ as a second unknown: two equations, two unknowns.

$$36=3s^2\,\frac{ds}{dt},\qquad 16=12s\,\frac{ds}{dt}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{36}{16}=\frac{3s^2}{12s}=\frac{s}{4}\;\Rightarrow\; s=9\ \text{in}$$

Dividing the equations cancels $\frac{ds}{dt}$ in one stroke. (Yes, you may divide one whole equation by another, just as you may add or subtract them.) The slower route works too: solve the second for $\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{16}{12s}$ and substitute into the first.

No numbers at all: describe the rate's behavior Ex. 6

The move

Conceptual version: water pours into a vertex-down cone at a constant rate; is $\frac{dh}{dt}$ constant, linear, or neither? No tank dimensions are given, so name the ratio a letter: $r=ch$ for some constant $c$. Then $V=\frac{\pi c^2}{3}h^3$, and with $\frac{dV}{dt}=k$:

$$k=\pi c^2 h^2\,\frac{dh}{dt}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi c^2 h^2}$$

Positive (water rises), but the $h^2$ downstairs means it shrinks as the level climbs, and not along a straight line. To certify "nonlinear," differentiate once more: $\frac{d^2h}{dt^2}=-\frac{2k}{\pi c^2h^3}\frac{dh}{dt}<0$ and depends on $h$, so the rate is not constant and not linear.

Constant inflow, vertex-down cone: the depth keeps rising but ever more slowly, so its rate curve decays. A cup of water poured near the empty tip jumps the level; the same cup near the wide top (or into a lake) barely moves it.

Common exam trap: plugging in the container instead of the contents. In the draining-tank problem, the 18 and the 6 describe the tank; they enter only through the ratio $c=\frac{1}{3}$. The value that goes into $\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi h^2}{9}h'$ is the water's snapshot depth. Substituting the tank's full depth is a tempting slip, and multiple-choice writers know it: the "full depth" answer is usually sitting among the options.

Exam framing. "Is $h$ increasing at an increasing or decreasing rate? Justify." The justification is the sign of the second derivative (or the sign of $\frac{d}{dh}\big(\frac{dh}{dt}\big)$): state $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi c^2h^2}>0$ so $h$ increases, and since $h$ increases, $\frac{k}{\pi c^2 h^2}$ decreases, so $h$ increases at a decreasing rate. Two sentences, full credit.

4

Worked Examples

Six problems spanning every pattern on this sheet. Set each one up yourself first (givens, equation, derivative), then open the solution and compare line by line. The quiz questions reuse these setups with different numbers.

Ex. 1 · The draining conical tank

Similar triangles · substitute first · negative rate

A conical reservoir, vertex down, is 18 ft deep and has a top radius of 6 ft. Water leaks out so that the surface is falling at $\frac{1}{2}$ ft/hr. At what rate, in ft³/hr, is water leaving the reservoir when the water is 6 ft deep?

Answer: water is leaving at $2\pi$ ft³/hr (that is, $\frac{dV}{dt}=-2\pi$).

Givens. Tank: $H=18$, $R=6$ (constants). Rate: $\frac{dh}{dt}=-\frac{1}{2}$ ft/hr (falling). Snapshot: $h=6$. Want: $\frac{dV}{dt}$.

Equation. $V=\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$, with similar triangles $\frac{r}{h}=\frac{6}{18}=\frac{1}{3}$, so $r=\frac{h}{3}$ and $V=\frac{\pi h^3}{27}$.

Differentiate. $\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi h^2}{9}\,\frac{dh}{dt}$.

Plug in. $\frac{dV}{dt}=\frac{\pi(36)}{9}\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)=4\pi\cdot\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)=-2\pi$. Negative, so volume is decreasing: water leaves at $2\pi$ ft³/hr.

Trap check. Plugging in the tank's depth 18 instead of the water's depth 6 gives $-18\pi$; dropping the negative gives $+2\pi$; assuming $r=\frac{h}{2}$ (the classic 20-and-10 tank from other versions of this problem) changes the constant. All three are the kinds of wrong answers test writers list.

Ex. 2 · The box with a frozen height

Constant dimension · product rule · distribute the constant

A rectangular box has a constant height of 5 in. Its length increases at 3 in/sec and its width decreases at 2 in/sec. When the length is 10 in and the width is 4 in, at what rate, in in³/sec, is the volume changing?

Answer: $\frac{dV}{dt}=-40$ in³/sec (the volume is shrinking at 40 in³/sec).

Givens. $h=5$ (constant, so $\frac{dh}{dt}=0$). $\frac{d\ell}{dt}=+3$, $\frac{dw}{dt}=-2$. Snapshot: $\ell=10$, $w=4$. Want: $\frac{dV}{dt}$.

Equation. $V=\ell wh=5\,\ell w$ once the constant is frozen in.

Differentiate. $\frac{dV}{dt}=5\left[\frac{d\ell}{dt}w+\ell\frac{dw}{dt}\right]$.

Plug in. $\frac{dV}{dt}=5\big[(3)(4)+(10)(-2)\big]=5\,[12-20]=5(-8)=-40$.

Trap check. The 5 multiplies the whole bracket: $5\cdot12-20=40$ is the only-one-term error. And the width's rate stays negative inside the product rule; making it $+2$ gives $+160$. The answer choices will contain both.

Ex. 3 · Two cars on perpendicular roads

Pythagoras · positions at the asked time · toward = negative

Car A travels north at 60 mph and reaches the intersection at noon. At that moment car B is 160 mi east of the intersection, traveling toward it at 80 mph. At 1 PM, at what rate, in mph, is the distance between the cars changing?

Answer: $\frac{dz}{dt}=-28$ mph (the cars are getting closer at 28 mph).

Givens. $\frac{dy}{dt}=+60$ (A moving away, north of the corner). $\frac{dx}{dt}=-80$ (B moving toward the corner). At 1 PM: $y=60(1)=60$; $x=160-80(1)=80$. So $z=\sqrt{80^2+60^2}=100$, a scaled 3-4-5.

Equation. $x^2+y^2=z^2$.

Differentiate. $2x\,x'+2y\,y'=2z\,z'$, so $z'=\dfrac{x\,x'+y\,y'}{z}$.

Plug in. $z'=\dfrac{(80)(-80)+(60)(60)}{100}=\dfrac{-6400+3600}{100}=\dfrac{-2800}{100}=-28$.

Trap check. Using the noon picture ($x=160$, $y=0$) answers the wrong instant. Dropping the sign on $x'$ gives $+100$. Reporting $+28$ misses that the distance is shrinking. Three traps, one problem.

Ex. 4 · The cube with two given rates

Two equations · divide to cancel the rate · solve for a dimension

The volume of a cube is increasing at 36 in³/min and its surface area is increasing at 16 in²/min. What is the length of each edge of the cube?

Answer: $s=9$ in.

Givens. $\frac{dV}{dt}=36$, $\frac{dA}{dt}=16$. Unknowns: $s$ and $\frac{ds}{dt}$. Want: $s$.

Equations. $V=s^3$ and $A=6s^2$.

Differentiate both. $36=3s^2\frac{ds}{dt}$ and $16=12s\frac{ds}{dt}$.

Divide. $\dfrac{36}{16}=\dfrac{3s^2}{12s}=\dfrac{s}{4}$, so $s=\dfrac{4\cdot36}{16}=9$.

Check. From the area equation $\frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{16}{12\cdot 9}=\frac{4}{27}$; then $3(81)\frac{4}{27}=36$. Both equations agree, so $s=9$ is consistent. Answering $\frac{36}{16}=2.25$ (the ratio itself) forgets the $\frac{s}{4}$.

Ex. 5 · The sliding ladder

Constant hypotenuse · 5-12-13 · opposite signs

A 13-ft ladder leans against a wall. The base slides away from the wall at 2 ft/sec. When the base is 5 ft from the wall, at what rate is the top of the ladder sliding down?

Answer: the top slides down at $\frac{5}{6}$ ft/sec ($\frac{dy}{dt}=-\frac{5}{6}$).

Givens. $L=13$ (true constant). $\frac{dx}{dt}=+2$ (base moving away). Snapshot: $x=5$, so $y=\sqrt{169-25}=12$ (the 5-12-13 triple).

Equation. $x^2+y^2=13^2$. The constant right side is the whole point: its derivative is 0.

Differentiate. $2x\,x'+2y\,y'=0$, so $y'=-\dfrac{x\,x'}{y}$.

Plug in. $y'=-\dfrac{(5)(2)}{12}=-\dfrac{5}{6}$.

Trap check. The top does not fall at 2 ft/sec just because the base moves at 2 ft/sec; the rates are linked by the positions. And a ladder problem where the base moves away must have the top moving down: if your signs agree, re-check them.

Ex. 6 · Filling a cone at a constant rate (conceptual)

No numbers · name the ratio c · behavior of dh/dt

Water is poured into a vertex-down conical reservoir at a constant rate. If $h(t)$ is the depth of the water, is $\frac{dh}{dt}$ constant, linear and decreasing, linear and increasing, nonlinear and increasing, or nonlinear and decreasing?

Answer: nonlinear and decreasing (positive, but shrinking as the level climbs, and not along a line).

Intuition first. A cup of water poured into the nearly empty tip raises the level a lot; the same cup poured near the wide top barely moves it. So the level always rises, ever more slowly.

The math. No dimensions are given, so set $r=ch$ with $c=\frac{R}{H}$ a constant. Then $V=\frac{\pi c^2}{3}h^3$, and with $\frac{dV}{dt}=k$ constant: $k=\pi c^2h^2\frac{dh}{dt}$, so $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi c^2h^2}$.

Certify it. That expression is positive and falls as $h$ grows. Differentiating again gives $\frac{d^2h}{dt^2}=-\frac{2k}{\pi c^2h^3}\frac{dh}{dt}<0$, which is itself not constant, so $h(t)$ is concave down and $\frac{dh}{dt}$ is genuinely nonlinear. Contrast: a cylinder gives $\frac{dh}{dt}=\frac{k}{\pi r^2}$, a true constant.
