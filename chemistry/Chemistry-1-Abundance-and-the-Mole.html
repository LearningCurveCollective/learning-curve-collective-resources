One-Page Cheat Sheet

This sheet covers the first chemistry unit on counting atoms: what a mole is, abundance and average atomic mass, molar mass, and the conversions between atoms, moles and grams. If you can explain every row below in your own words and do the calculation next to it, you know the material. The study tabs teach each idea with a widget you can play with, then the practice tabs check it.

A mole is a counting number

ChemA mole is a group of $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms or molecules. That number is called Avogadro's number. One mole of anything has that many pieces in it.

Plain English"Mole" works like "dozen" or "ton." A dozen means 12, a ton means 2,000 pounds, a mole means $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. It is huge only because atoms are tiny.

Scientific notation: handle the two parts separately

ChemTo divide, divide the front numbers and subtract the exponents. To multiply, multiply the front numbers and add the exponents. If the front number ends up 10 or more, move the decimal one place left and add 1 to the exponent.

Plain EnglishExample: $4.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 27.09 \times 10^{23}$, which is written $2.709 \times 10^{24}$. Same amount, tidier form.

Counting atoms inside molecules

ChemThe subscript after a symbol tells you how many of that atom are in one molecule. No subscript means 1. Add the subscripts to get atoms per molecule, then multiply by the number of molecules.

Plain EnglishH$_2$O has 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen, so 3 atoms. 50 water molecules hold $50 \times 3 = 150$ atoms. CaSO$_4$ has $1 + 1 + 4 = 6$ atoms.

Abundance = part ÷ whole

ChemAbundance is the fraction of all the atoms that are one isotope, written as a decimal. Add up every isotope count to get the whole, then divide each isotope's count by it. The abundances should add to 1.

Plain EnglishBoron: 26 + 4 + 10 = 40 atoms. B-11 is $\frac{26}{40} = 0.65$, B-12 is $\frac{4}{40} = 0.10$, B-13 is $\frac{10}{40} = 0.25$. If the percents are already given (63%, 27%, 10%), just move the decimal: 0.63, 0.27, 0.10.

Mass number vs the number on the periodic table

ChemThe number after an isotope's name (Boron-11, Oxygen-18) is its mass number: protons plus neutrons. All isotopes of one element have the same protons and differ only in neutrons. The decimal on the periodic table (B is 10.81) is the average over all the isotopes.

Plain EnglishWhen a problem says "mass number," use the number in the name. When it says "molar mass" or "atomic mass," use the periodic table.

Mass contributed = abundance × mass number

ChemEach isotope "contributes" a share of the average equal to its abundance times its mass number. This is the weighted part of a weighted average: common isotopes count more.

Plain EnglishIf a test says "element X-40 has abundance 0.8, find the mass contributed," the answer is $0.8 \times 40 = 32$. Nothing else to do.

Average atomic mass in 3 steps

Chem1. Find each abundance (part ÷ whole). 2. Multiply each abundance by its mass number. 3. Add all the mass contributed. The answer is the average atomic mass, the decimal you see on the periodic table.

Plain EnglishBoron: $7.15 + 1.20 + 3.25 = 11.60$. Check: the average has to land between the lightest and heaviest isotope, and closest to the most common one.

Molar mass: subscript × atomic mass, then add

ChemMolar mass is how many grams 1 mole weighs, in g/mol. For one element it is the atomic mass on the periodic table. For a compound, multiply each element's atomic mass by its subscript and add everything up.

Plain English1 mole of O weighs 16.00 g. O$_2$ weighs 32.00 g because 16 × 2. CO$_2$ weighs $12.01 + 2(16.00) = 44.01$ g. Same idea every time.

Atoms to moles, moles to atoms

ChemSee atoms or molecules? Divide by Avogadro's number to get moles. See moles? Multiply by Avogadro's number to get atoms. The number you are given tells you which way to go.

Plain English4.5 mol of Au is $4.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.709 \times 10^{24}$ atoms. $1.806 \times 10^{23}$ atoms of Al is $1.806 \times 10^{23} \div 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 0.3$ mol.

Grams to moles, moles to grams

ChemSee grams? Divide by the molar mass to get moles. See moles? Multiply by the molar mass to get grams. First find the molar mass of the compound, then do the one step.

Plain English2 mol of CaSO$_4$ weighs $2 \times 136.15 = 272.30$ g. Think "two dozen CaSO$_4$": you have 2 of the 136.15 g bundles. 30 g of CaSO$_4$ is $30 \div 136.15 = 0.2203$ mol, less than one bundle.

Atoms to grams takes two steps

ChemThere is no direct road from atoms to grams. Go atoms to moles (divide by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$), then moles to grams (multiply by the molar mass). Grams to atoms is the same road backwards.

Plain English$5.55 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Cu: step 1 gives 9.2193 mol, step 2 gives $9.2193 \times 63.55 = 585.88$ g.

Which tool? Isotopes of one element, or a molecule of different elements

Chem"Mass contributed" and "average atomic mass" are only for isotopes of a single element (B-11, B-12, B-13). A molecule like CO$_2$ mixes different elements, so you add atomic masses from the periodic table instead.

Plain EnglishIf you see a dash and a number (Boron-11), think abundance. If you see a formula with subscripts (CO$_2$), think molar mass.

Numbers to know. Avogadro's number is $6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Atomic masses used on this sheet: H 1.01, C 12.01, O 16.00, Al 26.98, P 30.97, S 32.07, Cl 35.45, Ca 40.08, Cu 63.55 (use your own periodic table; some tables round a little differently). Results to recognize: boron average 11.60, nitrogen 14.50, oxygen 16.47; CaSO$_4$ 136.15 g/mol, Al$_2$O$_3$ 101.96 g/mol, CO$_2$ 44.01 g/mol; 50 H$_2$O molecules hold 150 atoms.

What a mole is

Mole

What we call a group of $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms or molecules. It is the ratio between moles and atoms: 1 mole = $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ atoms or molecules. A mole of carbon atoms, a mole of water molecules, even a mole of dollars: the word means the same count every time.

Avogadro's number

$6.02 \times 10^{23}$. Named after the scientist who got to name the count. It is often written "A#" for short. It has no units of its own; you say what you are counting, the way you say "a dozen eggs."

Why so big?

Atoms are tiny, so it takes an enormous number of them to make an amount you can hold. A mole of carbon atoms is about 12 g, a spoonful. A ton is 2,000 pounds for the same reason: a pound is too small a unit for a truck.

Scientific notation: the two moves you need

Avogadro's number forces you to multiply and divide in scientific notation. Treat the front number and the power of 10 as two separate jobs.

Rule: multiplying
Multiply the front numbers. Keep the power of 10. If the front number is 10 or more, move the decimal one place left and add 1 to the exponent.
Example
$4.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 27.09 \times 10^{23} = 2.709 \times 10^{24}$
Rule: dividing
Divide the front numbers. Subtract the exponents (top minus bottom). If the exponents match, they cancel and you only divide the front numbers.
Example
$\dfrac{9.02 \times 10^{25}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 1.498 \times 10^{2} = 149.8$ and $\dfrac{1.806 \times 10^{23}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 0.3$

On a calculator, type the whole thing with the EE or EXP key and let it handle the exponents. The rules above are for checking that the answer is the right size.

Reading a formula: subscripts count atoms

Rule
The small number after a symbol is how many of that atom are in one molecule. No small number means 1. Add them for the atoms in one molecule, then multiply by how many molecules you have.
Example
H$_2$O: 2 H + 1 O = 3 atoms. 50 molecules of H$_2$O: $50 \times 3 = 150$ atoms. You can also do it per element: $50 \times 2 = 100$ H and $50 \times 1 = 50$ O, and $100 + 50 = 150$.

Common mistakes and what to check. Leaving an answer as $27.09 \times 10^{23}$: the front number should be between 1 and 10, so rewrite it as $2.709 \times 10^{24}$. Treating "mole" as a unit like grams: it is a count, so always say a mole of what. Reading CaSO$_4$ as 4 of everything: the 4 belongs to the oxygen only.

Exam framing. "How many atoms of Au are in 4.5 mols of Au?" A full-credit answer shows the rule and the arithmetic: you see moles, so multiply by Avogadro's number: $4.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.709 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Au. One line of setup, one line of math, units on the answer.

Abundance and Average Atomic Mass

Most elements come in a few versions called isotopes, which differ only in neutrons. The decimal on the periodic table is a weighted average of those versions. This tab is the three-step recipe for that average. Try the calculator with the boron example, then change the numbers.

Step 1: abundance = part ÷ whole

Formula
$$\text{abundance} = \frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \frac{\text{atoms of this isotope}}{\text{total atoms of the element}}$$

Part: the count for one isotope. Whole: every isotope's count added together. The result is a decimal between 0 and 1, and all the abundances add to 1.

Rule
Add the counts first to get the whole. Then divide each count by that same whole. Never divide by the number of isotopes.
Example: boron
Whole: $26 + 4 + 10 = 40$. B-11: $\frac{26}{40} = 0.65$. B-12: $\frac{4}{40} = 0.10$. B-13: $\frac{10}{40} = 0.25$. Check: $0.65 + 0.10 + 0.25 = 1$.
Rule: already in percents
If the counts are given as percents, the whole is 100%. Convert each percent to a decimal by dividing by 100.
Example: oxygen
63%, 27%, 10%: abundances are 0.63, 0.27, 0.10. The "find the total" box is 100%. This one is a quick check, not a calculation.

Step 2: mass contributed = abundance × mass number

The mass number is the number in the isotope's name. Boron-11 has mass number 11; it is protons plus neutrons. Every boron atom has 5 protons, so B-11 has 6 neutrons, B-12 has 7, B-13 has 8.

Rule
Multiply each isotope's abundance (a decimal) by its mass number (the number in the name). That product is the mass that isotope contributes to the average.
Example: boron
B-11: $0.65 \times 11 = 7.15$. B-12: $0.10 \times 12 = 1.20$. B-13: $0.25 \times 13 = 3.25$.

A test can ask for this step on its own: "Element X-40 has abundance 0.8. What mass does it contribute?" Answer: $0.8 \times 40 = 32$.

Step 3: add the mass contributed

$$\text{average atomic mass} = (\text{ab}_1 \times \text{mass}_1) + (\text{ab}_2 \times \text{mass}_2) + (\text{ab}_3 \times \text{mass}_3)$$

Boron: $7.15 + 1.20 + 3.25 = 11.60$. The periodic table says 10.81 for real boron because the real abundances are different from the practice numbers; the method is the same.

Sanity check. The average lands between the lightest and heaviest isotope, closest to the most common one. Boron's 11.60 sits near 11 because 65% of the atoms are B-11.

The three steps in words, for the "break it down" box

  1. Find each isotope's abundance: part ÷ whole, as a decimal.
  2. Find the mass each isotope contributes: abundance × mass number.
  3. Add all the mass contributed together. That sum is the average atomic mass.

Common mistakes and what to check. Dividing by 3 because there are three isotopes: the whole is the total number of atoms (40 for boron), not the number of rows. Using 10.81 from the periodic table instead of 11 for B-11: step 2 needs the mass number from the name. Multiplying by 65 instead of 0.65: abundance goes in as a decimal. Applying "mass contributed" to CO$_2$: that is a molecule of different elements, so add atomic masses instead (tab 3).

Exam framing. "Find the average atomic mass of nitrogen: N-14 45 atoms, N-15 30 atoms, N-16 5 atoms." Show the whole (80), the three abundances (0.5625, 0.375, 0.0625), the three products (7.875, 5.625, 1.00), and the sum, 14.50. A grader can follow every number back to the step it came from.

Molar Mass and Conversions

Molar mass is the bridge between moles and grams; Avogadro's number is the bridge between moles and atoms. Every conversion problem is one trip across one bridge, or two trips with moles in the middle. Use the map first: pick what you have and what you want, and watch the route light up.

Play first, read second

Atoms, moles, grams: the map

Type numbers like 9.02e25 for $9.02 \times 10^{25}$.

Molar mass

Formula
$$\text{molar mass} = (\text{subscript} \times \text{atomic mass})_{\text{1st element}} + (\text{subscript} \times \text{atomic mass})_{\text{2nd element}} + \ldots \quad \text{(g/mol)}$$

Subscript: how many atoms of that element are in the formula (1 if there is no small number). Atomic mass: the decimal under the symbol on the periodic table. Add one term per element.

Rule: one element
The molar mass of an element is its atomic mass from the periodic table, in g/mol. 1 mole of Ca weighs 40.08 g.
Example
O: 16.00 g/mol. O$_2$ (oxygen gas, two atoms stuck together): $2 \times 16.00 = 32.00$ g/mol. Cl$_2$: $2 \times 35.45 = 70.90$ g/mol.
Rule: a compound
Multiply each element's atomic mass by its subscript, then add the pieces. The subscript multiplies only the element it sits next to.
Example
CaSO$_4$: $40.08 + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 40.08 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 136.15$ g/mol. Al$_2$O$_3$: $2(26.98) + 3(16.00) = 53.96 + 48.00 = 101.96$ g/mol.

One bridge at a time

Four rules cover every conversion. Each one is "look at what you were given, then do one operation."

The "two dozen" picture. One mole of CaSO$_4$ is a bundle that weighs 136.15 g. Two moles is two bundles: $2 \times 136.15 = 272.30$ g. 30 g is less than one bundle, so the moles come out below 1: $30 \div 136.15 = 0.2203$ mol. If your answer is bigger than 1 when you have less than one bundle's worth of grams, the division went the other way.

Two bridges: atoms to grams and back

Atoms and grams are not next to each other on the map. Moles sit in the middle, so it always takes 2 steps.

Rule: atoms to grams
Step 1: atoms ÷ $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ gives moles. Step 2: moles × molar mass gives grams.
Example
$5.55 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Cu. Step 1: $\div\, 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 9.2193$ mol. Step 2: $9.2193 \times 63.55 = 585.88$ g.
Rule: grams to atoms
Step 1: grams ÷ molar mass gives moles. Step 2: moles × $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ gives atoms.
Example
250 g of Cu. Step 1: $250 \div 63.55 = 3.9339$ mol. Step 2: $3.9339 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.3682 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.

Common mistakes and what to check. Multiplying every element by the subscript: in CaSO$_4$ the 4 multiplies oxygen only. Forgetting the "2 mols" in front: find the molar mass first, then multiply by how many moles you have. Dividing when you should multiply: if you have moles, the number of atoms or grams gets bigger; if you have grams or atoms, the moles usually come out smaller. Rounding the molar mass to a whole number: keep the decimals from the table.

Exam framing. "If I have 55 g of Al$_2$O$_3$, how many mols can I create?" Full credit shows both pieces: the molar mass, $2(26.98) + 3(16.00) = 101.96$ g/mol, then the rule (grams, so divide) and the division, $55 \div 101.96 = 0.5394$ mol. The sub-question "how much does 1 mole of this weigh?" is where the work starts.

Worked Examples

Abundances and average atomic mass

Abundance · mass contributed · average

Boron: B-11 26 atoms, B-12 4 atoms, B-13 10 atoms. Nitrogen: N-14 45, N-15 30, N-16 5. Oxygen: O-16 63%, O-17 27%, O-18 10%.

Boron. Total $26 + 4 + 10 = 40$. Abundances $\frac{26}{40} = 0.65$, $\frac{4}{40} = 0.10$, $\frac{10}{40} = 0.25$. Mass contributed $0.65 \times 11 = 7.15$, $0.10 \times 12 = 1.20$, $0.25 \times 13 = 3.25$. Average $= 11.60$.

Nitrogen. Total $45 + 30 + 5 = 80$. Abundances $0.5625$, $0.375$, $0.0625$. Mass contributed $7.875$, $5.625$, $1.00$. Average $= 14.50$.

Oxygen. Total $100\%$. Abundances $0.63$, $0.27$, $0.10$ (percents to decimals). Mass contributed $10.08$, $4.59$, $1.80$. Average $= 16.47$.

Atoms to moles, moles to atoms

Avogadro's number

How many atoms of Au are in 4.5 mols? How many mols of Au are in $9.02 \times 10^{25}$ atoms? How many atoms of Al are in 7.5 mols? How many mols of Al are in $1.806 \times 10^{23}$ atoms?

Au, 4.5 mol (moles, so multiply): $4.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 27.09 \times 10^{23} = 2.709 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.

Au, $9.02 \times 10^{25}$ atoms (atoms, so divide): $\frac{9.02 \times 10^{25}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 1.4983 \times 10^{2} = 149.83$ mol.

Al, 7.5 mol: $7.5 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 45.15 \times 10^{23} = 4.515 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.

Al, $1.806 \times 10^{23}$ atoms: $\frac{1.806 \times 10^{23}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 0.3$ mol. The exponents match, so they cancel.

Molar mass table

Subscript × atomic mass, then add

Ca, C$_3$H$_8$, H$_2$O$_2$, CaSO$_4$, Cl$_2$, AlPO$_4$, P$_2$O$_5$, Al$_2$O$_3$.

Ca $= 40.08$. Cl$_2 = 2(35.45) = 70.90$. H$_2$O$_2 = 2(1.01) + 2(16.00) = 34.02$. CaSO$_4 = 40.08 + 32.07 + 64.00 = 136.15$.

AlPO$_4 = 26.98 + 30.97 + 64.00 = 121.95$. P$_2$O$_5 = 61.94 + 80.00 = 141.94$. Al$_2$O$_3 = 53.96 + 48.00 = 101.96$.

C$_3$H$_8$ (propane) $= 36.03 + 8.08 = 44.11$. All in g/mol.

Grams to moles, moles to grams

Molar mass bridge

2 mols of CaSO$_4$ to grams. 30 g of CaSO$_4$ to mols. 4.5 mols of Al$_2$O$_3$ to grams. 55 g of Al$_2$O$_3$ to mols.

CaSO$_4$ is 136.15 g/mol. 2 mol: $2 \times 136.15 = 272.30$ g. 30 g: $30 \div 136.15 = 0.2203$ mol.

Al$_2$O$_3$ is 101.96 g/mol. 4.5 mol: $4.5 \times 101.96 = 458.82$ g. 55 g: $55 \div 101.96 = 0.5394$ mol.

Challenge questions

Counting atoms · two-step conversions

50 molecules of H$_2$O: how many atoms in total? How many steps from atoms to grams, and what are they? How many grams would $5.55 \times 10^{24}$ atoms of Cu weigh? How many atoms are in 250 g of Cu?

Water. One H$_2$O has $2 + 1 = 3$ atoms, so $50 \times 3 = 150$ atoms.

Steps. Two. Atoms to moles (divide by $6.02 \times 10^{23}$), then moles to grams (multiply by the molar mass).

Cu, $5.55 \times 10^{24}$ atoms. $\frac{5.55 \times 10^{24}}{6.02 \times 10^{23}} = 9.2193$ mol, then $9.2193 \times 63.55 = 585.88$ g.

Cu, 250 g. $250 \div 63.55 = 3.9339$ mol, then $3.9339 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.3682 \times 10^{24}$ atoms.

Two definitions to finish

Vocabulary

Complete each one: "1 mole of ____ weighs ____ g." "Part / whole = ____."

1 mole of any element or compound weighs its molar mass in grams. Examples: 1 mole of C weighs 12.01 g; 1 mole of O$_2$ weighs 32.00 g; 1 mole of CO$_2$ weighs 44.01 g.

Part / whole = abundance, written as a decimal.
