One-Page Cheat Sheet

This sheet covers a first unit on atomic structure and the periodic table: electron configuration, quantum numbers, magnetism, atomic radius, ionization energy, electronegativity and nuclear charge. It comes in two parts. Part A (tabs 1 to 3) is where electrons live: shells, the snake through the periodic table, orbital boxes, quantum numbers. Part B (tab 4) is why the trends happen, and every "why" comes back to full shells and distance from the nucleus. extra depth marks detail that goes past what most first-year courses ask for.

Part A

Where electrons live

Shells and how many electrons fit: $2n^2$

ChemShells are numbered energy levels, $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, counting outward from the nucleus. Shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32. That is the capacity of each shell on its own, not a running total.

Plain EnglishRings around the nucleus, and the farther out you go, the more seats on the ring. The first ring is full at 2 (helium), the second at 8 (neon).

Sublevels: s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14

ChemEach shell is split into sublevels, lettered s, p, d, f. Each sublevel is a set of boxes (orbitals) that hold 2 electrons each: s has 1 box, p has 3, d has 5, f has 7. Shell 1 has only s; shell 2 has s and p; shell 3 has s, p, d; shell 4 adds f.

Plain EnglishShells are numbered, sublevels are lettered. "2p" means the p sublevel inside shell 2, and it holds 6. The first shell is 1s, which holds 2; there is no "p shell."

The snake: the periodic table is the filling order

ChemElectrons fill the lowest-energy sublevel first. The periodic table is laid out in that order: read left to right across a row, then wrap to the next row. The two left columns are the s block, the middle ten are the d block, the right six are the p block, and the two rows at the bottom are the f block. A block's width is its capacity: 2, 10, 6, 14.

Plain EnglishCount boxes as you go: "2 boxes, then 6 boxes, 2 boxes, 6 boxes, then 2, then 10, then 6." Stop when you reach the element and write down what you walked through.

d is one behind, f is two behind

ChemIn any row $n$, the s and p blocks are $n$s and $n$p. The d block is $(n-1)$d and the f block is $(n-2)$f. So row 4 reads 4s, 3d, 4p; row 6 reads 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p. The full order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p.

Plain EnglishYes, the d block really is "down one." That is why 4s fills before 3d, and why the first twenty elements go 2, 8, 8, 2 by shell instead of filling shell 3 to 18.

Noble gas shortcut

ChemEverything up to the end of the previous row is the configuration of that row's noble gas. Replace it with the symbol in brackets and write only the rest. Ti $= 1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^2 = [\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^2$.

Plain EnglishGo back one row, find the noble gas on the far right, and start from there. Calcium is [Ar] 4s$^2$; cesium is [Xe] 6s$^1$.

Shell chart: regroup by shell number

ChemA shell chart (or Bohr diagram) lists electrons per shell: 2, 8, 18, 18, 2 for cadmium. Build it from the configuration by adding up every term with the same number in front, whatever order they filled in. $3s^2 + 3p^6 + 3d^{10} = 18$ in shell 3.

Plain EnglishThe number in front says which ring the electron lives on. 3d lives on ring 3 even though you wrote it after 4s. The last ring holds the valence electrons.

Orbital boxes: Hund's rule and Pauli

ChemDraw the boxes for a sublevel (1, 3, 5 or 7). Hund's rule: put one up arrow in each box, left to right, before pairing any. Then go back and add down arrows. Pauli: a box holds at most 2 electrons, and they spin opposite ways, so each electron has its own address.

Plain EnglishElectrons spread out before they share, like people choosing empty bus seats. Oxygen's four p electrons sit as a pair plus two singles, not two pairs.

Magnetism: count the unpaired electrons

ChemParamagnetic: at least one unpaired electron, weakly attracted to a magnet. Diamagnetic: every electron paired, not attracted. Draw the boxes for the last sublevel with Hund's rule and count the single arrows. Odd number of electrons: always paramagnetic. Groups 2, 12 and 18: diamagnetic, because they end in a full $s^2$, $d^{10}$ or $p^6$.

Plain EnglishEach electron is a tiny magnet. Paired electrons spin opposite ways and cancel; a lone electron does not. Most single atoms have a loner somewhere.

Quantum numbers: the electron's address

ChemFour numbers for the last electron. $n$ = the shell (the big number in the term). $\ell$ = the sublevel as a code: s 0, p 1, d 2, f 3. $m_\ell$ = the label of the box the electron landed in, boxes labeled $-\ell$ to $+\ell$. $m_s$ = spin: $+\frac{1}{2}$ for an up arrow, $-\frac{1}{2}$ for a down arrow. No two electrons in an atom share all four (Pauli).

Plain EnglishState, city, street, house. Write the configuration, look at the last term, read the number, convert the letter, draw the boxes, read the last arrow.

Reading one term: number, letter, exponent

ChemA term like $5d^{10}$ has three parts. The number in front is $n$. The letter gives $\ell$ (s 0, p 1, d 2, f 3). The exponent is how many electrons are in that sublevel; it is not a quantum number, but you count to it to find $m_\ell$ and $m_s$. $\ell$ can only run from 0 up to $n - 1$, which is why shell 1 has only s and there is no 2d.

Plain EnglishCover the numbers with your fingers and read the letter. d is 2 whether the term says $3d^2$ or $5d^{10}$. If you ever get $\ell = 10$, you grabbed the exponent.

Part B

Why the trends happen

Coulomb's law: the rubber band between nucleus and electron

ChemThe force of attraction depends on two things: the number of protons (nuclear charge) and the distance. $F \propto \dfrac{q_1 q_2}{d^2}$, where $q_1$ is the nuclear charge, $q_2$ the electron's charge, and $d$ the distance between them. More protons means a stronger pull; more distance means a much weaker pull, because the distance is squared.

Plain EnglishA short rubber band with a big nucleus on one end holds the electron tight. A long band with a small nucleus barely holds it. Every trend on this sheet is one of those two levers moving.

The trend arrows

ChemIonization energy and electronegativity increase up and to the right, toward the top-right corner. Atomic radius (and how reactive a metal is) increases down and to the left, toward francium. Across a row: more protons, same shell, tighter pull, smaller atom. Down a column: a new shell each row, bigger atom, weaker pull on the outside electron.

Plain EnglishRadius points one way, everything else points the other way, because a small atom holds its electrons tightest. Ionization energy peaks at helium; electronegativity peaks at fluorine (the noble gases are left out of that one).

The ionization energy graph: peaks, drops and the climb

ChemThe graph plots the energy needed to remove one electron against atomic number. Peaks are the noble gases: a full outer shell held tight. Drops are the alkali metals right after them: a single electron on a brand-new, farther-out shell, shielded by everything inside. From Li to Ne the line climbs because protons increase while the electrons stay in shell 2.

Plain EnglishNoble gases are hoarders: full and not letting go. Lithium would rather ditch one electron than collect seven. Small dips at Be to B and N to O come from starting the p sublevel and from the first pairing.

Numbers to know. Shell capacities $2n^2$: 2, 8, 18, 32. Sublevels: s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14 (boxes: 1, 3, 5, 7). $\ell$ codes: s 0, p 1, d 2, f 3. Spin: $+\frac{1}{2}$ up, $-\frac{1}{2}$ down. Shell charts: Cd 2, 8, 18, 18, 2; Cs 2, 8, 18, 18, 8, 1; Li 2, 1. Ionization energy (kJ/mol, rounded): Li about 520, F about 1680, He about 2370, Cs about 376 extra depth. Most electronegative element: fluorine; highest ionization energy: helium; biggest atom: francium.

Rule: shells vs sublevels
Shells are numbered (1, 2, 3). Sublevels are lettered (s, p, d, f) and live inside shells. Shell 1 has only 1s. Shell 2 has 2s and 2p. The "octet" of 8 that makes an outer shell stable is 2s + 2p (or 3s + 3p).
Example
"How many electrons does it take to fill the second shell?" $2(2)^2 = 8$. "How many fit in a p sublevel?" 6, which is why the p block is six columns wide. There is no "p shell"; p is a sublevel.

Reading the snake: the three rules

Rule 1: s and p match the row
In row $n$, the s block fills $n$s and the p block fills $n$p. Row 3 is 3s then 3p.
Example
Sodium is in row 3, first column: $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^1$. Chlorine is row 3, fifth p column: $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^5$.
Rule 2: d is one row behind
In row $n$, the d block fills $(n-1)$d. Row 4 is 4s, then 3d, then 4p. Yes, the d block really is "down one."
Example
Titanium is row 4, second d column: $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^2$. Not 4d. Cadmium is row 5, tenth d column: $[\text{Kr}]\,5s^2\,4d^{10}$.
Rule 3: f is two rows behind
In row $n$, the f block fills $(n-2)$f, and it comes right after the s block. Row 6 is 6s, then 4f, then 5d, then 6p.
Example
Mercury is row 6, last d column: $[\text{Xe}]\,6s^2\,4f^{14}\,5d^{10}$. Count: $54 + 2 + 14 + 10 = 80$.

Common mistakes and what to check. Using $2n^2$ as a running total: it is one shell at a time (shell 2 holds 8 by itself; 2p holds 6 of those). Writing 4d in row 4: d is one row behind, so row 4 is 3d. Skipping 3d entirely and jumping from 4s to 4p: the middle of the table is part of the path. Three rules for placing electrons

Aufbau (building up)

Fill the lowest-energy sublevel first. That is the snake order from tab 1: 1s before 2s, 4s before 3d.

Hund's rule

Inside one sublevel, put one electron in each box (all spinning the same way, drawn as up arrows) before pairing any of them. Then go back to the first box and add down arrows. Electrons spread out before they share, like riders choosing empty bus seats.

Pauli exclusion principle

A box holds at most 2 electrons, and the two must spin in opposite directions (one up, one down). No two electrons in the same atom have the same four quantum numbers.

How to draw the last sublevel

Rule
Write the configuration, take the last term, draw its boxes (s 1, p 3, d 5, f 7), and fill with Hund's rule: up arrows left to right first, then down arrows left to right. Full sublevels before it are always completely paired, so you can skip drawing them.
Example: oxygen, $2p^4$
Electrons 1, 2, 3 go up into boxes 1, 2, 3. Electron 4 goes back to box 1 as a down arrow: $[\uparrow\downarrow]\,[\uparrow]\,[\uparrow]$. Two singles left over.
The common mistake
Pairing as you go, $[\uparrow\downarrow]\,[\uparrow\downarrow]\,[\;\;]$, breaks Hund's rule and gives a count of 0 unpaired electrons instead of 2.
Example: titanium, $3d^2$
$[\uparrow]\,[\uparrow]\,[\;\;]\,[\;\;]\,[\;\;]$. Two unpaired. Cadmium, $4d^{10}$: all five boxes $[\uparrow\downarrow]$, nothing unpaired.

Paramagnetic or diamagnetic

TermMeaningAttracted to a magnet?Examples
Paramagneticat least 1 unpaired electronyes, weaklyH, Li, N, O, Ti, Fe, Cs
Diamagneticevery electron pairedno (pushed away very slightly)He, Be, Ne, Ca, Zn, Cd, Hg

Each electron acts like a tiny magnet. A pair in one box spins opposite ways and cancels out; a single electron does not cancel, so the atom responds to a magnet.

  1. Write the configuration (use the snake and the noble gas shortcut).
  2. Draw the boxes for the last sublevel and fill them with Hund's rule.
  3. Count the single arrows. One or more: paramagnetic. Zero: diamagnetic.
Shortcut 1
An odd number of electrons (odd atomic number) means at least one has to be alone, so the atom is always paramagnetic.
Example
Li (3), N (7), Cs (55) and Cu (29) are paramagnetic without drawing anything.
Shortcut 2
Groups 2, 12 and 18 end in a full $s^2$, $d^{10}$ or $p^6$ with everything before them full, so they are diamagnetic.
Example
Be, Mg, Ca; Zn, Cd, Hg; He, Ne, Ar. Also palladium, $[\text{Kr}]\,4d^{10}$.

So are most elements magnetic? As single atoms, yes, most are paramagnetic. Real materials are mostly bonded atoms, and bonding usually pairs the loners, so water, salt, copper metal and plastic are diamagnetic. Fridge magnets stick to iron, cobalt and nickel because of a much stronger effect, ferromagnetism, where many atoms line up their unpaired electrons together. extra depth

Common mistakes and what to check. Pairing electrons before every box has one: fill all the up arrows first. Drawing too few boxes: p is 3 and d is 5 no matter how many electrons are in it. Exam framing. "Is calcium paramagnetic or diamagnetic? Show your reasoning." Configuration $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2$. Last sublevel 4s, one box, two electrons: $[\uparrow\downarrow]$. Zero unpaired electrons, so calcium is diamagnetic. Three lines, and the box drawing is the evidence.

Rule for $n$
$n$ is the big number in front of the last term. $3d^2$ gives $n = 3$; $6s^1$ gives $n = 6$.
Rule for $\ell$: cover the numbers, read the letter
Cover the number in front and the exponent. Convert the letter with the code: s = 0, p = 1, d = 2, f = 3. Say "s-p-d-f, 0-1-2-3."
Examples
$1s^1$: s, so $\ell = 0$. $2p^3$: p, so $\ell = 1$. $3d^2$ and $5d^{10}$: d, so $\ell = 2$ for both. The 10 is a count of electrons; $\ell$ never gets that big.
Allowed values of $\ell$
$\ell$ runs from 0 up to $n - 1$. Shell 1: only 0 (s). Shell 2: 0, 1 (s, p). Shell 3: 0, 1, 2 (s, p, d). That is why there is no 1p and no 2d. For real elements $\ell$ is 0, 1, 2 or 3.

Watch for this one. "If there is no d, $\ell$ is 0" is not the rule. A p term has $\ell = 1$: nitrogen's $2p^3$ gives $\ell = 1$, not 0. Only an s term gives 0.

Step 2: $m_\ell$ and $m_s$ from the boxes

  1. Label the boxes from $-\ell$ up to $+\ell$. s: one box, 0. p: $-1, 0, +1$. d: $-2, -1, 0, +1, +2$. (There are $2\ell + 1$ boxes, which is where 1, 3, 5, 7 come from.)
  2. Fill with Hund's rule: up arrows left to right, then down arrows left to right.
  3. Read the last electron. The label under its box is $m_\ell$. If it is an up arrow, $m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$; a down arrow, $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$.

Part B: Why the Trends Happen

Atomic radius, ionization energy and electronegativity all come from one question: how hard does the nucleus pull on the outermost electron? Two things set that pull, the number of protons and the distance, and Part A told you where the distance comes from (which shell the electron is on). Click through the trends on the table, then read why each one points the way it does.

Play first, read second

Trend arrows on the periodic table

Pick a trend. Darker means a higher value; the shading shows the direction, not exact data.

Coulomb's law: the one idea behind every trend

Formula
$$F \propto \frac{q_1 \, q_2}{d^2}$$

$F$: the force pulling the electron toward the nucleus. $q_1$: the nuclear charge (number of protons). $q_2$: the electron's charge. $d$: the distance between the nucleus and that electron. The symbol $\propto$ means "is proportional to."

Lever 1: distance
Going down a column adds a shell. The outer electron is farther away and shielded by more inner electrons, so the pull is weaker.
Example
Li's outer electron is on shell 2 with 2 inner electrons shielding it. Cs's is on shell 6 behind 54. Cs lets go far more easily.
Lever 2: nuclear charge
Going across a row adds a proton each step while the electrons stay on the same shell, so the pull gets stronger and the atom shrinks.
Example
Li has 3 protons, Ne has 10, both filling shell 2. Ne's electrons are held much tighter.

Effective nuclear charge, $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - S$, where $Z$ is the protons and $S$ the number of inner (shielding) electrons. Na: $11 - 10 \approx 1$. Cl: $17 - 10 \approx 7$. It increases across a row and stays about the same down a column. extra depth

A. Atomic radius

Definition

Distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron. Increases closer to francium (down and to the left).

Why
  • Down a column: each row adds a shell, so the outer electron sits on a bigger ring. Bigger atom.
  • Across a row: more protons pull the same shell in tighter. Smaller atom. Ne is smaller than Li even though it has more electrons.

Biggest: Fr (bottom left). Smallest: He (top right).

B. Ionization energy

Definition

Energy needed to remove 1 electron. Increases closer to helium (up and to the right).

Why
  • It is the opposite of atomic radius: the bigger the atom, the farther and more shielded the outer electron, the easier to pluck off.
  • Full shells hold on. Noble gases have a complete outer shell and do not want to give anything up: the hoarders. That is every peak on the graph.
  • One lonely electron lets go. An alkali metal (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) has a single electron on a brand-new shell. Giving it away leaves a full shell behind, which is far easier than collecting seven more. That is every drop on the graph.

Highest: He. Lowest: Cs and Fr.

C. Electronegativity

Definition

An atom's ability to attract electrons (how tightly it holds its own and pulls on others in a bond). Increases up and to the right. Noble gases technically do not have a value because they rarely bond.

Why
  • Same two levers: small atom, strong effective nuclear charge, strong pull.
  • Atoms one or two electrons short of a full shell get greedy: fluorine needs just one more, so it pulls hardest of all.

Highest: F (3.98 on the Pauling scale). Lowest: Cs and Fr (about 0.7 to 0.8). extra depth Some study guides say EN "increases closer to He"; the direction is right, but the top of the scale is fluorine, because helium and the other noble gases are left out.

Reactivity and nuclear charge

Reactivity

How likely an element is to react. For metals it goes with low ionization energy: the easier the outer electron is to lose, the more reactive. Cs is far more reactive than Li. For nonmetals it goes with high electronegativity: F is the most reactive nonmetal.

Nuclear charge

The number of protons. It rises by one each step across a row, which is lever 2 in Coulomb's law. Sodium has 11, chlorine 17.

Coulomb's law, in the vocab table's words

Distance affects the force of attraction. The number of protons affects the force of attraction. Those two lines are the whole of Part B.

Memory hook. Radius and metal reactivity point to the bottom left (Fr). Everything else points to the top right (He, F). They go opposite ways because a small atom holds its electrons tightly: hard to remove one (high IE) and good at grabbing more (high EN).

The three graph questions, answered

What is the graph about?
First ionization energy (the energy to remove one electron, kJ/mol) for each element, in order of atomic number. Peaks are noble gases; low points are alkali metals; the pattern repeats every row.
Why do the elements suddenly drop?
Each drop is the first element of a new row, an alkali metal (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs). Its outer electron is on a new shell: farther from the nucleus and shielded by all the inner electrons. By Coulomb's law the pull is weak, so the electron is easy to remove.
Why does it rise from Li to Ne?
Each step adds a proton (Li 3, Ne 10) while every electron stays in shell 2, so shielding barely changes. The stronger nucleus pulls the shell in tighter and holds each electron harder. Ne is highest because its shell is full and stable.
The small dips (if asked)
Be to B: boron's new electron starts the 2p sublevel, slightly higher in energy and easier to remove. N to O: oxygen's fourth p electron is the first one paired, and the two repel, so one leaves more easily.

Application: batteries

Why not a cesium battery?
Cs and Li both have one valence electron (group 1), but Cs's sits on shell 6 behind 54 inner electrons. It has a very low ionization energy (about 376 kJ/mol, versus about 520 for Li), so it gives up the electron very easily and reacts violently with water and even moist air. A cesium battery could catch fire or explode.
Lithium or fluorine, ignoring safety?
Lithium. Batteries need an element that gives up electrons easily, and Li's ionization energy is low. Fluorine's is very high (about 1680 kJ/mol), because its seven outer electrons are held by a strong nucleus in a tiny atom; F wants to gain an electron (highest electronegativity), not lose one.

Common mistakes and what to check. Saying atomic radius increases across a row because electrons are added: the protons added at the same time pull harder, so it shrinks. Naming helium as the most electronegative element: noble gases are left out; it is fluorine. Explaining a trend with only "because it's closer to He": name the lever (protons or distance) and the full-shell idea. Mixing up which way the drops go on the graph: the drop happens at the alkali metal after the noble gas, not at the noble gas.

Exam framing. "Explain why fluorine has a higher ionization energy than lithium." Both have electrons in shell 2. Fluorine has 9 protons to lithium's 3, so by Coulomb's law its nucleus pulls the shell in tighter and holds every electron harder (smaller radius, stronger effective nuclear charge). Fluorine is also one electron from a full shell and keeps what it has, while lithium's single outer electron is easy to lose. Two levers plus the full-shell idea is a full-credit answer.

Worked Examples

Eight elements worked start to finish: configuration by the snake, noble gas shorthand, shell chart, boxes, magnetism and quantum numbers. Cover the solution, do it on paper, then compare.

Titanium (Z = 22)

Snake · shorthand · boxes · quantum numbers

Write the full and shorthand configuration, draw the last sublevel, decide the magnetism, and give the four quantum numbers of the last electron.

Rows 1 to 3: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6$ (18, argon). Row 4: $4s^2$, then two into the d block, $3d^2$. Full: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^2$. Shorthand: $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^2$. Check: $18 + 2 + 2 = 22$.

Boxes for $3d^2$: $\underset{-2}{[\uparrow]}\,\underset{-1}{[\uparrow]}\,[\;]\,[\;]\,[\;]$. Two unpaired, so paramagnetic.

Last electron: $n = 3$, $\ell = 2$ (d), $m_\ell = -1$ (second box), $m_s = +\frac{1}{2}$ (up arrow). Shell chart: 2, 8, 10, 2.

Silver (Z = 47)

Snake · counting into the d block · an exception

Write the full configuration of Ag.

Rows 1 to 4 give krypton's 36: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^6$. Row 5: $5s^2$, then count into the d block: Y 1, Zr 2, Nb 3, Mo 4, Tc 5, Ru 6, Rh 7, Pd 8, Ag 9, so $4d^9$.

Pattern answer: $[\text{Kr}]\,5s^2\,4d^9$. Check: $36 + 2 + 9 = 47$.

The real atom holds $[\text{Kr}]\,5s^1\,4d^{10}$: one electron moves from 5s into 4d because a completely full d sublevel (and a half-full $d^5$) is extra stable. Copper and chromium do the same. Give the pattern answer unless your class has covered exceptions.

Cadmium (Z = 48)

Snake · shell chart · magnetism · quantum numbers

Configuration, shell chart, magnetism, quantum numbers of the last electron.

$[\text{Kr}]\,5s^2\,4d^{10}$, the last column of the d block in row 5. Full: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^6\,5s^2\,4d^{10}$. Check: $36 + 2 + 10 = 48$.

Shell chart, grouping by the number in front: shell 1: $1s^2 = 2$; shell 2: $2s^2\,2p^6 = 8$; shell 3: $3s^2\,3p^6\,3d^{10} = 18$; shell 4: $4s^2\,4p^6\,4d^{10} = 18$; shell 5: $5s^2 = 2$. Pattern 2, 8, 18, 18, 2, with 2 valence electrons.

$4d^{10}$ is all pairs, so diamagnetic. Last electron: $n = 4$, $\ell = 2$, $m_\ell = +2$ (10th electron is the last down arrow), $m_s = -\frac{1}{2}$.

Cesium (Z = 55) and lithium (Z = 3)

Shell charts · why one is dangerous

Configurations, shell charts, and the link to the battery question.

Cs: row 6, first column, right after xenon: $[\text{Xe}]\,6s^1$. Full: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^{10}\,4p^6\,5s^2\,4d^{10}\,5p^6\,6s^1$, total 55. Shell chart 2, 8, 18, 18, 8, 1. Last electron $(6, 0, 0, +\frac{1}{2})$. One unpaired: paramagnetic.

Li: $1s^2\,2s^1 = [\text{He}]\,2s^1$. Shell chart 2, 1. Last electron $(2, 0, 0, +\frac{1}{2})$. Paramagnetic.

Both have exactly one valence electron, so they react the same way. Cs's electron is on shell 6 behind 54 inner electrons; Li's is on shell 2 behind 2. Coulomb's law says Cs's is held far more weakly, so Cs loses it very easily: very low ionization energy, violent reaction with water. Lithium gives its electron up easily enough to run a battery but holds it tightly enough to stay safe.

Mercury (Z = 80)

Row 6 with the f block · reading a term

Configuration and the quantum numbers of the last electron. Why is $\ell$ not 10?

Row 6 reads 6s, then 4f, then 5d, then 6p. Mercury is the last d column: $[\text{Xe}]\,6s^2\,4f^{14}\,5d^{10}$. Check: $54 + 2 + 14 + 10 = 80$.

Last term $5d^{10}$: number in front is $n = 5$; the letter d gives $\ell = 2$; the 10 is the number of electrons in the sublevel and is not a quantum number. Boxes all $[\uparrow\downarrow]$: the 10th electron is the last down arrow in the $+2$ box.

$(5, 2, +2, -\frac{1}{2})$. Every sublevel is full, so diamagnetic (group 12, like Zn and Cd).

Nitrogen, hydrogen, oxygen

Quantum numbers for p and s terms · Hund's rule

Four quantum numbers for the last electron of N, H and O.

N: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$. Boxes $\underset{-1}{[\uparrow]}\,\underset{0}{[\uparrow]}\,\underset{+1}{[\uparrow]}$. $(2, 1, +1, +\frac{1}{2})$. Note $\ell = 1$ for p, not 0. Three unpaired: paramagnetic.

H: $1s^1$. One box labeled 0. $(1, 0, 0, +\frac{1}{2})$. Paramagnetic as a lone atom.

O: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4$. Boxes $\underset{-1}{[\uparrow\downarrow]}\,\underset{0}{[\uparrow]}\,\underset{+1}{[\uparrow]}$: the 4th electron goes back and pairs in the first box. $(2, 1, -1, -\frac{1}{2})$. Two unpaired: paramagnetic.

Graph and battery questions

Short answers, sized for the boxes

What is the graph about? The ionization energy of each element (energy to remove one electron) in order of atomic number. Peaks are noble gases, low points are alkali metals.

Why do the elements suddenly drop? The drops are the alkali metals right after each noble gas. Each starts a new shell with one electron that is farther from the nucleus and shielded by the inner electrons, so it is held weakly and is easy to remove.

Why is ionization energy increasing from Li to Ne? Protons increase while the electrons stay in the same shell, so the nucleus pulls them in tighter and it takes more energy to remove one. Ne has a full outer shell of 8 and is very stable.

Why would a cesium battery be dangerous? Cesium's outer electron is much farther from the nucleus and more shielded than lithium's, so it is lost very easily (low ionization energy). Cesium reacts explosively with water and air.

Lithium or fluorine, disregarding safety? Lithium: low ionization energy, so its one outer electron is easy to remove. Fluorine has a high ionization energy because its electrons are held tightly by more protons in a smaller atom, and it would rather gain an electron than give one up.
