The Whole Story on One Page

Everything from this week is two sides of one coin — exactly like finding a consumer's optimal bundle had two sides (what you want vs. what you can afford). For a firm:

💰 Side 1: Money coming in (Revenue)

$$TR = P \times Q \qquad MR = TR'$$

Built from the demand curve. Nothing about costs lives here.

🏭 Side 2: Money going out (Cost)

$$TC = FC + VC(Q) \qquad MC = TC' = VC'$$

Built from production. Nothing about demand lives here.

Putting the two sides together — the one rule that runs everything:

$$\pi = TR - TC \;\; \text{is maximized where} \;\; \boxed{MR = MC}$$

The two sides are independent of each other — demand doesn't know about your costs, and your cost curves don't know about demand. They only meet at the profit-max decision.

🧮

The Calculus Thread

One dictionary translates everything between econ and calculus:

"Marginal" = derivative

$MC = \dfrac{d\,TC}{dQ}$,   $MR = \dfrac{d\,TR}{dQ}$,   $MP = \dfrac{d\,TP}{dL}$. Mathematically it's the tangent-line slope; economists just renamed it.

"Average" = divide by quantity

$ATC = \dfrac{TC}{Q}$,   $AVC = \dfrac{VC}{Q}$,   $AFC = \dfrac{FC}{Q}$,   $AP = \dfrac{TP}{L}$. That's all an average ever is.

"Maximized" = derivative equals zero

$TR$ is maxed where $MR = 0$. Profit $\pi$ is maxed where $\pi' = MR - MC = 0$, i.e. where $MR = MC$.

Integral = undo the derivative

$\displaystyle\int MC \, dQ = VC$ — and the missing "constant of integration" is exactly the fixed cost.

⚡

The Three Things Everyone Forgets

1. From product to revenue: $MR = MP \times P$. Marginal product alone says nothing about money — multiply by the price of the product. (And the wage is your marginal cost of hiring.)

2. From demand to marginal revenue: you must go through total revenue. Plug inverse demand into $TR = P(Q)\cdot Q$ first, then take the derivative. You cannot jump from demand straight to $MR$.

3. Optimal $Q$ vs. actual profit: fixed costs never change where you produce (no $Q$ in them — the derivative kills them), but you can't know whether you're making money until you go back and include $FC$.

1

Production: Total, Marginal & Average Product

The three product curves

Concept

Givens / definitions:

$TP(L)$ = total product — total output as a function of labor $L$

$MP = \dfrac{d\,TP}{dL}$ = marginal product — how much new stuff one more worker makes

$AP = \dfrac{TP}{L}$ = average product — output per worker

The inflection point of $TP$ is where diminishing marginal returns begin — concavity switches, and $MP$ is at its peak. $AP$ keeps rising until $MP$ crosses it.

Too many cooks in the kitchen: before the inflection point, each new worker adds more than the last. After it, each new worker still adds output — just less than the one before. $MP$ is falling but still positive.

Key fact: $MP$ crosses $AP$ exactly at $AP$'s maximum. While $MP > AP$, the average is being pulled up; once $MP < AP$, it's being pulled down.

Reality check: $AP$ is a "thermometer stat" — good for quick number-checking (like $AP \times L = TP$), but it does not drive any business decision. The optimal production point is not where $MP$ crosses $AP$.

The conversion everyone forgets: product → money

Concept

The product curves are only one side of the coin — physical stuff, no dollars. To convert to money:

$$\boxed{MR = MP \times P} \qquad\qquad \text{marginal cost of hiring} = \text{wage } w$$

If one more worker makes $MP$ extra units, and each sells for price $P$, that worker brings in $MP \times P$ of extra revenue. You keep hiring as long as $MP \times P > w$.

Common trap: "marginal product times price" is revenue, not profit. To talk about profit you need the cost side — and there are no cost curves on a product graph.

Worked example
Given

The next worker's marginal product: $MP = 30$ units/day

Product price: $P = \$4$/unit

Daily wage: $w = \$100$/day

Step 1 — convert product to revenue: $MR = MP \times P = 30 \times 4 = \$120$/day.

Step 2 — compare to marginal cost of hiring: $w = \$100$/day.

Step 3 — decide: $\$120 > \$100$, so hire. Keep hiring until $MP \times P$ falls to the wage.

2

Total Revenue Is Always the Box

TR = P × Q, every single time

Concept

Total revenue is always price times quantity — there is no other formula. On a demand curve (price vs. quantity), any point $(Q, P)$ makes a rectangle with the axes, and that rectangle's area is total revenue. This works no matter how curvy the demand curve gets.

Pick any point on demand; the shaded box has area $P \times Q = TR$.

Demand curve vs. inverse demand curve

Concept

Economists write the same line two ways, and the naming is backwards from what you'd expect:

Demand curve — what econ usually hands you

$$Q(P) = -\tfrac{P}{2} + 50$$

Quantity as a function of price: "how much people want at each price." Annoyingly, this is $x$ as a function of $y$ on the usual graph.

Inverse demand curve — what you actually work with

$$P(Q) = 100 - 2Q$$

Price as a function of quantity — the familiar $y = mx + b$ from middle school. Same line, solved the other way.

Notation trap: the $^{-1}$ in $f^{-1}(x)$ means inverse function, not "to the power $-1$." Worst notation in math. To invert: swap the variables, then solve for the new $y$.

Worked example
Given

Demand curve: $Q(P) = -\dfrac{P}{2} + 50$

Step 1 — set it up as $Q = -\dfrac{P}{2} + 50$ and solve for $P$.

Step 2 — subtract 50: $Q - 50 = -\dfrac{P}{2}$

Step 3 — multiply by $-2$: $P = -2Q + 100$

Result: inverse demand $P(Q) = 100 - 2Q$ — exactly the line we started with, written the useful way.

Getting MR from demand — always through TR

Concept

There will always come a moment where you have a demand curve and need marginal revenue. The route is always the same three steps:

1

Get inverse demand $P(Q)$ — invert if they gave you $Q(P)$.

2

Build total revenue: $TR = P(Q) \cdot Q$ — plug inverse demand in for the price.

3

Differentiate: $MR = \dfrac{d\,TR}{dQ}$.

Worked example
Given

Inverse demand: $P(Q) = 100 - 2Q$

Step 1: $TR = P \cdot Q = (100 - 2Q)\,Q = 100Q - 2Q^2$

Step 2: $MR = TR' = 100 - 4Q$

Notice: demand had slope $-2$; $MR$ has slope $-4$.

Linear shortcut (and its limits): if demand is linear, $MR$ is the same line with twice the slope (same intercept). Great for speed and for checking your work — but it dies the moment demand isn't linear. A demand curve with a $Q^2$ term gives a $Q^3$ term in $TR$, and the derivative triples it instead of doubling. When in doubt, just do the three steps.

Where TR peaks — a preview of elasticity preview — full treatment next

Concept

$TR$ is a downward-opening parabola (for linear demand), so it has a peak — and the peak is exactly where its tangent slope is zero: $MR = 0$. That point on the demand curve has a special name: the unit elastic point.

$TR$ peaks exactly where $MR = 0$. Above that point demand is elastic; below it, inelastic.
The formula (for next session)

$$E = \frac{dQ}{dP} \cdot \frac{P}{Q} \qquad |E| = 1 \text{ at the } TR \text{ peak}, \quad |E| > 1 \text{ above (elastic)}, \quad |E| < 1 \text{ below (inelastic)}$$

Don't worry about mastering this yet — we hammer it home in the elasticity unit. Just anchor the picture: revenue max ↔ $MR=0$ ↔ unit elastic.

The Cost Side: All Four Curves

Two buckets: fixed and variable

Concept
Definitions

Fixed cost $FC$: doesn't depend on $Q$ — capital goods, the building, farm equipment, the pizza oven.

Variable cost $VC(Q)$: grows with output — grain if you bake bread, gasoline if you run a gas station.

$$TC(Q) = FC + VC(Q)$$

The calculus punchline: $FC$ has no $Q$ in it, so when you differentiate it dies:

$$MC = \frac{d\,TC}{dQ} = \frac{d\,VC}{dQ}$$

This is why you can find marginal cost from either total cost or variable cost — they differ by a constant, and derivatives don't see constants. (An economist calling a derivative "marginal cost" is the same person a mathematician would call "tangent slope.")

Building all four curves

Concept

Marginal cost

$$MC = TC' = VC'$$

The most important curve — dips while you scale, then rises (diminishing returns). Its exact shape depends on the industry.

Average fixed cost

$$AFC = \frac{FC}{Q}$$

Just a number over $Q$ — like $f(x) = \tfrac{1{,}000{,}000}{x}$. Starts huge, only falls. Asymptotes at both axes; never zero, never infinite.

Average variable cost

$$AVC = \frac{VC}{Q}$$

Take only the part of $TC$ that depends on $Q$, divide by $Q$. U-shaped for the same reason $MC$ eventually rises.

Average total cost

$$ATC = \frac{TC}{Q} = AFC + AVC$$

Vertically add the two average curves. Hugs $AFC$ early (fixed costs dominate), hugs $AVC$ late (fixed costs spread thin).

$ATC$ is built by stacking $AFC$ on top of $AVC$. $MC$ cuts through both average curves exactly at their minimums.

In practice: you'll mostly work with $MC$ and $ATC$. $AVC$ exists mainly so you can still recover $MC$ when they don't hand you total cost; $AFC$ is there so the pieces add up.

Why MC crosses ATC at the minimum — the GPA metaphor

Concept

$ATC$ is your running average; $MC$ is the stream of numbers being averaged. Think of it as: $MC$ is the grade you just got on a test, $ATC$ is your GPA.

  • Score below your GPA ($MC < ATC$) → your GPA falls.
  • Score above your GPA ($MC > ATC$) → your GPA rises.
  • So the GPA bottoms out exactly where the test grade equals the GPA: $MC = ATC$ at $ATC$'s minimum.

What that minimum point means (and doesn't mean): it's the lowest possible average cost per unit — a neat mathematical fact, but a business owner has no reason to produce there on purpose.

Except in fairy-tale land: in perfect competition, the market magically pushes firms to produce exactly there, because that point ends up coinciding with $MR = MC$. That's why economists care about it so much.

Going backwards: the integral

Concept

An integral undoes a derivative — but it only recovers the shape, not where the curve is anchored. In math, that's the "$+\,C$" constant of integration. In econ, that constant is a real object:

$$\int MC \; dQ = VC(Q), \qquad TC(Q) = VC(Q) + \underbrace{FC}_{\text{the "}+C\text{" — must be told separately}}$$

Integrating $MC$ rebuilds variable cost; the fixed cost is the anchor point the integral can't see.

Putting It Together: Profit Maximization

Deriving the rule (it's three lines)

Concept
0

The plan. Profit is revenue minus cost. To maximize anything, take its derivative and set it to zero. That's the whole derivation.

1

Write profit. $\pi(Q) = TR(Q) - TC(Q)$  (economists use $\pi$ for profit — not 3.14159).

2

Differentiate term by term. $\pi' = TR' - TC' = MR - MC$. (You could also combine first and then differentiate — same answer; term-by-term is easier.)

3

Set to zero. $\pi' = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; MR - MC = 0$.

$$\boxed{\;MR = MC \;\Rightarrow\; \text{solve for } Q^*, \text{ then plug } Q^* \text{ into demand to get } P^*\;}$$

Intuition: $MR$ starts high, $MC$ starts low. Every unit where $MR > MC$ adds profit, so keep going until they intersect. Past that point each extra unit costs more than it brings in. (And note which variable you're choosing: the firm sets quantity; the demand curve then tells you the price — Joe Schmoe's lemonade stand doesn't get to declare $10 a cup.)

The fixed-cost subtlety

Concept

Since $MC = VC'$, fixed costs never appear in the $MR = MC$ equation. Two consequences that sound contradictory but aren't:

Fixed costs do NOT decide how much you produce

No $Q$ in $FC$ → the derivative kills it → $Q^*$ comes out the same whether your rent is $\$10$ or $\$10$ million. You can find the optimum without ever knowing $FC$.

But you can't know your PROFIT without them

"Great, we're at the optimum — are we actually making money?" is unanswerable until you go back and subtract $FC$: $\pi = TR - VC - FC$.

Day-to-day vs. bottom line

On a daily decision level a business cares about doing the optimal thing; whether the whole operation is profitable is a separate (fixed-cost-dependent) question.

Seeing it on a graph: the monopoly profit box

Concept

A monopolist faces the whole market demand curve, so its $MR$ is the twice-as-steep line. The recipe on the graph:

Find $MR = MC$ → drop down for $Q^*$ → go up to the demand curve for $P^*$ → profit is the box between $P^*$ and $ATC$.

$$\pi = (P^* - ATC(Q^*)) \times Q^*$$ Height = profit per unit; width = units sold. If $ATC$ sits above $P^*$ at $Q^*$, the "profit box" is negative — the firm is losing money even at its best $Q$.

Trap: the price comes from the demand curve at $Q^*$, never from the $MR = MC$ intersection point itself. The intersection tells you the quantity; demand tells you what price that quantity fetches.

1

Reading the product curves.

Given

A total product curve $TP(L)$ that rises steeply at first, has an inflection point at $L = 6$, and flattens out near $L = 12$. ($L$ = number of workers.)

(a) At what $L$ does diminishing marginal returns begin, and what does that mean for the concavity of $TP$?

(b) At what $L$ is marginal product $MP$ maximized?

(c) True or false: the intersection of $MP$ and $AP$ is the optimal production point.

(d) Your boss asks "how much stuff did we make last month, per worker?" Which curve answers that — and is it a decision-making curve or a thermometer stat?

Production curves

(a) At $L = 6$, the inflection point. Concavity switches from concave-up (each worker adds more than the last) to concave-down (each worker adds less than the last). Output still grows — just more slowly per worker.

(b) Also $L = 6$. $MP$ is the slope of $TP$, and the slope is steepest exactly at the inflection point.

(c) False. That intersection marks $AP$'s maximum — a mathematical fact, not a decision. The profit-relevant point is where $MR = MC$, and there are no costs or revenues anywhere on this graph.

(d) Average product $AP = TP/L$. It's a thermometer stat — great for quick checks (multiply back up to get totals), not used to decide anything.

2

Should the bakery hire?

Given

Marginal product of the next baker: $MP = 45$ loaves/day

Price of a loaf: $P = \$3$

Daily wage: $w = \$150$/day

(a) What is the marginal revenue from hiring this baker?

(b) What is the marginal cost of hiring them?

(c) Hire or don't hire? And at what wage would you be exactly indifferent?

MR = MP × P

(a) Convert product to money: $MR = MP \times P = 45 \times 3 = \$135$/day.

(b) The wage: $w = \$150$/day.

(c) Don't hire: $\$135 < \$150$ — this worker costs more than they bring in. Indifference at $w = \$135$, i.e. exactly $MP \times P$. (Common trap: $MP \times P$ is revenue, not profit — the comparison against the wage is what brings cost in.)

3

From demand curve to marginal revenue — the full pipeline.

Given

Demand curve (as econ hands it to you): $Q(P) = 30 - \dfrac{P}{4}$

(a) Find the inverse demand curve $P(Q)$.

(b) Find total revenue $TR(Q)$.

(c) Find marginal revenue $MR(Q)$.

(d) Check your answer to (c) with the linear-demand shortcut.

Inverse demand → TR → MR

(a) Solve for $P$:   $Q = 30 - \dfrac{P}{4} \;\Rightarrow\; \dfrac{P}{4} = 30 - Q \;\Rightarrow\; P(Q) = 120 - 4Q$

(b) $TR = P \cdot Q = (120 - 4Q)\,Q = 120Q - 4Q^2$

(c) $MR = TR' = 120 - 8Q$

(d) Inverse demand is linear with intercept $120$ and slope $-4$; the shortcut says $MR$ = same intercept, twice the slope = $120 - 8Q$. ✓ Matches.

4

Where does revenue peak? elasticity preview

Given

The same market as the previous problem: inverse demand $P(Q) = 120 - 4Q$, so $MR = 120 - 8Q$.

(a) At what quantity is total revenue maximized? What price goes with it, and what is the max $TR$?

(b) What is that point on the demand curve called?

(c) At $Q = 5$, is the firm on the elastic or inelastic part of demand? If it wants more revenue from there, should it move toward higher or lower $Q$?

TR max ↔ MR = 0

(a) Maximize by setting the derivative of $TR$ (which is $MR$) to zero: $120 - 8Q = 0 \Rightarrow Q = 15$. Price: $P = 120 - 4(15) = \$60$. Max revenue: $TR = 60 \times 15 = \$900$.

(b) The unit elastic point ($|E| = 1$).

(c) $Q = 5 < 15$, so we're high on the demand curve — the elastic region. Moving toward the peak means increasing $Q$ (lowering price): in elastic territory, price cuts raise revenue.

5

Dissect the total cost function.

Given

$TC(Q) = 20 + 5Q + Q^2$

Find all six: (a) $FC$   (b) $VC(Q)$   (c) $MC(Q)$   (d) $AFC(Q)$   (e) $AVC(Q)$   (f) $ATC(Q)$ — and (g) verify that $ATC = AFC + AVC$.

Cost curve anatomy

(a) $FC = 20$ — the part with no $Q$.

(b) $VC = 5Q + Q^2$ — everything that depends on $Q$.

(c) $MC = TC' = 5 + 2Q$. (Note the $20$ died in the derivative — $MC = VC'$ gives the same thing.)

(d) $AFC = \dfrac{20}{Q}$

(e) $AVC = \dfrac{5Q + Q^2}{Q} = 5 + Q$

(f) $ATC = \dfrac{TC}{Q} = \dfrac{20}{Q} + 5 + Q$

(g) $AFC + AVC = \dfrac{20}{Q} + (5 + Q) = ATC$ ✓

6

The trickster: no total cost given.

Given

Average variable cost: $AVC(Q) = 2Q^2$

The firm can sell all it wants at the market price $P = \$96$ (so $MR = 96$).

(a) Find $VC(Q)$ and then $MC(Q)$.

(b) Find the profit-maximizing quantity $Q^*$.

(c) Find total revenue and variable cost at $Q^*$.

(d) What is total cost at $Q^*$?

Recovering MC from AVC

(a) Careful — multiply back up before differentiating: $VC = AVC \times Q = 2Q^2 \cdot Q = 2Q^3$. Then $MC = VC' = 6Q^2$.

(b) Set $MC = MR$:   $6Q^2 = 96 \Rightarrow Q^2 = 16 \Rightarrow Q^* = 4$.

(c) $TR = P \cdot Q^* = 96 \times 4 = \$384$.   $VC(4) = 2(4)^3 = \$128$.

(d) Spoiler: we can't know. $TC = VC + FC$ and nobody told us the fixed cost. We found the optimal quantity without it — that's the whole point — but the actual cost total (and profit) needs $FC$.

Watch the trap in (a): differentiating $AVC$ directly gives $4Q$ — wrong curve. $MC$ is the derivative of variable cost, not of average variable cost. $AVC$ exists in problems precisely to test whether you'll multiply by $Q$ first.

7

Full monopoly profit maximization.

Given

Inverse demand: $P(Q) = 80 - Q$

Total cost: $TC(Q) = Q^2 + 20Q + 50$

(a) Find $MR(Q)$ and $MC(Q)$.

(b) Find $Q^*$ and $P^*$.

(c) Find profit at the optimum.

(d) Verify (c) using the profit-box formula $\pi = (P^* - ATC(Q^*)) \times Q^*$.

MR = MC, then back to demand

(a) $TR = (80 - Q)Q = 80Q - Q^2$, so $MR = 80 - 2Q$ (twice the slope ✓). $MC = TC' = 2Q + 20$.

(b) $MR = MC$:   $80 - 2Q = 2Q + 20 \Rightarrow 60 = 4Q \Rightarrow Q^* = 15$. Plug into demand: $P^* = 80 - 15 = \$65$.

(c) $TR = 65 \times 15 = \$975$.   $TC = 15^2 + 20(15) + 50 = 225 + 300 + 50 = \$575$.   $\pi = 975 - 575 = \$400$.

(d) $ATC(15) = \dfrac{575}{15} = \dfrac{115}{3} \approx 38.33$. Box: $\left(65 - \dfrac{115}{3}\right) \times 15 = \dfrac{80}{3} \times 15 = \$400$ ✓

Reading the monopoly graph.

Given

The monopoly diagram from the Profit Max tab: demand, $MR$, $MC$, and $ATC$ all drawn on one graph.

(a) Describe the three-step recipe for finding $Q^*$, $P^*$, and the profit box on that graph.

(b) A classmate says "the firm should produce where $MC$ crosses $ATC$, because that's the cheapest average cost." What two things do you tell them?

(c) In what kind of market does the $MC$-crosses-$ATC$ point actually coincide with the profit-max point?

Graph literacy

(a) ① Find where $MR$ crosses $MC$ — drop straight down to read $Q^*$. ② Go up from $Q^*$ to the demand curve to read $P^*$ (not the intersection's height!). ③ Profit is the box between $P^*$ and $ATC(Q^*)$, with width $Q^*$.

(c) Perfect competition ("fairy-tale land"): there, $MR = P$ and the market drives firms to the bottom of $ATC$, so the special point and the profit-max point magically coincide.

Rebuilding cost from marginal cost (going backwards).

Given

Marginal cost: $MC(Q) = 6Q + 4$

Fixed cost: $FC = 30$

(a) Recover $VC(Q)$. (Hint: undo the derivative — what function has $6Q + 4$ as its derivative?)

(b) Write down $TC(Q)$.

(c) In the language of calculus, what role does the $30$ play — and why couldn't the integral find it on its own?

∫ MC = VC

(a) Reverse the power rule term by term: $6Q$ came from $3Q^2$, and $4$ came from $4Q$. So $VC(Q) = 3Q^2 + 4Q$. (Check by differentiating: $(3Q^2 + 4Q)' = 6Q + 4$ ✓)

(b) $TC(Q) = VC + FC = 3Q^2 + 4Q + 30$

(c) It's the constant of integration — the "$+\,C$". The integral recovers the exact shape of the cost curve but not where it's anchored vertically, because every constant has derivative zero. Econ's version of $C$ is the fixed cost: it has to be told to you separately.

10

The GPA logic.

Given

At the firm's current output, $MC = \$12$ per unit and $ATC = \$18$ per unit. The firm then produces one more unit.

(a) Does $ATC$ rise or fall as a result? Explain with the test-grade/GPA metaphor.

(b) As output keeps growing (with $MC$ rising), what is true at the exact moment $ATC$ stops falling?

(c) Does $MC$ also cross $AVC$ at $AVC$'s minimum? Why would the same logic apply?

MC vs the averages

(a) $ATC$ falls. The new unit "scored" $\$12$ against a running average of $\$18$ — like getting a grade below your GPA: the average gets dragged down.

(b) $MC = ATC$. The moment the incoming grade equals the GPA is exactly the bottom of the average curve — from then on, $MC > ATC$ pulls the average back up. That's why $MC$ always crosses $ATC$ at its minimum.

(c) Yes. $AVC$ is also a running average of the same marginal costs (just without fixed cost mixed in), so the identical grade-vs-GPA argument applies: $MC$ crosses $AVC$ at $AVC$'s minimum too.

11

Optimal Q without fixed costs — then the reveal.

Given

Variable cost: $VC(Q) = \dfrac{1}{2}Q^2 + 10Q$

Market price (perfect competition, so $MR = P$): $P = \$30$

Fixed cost: not given (yet)

(a) Find $Q^*$. Why is the missing fixed cost not a problem here?

(b) Find total revenue and variable cost at $Q^*$.

(c) Now the reveal: $FC = 250$. Find profit. Was the firm's quantity decision wrong?

The fixed-cost subtlety

(a) $MC = VC' = Q + 10$. Set $MC = MR$: $Q + 10 = 30 \Rightarrow Q^* = 20$. Fixed cost has no $Q$ in it, so it vanishes in the derivative — it literally cannot affect where $MR = MC$.

(b) $TR = 30 \times 20 = \$600$.   $VC(20) = \tfrac{1}{2}(400) + 200 = \$400$.

(c) $\pi = TR - VC - FC = 600 - 400 - 250 = -\$50$. The firm is losing money — but the quantity decision was still right: $Q = 20$ loses less than any other quantity would. Optimal $Q$ and "are we profitable?" are separate questions, and only the second needs $FC$.

12

The box, quickly.

Given

A firm sells at the point $(Q, P) = (18, \$22)$ on its demand curve. Separately, its average total cost at $Q = 18$ is $ATC = \$17$.

(a) Total revenue — and what does it look like on the demand graph?

(b) Total cost at this output. (Which quick-check trick from the session does this use?)

(c) Profit, two ways: as $TR - TC$, and as a box.

Boxes everywhere

(a) $TR = P \times Q = 22 \times 18 = \$396$ — the rectangle under the point $(18, 22)$, corners at the axes.

(b) $TC = ATC \times Q = 17 \times 18 = \$306$. That's the "average times how-many gets you back the total" quick-check — the main practical use of average curves.

(c) $\pi = 396 - 306 = \$90$; equivalently the profit box $(P - ATC) \times Q = (22 - 17) \times 18 = 5 \times 18 = \$90$ ✓

Bonus: Nash Equilibrium vs. Pareto Efficiency

Why this is here: this came up when we talked about firms undercutting each other, and it's the doorway to the game theory / Cournot material we're building toward. Zero pressure to master it now — just get the two ideas to sit in different mental boxes.

Two different questions about the same game

Concept

Nash equilibrium — "where do players end up?"

A stable point: each player is playing their best response to the other, so nobody has any incentive to change on their own. Drop the players there and they stay. It says nothing about whether the outcome is good.

Pareto efficiency — "which outcomes waste nothing?"

An outcome is Pareto efficient if you can't make anyone better off without making someone else worse off. It's about non-wastefulness, not stability — and it doesn't care whether players would actually stay there.

The punchline: these two points can coincide, but usually don't. The classic story: two firms would both be better off holding prices high, but each one's best response is to undercut — so they end up at a stable-but-worse outcome. Stable ≠ good.

Bonus problem: the pricing game

Practice
Given

Two rival firms each choose, at the same time, to price High or Low. Monthly profits (Firm A, Firm B) in thousands:

B prices HighB prices Low
A prices High(500, 500)(200, 700)
A prices Low(700, 200)(300, 300)

(a) If Firm B prices High, what is Firm A's best response? If B prices Low, what is A's best response?

(b) Find the Nash equilibrium of this game.

(c) Is there an outcome that both firms prefer to the Nash equilibrium? What is that property called?

(d) If the two firms simply agreed to both price High, why is that agreement unstable? (What is each firm tempted to do?)

Nash vs Pareto

(a) If B prices High: A comparing $500$ (High) vs $700$ (Low) → best response Low. If B prices Low: A comparing $200$ (High) vs $300$ (Low) → best response Low. (Pricing Low is best for A no matter what B does — a dominant strategy.)

(b) By symmetry B also always prefers Low. Both playing best responses to each other → Nash equilibrium is (Low, Low) with payoffs (300, 300). Nobody can gain by switching alone: deviating to High while the rival stays Low drops you from $300$ to $200$.

(c) Yes — (High, High) = (500, 500) makes both firms better off than $(300,300)$. An outcome that improves on another for everyone is a Pareto improvement; the Nash equilibrium here is not Pareto efficient.

(d) From (High, High), each firm is tempted to secretly undercut: unilaterally switching to Low jumps your profit from $500$ to $700$. Both firms face that temptation, both cave, and the game slides back to $(300, 300)$. That's why collusion agreements keep collapsing without enforcement — and it's the tick-for-tat world that Cournot equilibrium (coming later) makes rigorous.
