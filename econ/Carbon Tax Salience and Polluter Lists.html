What this worksheet covers

Two empirical papers, one province. British Columbia ran two unusual policy experiments: in 1990 it started publishing a public list of polluters, and in 2008 it launched North America's first broad carbon tax. Both papers ask the same underlying question: does the way a policy reaches people change behavior beyond its dollar size? Both answer yes.

R&SRivers & Schaufele, “Salience of Carbon Taxes in the Gasoline Market” (working paper draft, June 10, 2013; published later in JEEM).

FLLFoulon, Lanoie & Laplante (2002), “Incentives for Pollution Control: Regulation or Information?” JEEM 44: 169–187.

Rivers & Schaufele in one line: a 5¢/litre increase in BC's carbon tax cut short-run gasoline demand by about 12.5%, while a 5¢ increase in the market price cut it by only 1.8%: the tax was about 7 times more salient. R&S p. 2

Foulon, Lanoie & Laplante in one line: for 15 BC pulp and paper plants, appearing on the public out of compliance list cut emissions about as much as a court fine did, and it worked on both pollutants while fines only moved one. Tighter standards (1990) mattered most. FLL p. 185

2

The thread that ties them together

Standard theory says only the size of an incentive matters. A 5¢ tax and a 5¢ oil-price shock are the same 5¢ at the pump. A \$25,000 fine is a \$25,000 fine whether or not anyone hears about it. Both papers find the channel matters too:

R&STax vs. price

Same cents, different response. The authors' story: people care about the public good and resent free-riders, so a tax that makes everyone pay changes preferences, not just prices. The tax enters utility directly.

FLLInformation vs. fines

Same regulator, same data, different channel. Publishing the list brings in communities, investors and customers; a fine runs through a slow, uncertain court process. The list got a faster response.

Notes on the numbers (read before a session)

  • Draft vs. published. The Rivers & Schaufele file is the June 2013 working-paper draft. The published journal version may report slightly different estimates. Everything here uses the draft's numbers.
  • The 12.5%. The BC carbon-tax coefficient is $-0.0247$ per cent/litre. The quick reading gives $5 \times 2.47\% = 12.35\%$; the paper's own footnote-10 formula gives $1 - e^{-5 \times 0.0247} = 11.6\%$. The paper rounds to 12.5%. Either “about 12%” answer is fine; the ratio of about 7 is what matters.
  • “5¢ is about \$25/tonne.” BC's own conversion (Table 1) puts \$25/t at 5.56¢/L, so 5¢ is closer to \$22.50/t. The paper uses the round number.
  • A likely typo in Foulon et al. Footnote b under Table IV reads “We can reject” that the fine and list effects are equal, but the $t$-statistics are 0.364 and 0.466 and the text (p. 184) says the difference is not significant. Read it as “cannot.”
  • A second draft inconsistency (R&S). For IV model (3), one sentence on p. 15 says the Wu-Hausman null “must be rejected,” while another on the same page and Table 3 (0.91, p = 0.99) say it is not. Go with the table; the Sargan rejection in model (3) is real.
  • Sign convention in Foulon et al. Compliance rate $=\frac{\text{actual} - \text{allowable}}{\text{allowable}}$, so lower is better and a negative coefficient means improvement.

2

What “tax salience” means

Tax salience is the hypothesis that a price change caused by a tax produces a different demand response than an equal-sized price change caused by the market. R&S p. 1 A tax is more salient if it moves demand more.

Standard commodity-tax prediction. The consumer only sees the pump price $q = p + t$, so

$$\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial p} = \frac{\partial y^*}{\partial t}.$$

Rivers & Schaufele reject this equality in every monthly model. R&S pp. 13, 20

  • Why the carbon tax is a clean test. Earlier salience work used sales taxes, tolls and gasoline excise taxes (Chetty, Looney & Kroft 2009; Finkelstein 2009; Li, Linn & Muehlegger 2012, who find a ratio of 8.1). Excise revenue often builds roads, which lowers the cost of driving. A carbon tax's only purpose is to cut fossil-fuel use, so it isolates the environmental signal. R&S pp. 1–2
  • Why inattention is a weak story here. Gasoline prices are posted tax-inclusive on the sign and the pump, so there is no hidden tax to miscalculate at the register. R&S p. 3
  • Claim: first empirical study of the salience of an environmental tax, and first causal estimate of a North American carbon tax's effect on household gasoline demand. R&S pp. 1–2

Table 1 (R&S p. 34): the carbon tax rises while income taxes fall. That inverse link is what makes income-tax revenue an instrument later.

Feature 1

Gradual

\$10/t in 2008, up \$5 every July 1 to \$30/t in 2012. Phasing in was meant to reduce adjustment costs.

Feature 2

Revenue-neutral

Every dollar returned: lower personal income tax on the bottom two brackets, cuts to corporate and small-business rates, a low-income quarterly rebate (about \$300/yr for a family of four) and, from 2011, up to \$200 for northern and rural homeowners.

Why revenue neutrality mattered R&S p. 5: (1) public acceptability, sold as a “tax shift” not a tax increase; (2) cutting distortionary income taxes lowers the marginal cost of public funds (the double-dividend idea, Goulder 1995); (3) a commitment device: once income taxes were cut, the government needed the carbon revenue (\$741 million in 2011), so delaying the scheduled increases was costly; (4) an identification strategy, covered in tab 2.

Quebec's much smaller carbon tax (about \$3/t since October 2007, meant to raise \$100M a year for green projects) is in the data but not emphasized. R&S p. 5

The estimating equation

Key. $y_{it}$ = per-capita (age 15+) litres of gasoline in province $i$, month $t$  •  $p_{it}$ = real gasoline price excluding the carbon tax (includes excise taxes)  •  $\tau_{it}$ = real carbon tax, ¢/L  •  $X_{it}$ = controls (after-tax income, small-car share)  •  $\delta_i$ = province fixed effect  •  $\gamma_t$ = month-year (or year) fixed effect.

$$\ln y_{it} = \alpha\, p_{it} + \beta\, \tau_{it} + X_{it}\theta + \delta_i + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_{it} \qquad \text{(eq. 1, log-linear)}$$

Reading a coefficient. In a log-linear model, $100\beta$ is roughly the percent change in demand for a 1¢ increase (a semi-elasticity). Exactly: $\%\Delta y = 100\,(e^{\beta\Delta}-1)$. R&S p. 8, fn. 10

Why interact with BC. The preferred model uses $\alpha\,(p_{it}\times BC_i)$ and $\beta\,(\tau_{it}\times BC_i)$. BC drivers are more price-sensitive than the national average; without the interaction the tax coefficient soaks up BC's extra price sensitivity (national tax coefficient $-0.0457$ falls to $-0.0247$ once BC gets its own price slope). R&S pp. 8, 13

Data: Statistics Canada monthly litres by province, Kent Marketing prices, all deflated by provincial CPI; Jan 1990 to Dec 2011; 2,580 province-month observations (216 province-years in annual models). Newey-West (HAC) standard errors. R&S pp. 6, 12

Figure 1's eyeball evidence: when the tax started in July 2008, pump prices fell sharply in both BC and Ontario. Ontario demand rose, as a demand curve predicts. BC demand kept falling. The tax looked like a demand shifter, not just a price. R&S p. 7

Why would a tax cent bite harder?

Try it

Pick a mechanism and watch what a carbon tax does to the demand curve compared with an equal market-price rise.

Building on Chetty (2009), let the consumer split wealth $Z$ between a clean numeraire $x$ and gasoline $y$ with pump price $q = p + t$, and let the tax enter utility directly: R&S p. 20

$$u = x + v(y, t), \qquad \frac{dy^*}{dt} = \underbrace{\left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right|_{q}}_{\text{direct (preference) effect}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial y}{\partial q}\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}}_{\text{price effect}}$$

If $\left.\partial y/\partial t\right|_q = 0$ only the price effect is left and the standard prediction $\partial y/\partial p = \partial y/\partial t$ holds. The authors propose two non-standard, other-regarding preferences that make the direct effect nonzero:

Resentment of free-ridership: $\left.\partial y/\partial t\right|_q < 0$

An environmentally minded driver who cuts back on her own just makes gas cheaper and roads emptier for others, who drive more (a form of leakage). Resenting that, she contributes less. A carbon tax makes everyone pay for their emissions, removes the free-riding, and frees her to cut back. The tax works as a focal point or coordinating device. Predicts a tax more salient than price. R&S pp. 20–21

Relief of guilt: $\left.\partial y/\partial t\right|_q > 0$

She used to drive less out of guilt; once a tax lets her “pay the full environmental cost,” she feels licensed to drive more. Predicts a tax less salient than price. Which effect dominates is an empirical question, and BC's data say resentment. R&S pp. 21–22

The rivals: inattention and cognitive cost

Chetty et al., Finkelstein and Goldin & Homonoff explain salience by irrationality: people don't notice or don't compute the tax. Rivers & Schaufele instead point to non-standard preferences (Congdon, Kling & Mullainathan): people are “other-regarding.” R&S pp. 19–20

Link to Kotchen. Footnote 15 separates this from warm glow and impure public goods: in those models the driver may get a warm glow from driving less, but a tax still only works through prices. Here the tax itself is an argument of utility. R&S p. 21, fn. 15

Common exam trap: “salient” here does not mean the tax is more visible at the pump. The posted price already includes every tax. The paper's explanation is about preferences (the tax changes what people want to do), not about attention. If you write “drivers noticed the tax more,” add that the authors argue for resentment of free-riding instead.

Exam framing: “What is tax salience and what did Rivers & Schaufele find?” Full credit: (1) define salience as different responses to tax-driven vs. market-driven price changes; (2) state standard theory's $\partial y/\partial p = \partial y/\partial t$; (3) give the numbers: 5¢ of tax cuts demand about 12.5%, 5¢ of price about 1.8%, ratio about 7; (4) name the mechanism (resentment of free-ridership, tax enters utility directly).

Rivers & Schaufele: is it causal?

A 7× ratio is only interesting if the carbon tax caused the drop. The authors stack three defenses: fixed effects, instrumental variables, and a long list of robustness checks.

1

Defense 1: panel fixed effects

Ten provinces observed monthly for 22 years. R&S p. 8

  • Province fixed effects $\delta_i$ absorb anything about a province that doesn't change over time: geography, distances, urban density, driving culture.
  • Month-year fixed effects $\gamma_t$ absorb anything that hits every province in the same month: world oil prices, national recessions, federal policy, trade policy.
  • What's left to worry about: things that vary by province and month and are correlated with BC's tax (a BC-only recession, BC bike lanes). That's what IV and the robustness checks are for.

2

Defense 2: instrumental variables

Why they think plain least squares is already fine R&S p. 9: the gasoline market is competitive (wholesale price set in the West Coast market, thin retail margins); excise taxes pass through fully and immediately (Marion & Muehlegger 2011); the carbon tax came fast, was a surprise, covered all fuels and had one goal, so it is unlikely to be tangled up with other outcomes.

What could still go wrong: omitted province-time factors. New bike lanes could cut car ownership and congestion at the same moment the tax began (bias in either direction); the 2008 recession may have hit provinces differently (Davis & Kilian). R&S p. 9

An instrument $Z$ moves the suspect variable $X$ but has no path to demand $Y$ except through $X$.

Three requirements (Murray 2006), in the paper's words

(i) Excluded: not economically relevant in the demand equation itself (argued with economics, not tested); (ii) relevant / strong: correlated with the suspect variable, checked with the first-stage F on the excluded instruments (large F is good; weak instruments can make bias worse); (iii) valid: uncorrelated with the second-stage error, tested with a Sargan test only when there are more instruments than suspect variables (null: all valid). Wu-Hausman then asks whether IV was needed at all (null: OLS is consistent and more efficient). R&S pp. 10–11

Instrument for the market price

Provincial gasoline excise taxes (Davis & Kilian 2011). Strong: excise is a big share of the pump price. Excluded: tax laws change with a “considerable lag,” so they are not driven by short-run demand. Monthly data available. R&S p. 11

Instruments for the carbon tax

Provincial personal and corporate income-tax revenues. Revenue neutrality makes them move opposite to the carbon tax by design, and it is hard to see how total income-tax revenue would change gasoline use per person directly. Only annual data, so these models are annual. R&S p. 11

Defense 3: robustness checks

Match it

Each check in Table 4 (and §5.3) exists to kill one alternative story. Pick the story each check rules out, then hit Check.

What survives. Across all ten Table 4 models the BC carbon-tax coefficient stays between about $-0.013$ and $-0.032$ while the price coefficient stays near $-0.002$ to $-0.0036$. Even the extreme “consumers acted as if the tax were already \$30/t” model (7), which gives an upper bound on the tax elasticity, still finds the tax about 3× as salient. The 2007–2011-only model (6) gives 8.5×. With income controls, 5.5×. R&S pp. 16–19

Not testable, argued away R&S pp. 23–24: cross-border shopping (Alberta and Washington were already cheaper for years; 95% of BC residents live over two hours from Alberta; Washington means an international border) and vehicle programs (SCRAP-IT covered 18,000 vehicles, under 1% of 2 million; hybrids were 1.1% of 2006 sales and mostly replaced other small cars). The analysis is short-run, so vehicle-stock turnover can't explain it.

4

How big, and does it check out?

Section 5.2: three scenarios using the preferred model. The gap between “no tax” and “tax treated like a price” is the ordinary price effect; the rest is salience. R&S pp. 22–23

Over the first four years the tax cut gasoline emissions by 3.61 MtCO2e; about 85% of that (roughly 3.1 Mt) is the extra salience, an “environmental bonus.” By 2011, predicted per-capita demand was 13.5 L/month lower than with no tax. Conversion: 1 litre of gasoline ≈ 2.47 kg CO2e. R&S pp. 2, 22–23, fn. 17

External validity check: the tax was supposed to be revenue-neutral but came in revenue-negative: only 83% of forecast revenue arrived, \$510 million short over four years (16.7%), too big to blame on forecasting error. Demand fell more than the government's (standard-elasticity) forecast assumed. R&S pp. 13, 25

Common exam trap: failing to reject Wu-Hausman is good news for the preferred model. It says OLS and IV estimates differ only by noise, so the more precise OLS numbers can be used. Don't write “the IV test failed.” The honest weak spot is different: in the annual IV models (2) and (3) the authors cannot reject that tax and price effects are equal, and model (3) fails the Sargan test. They blame imprecise annual data and IV's larger standard errors.

Exam framing: “How do Rivers & Schaufele argue their estimate is causal?” Full credit names all three layers: (1) province and month-year fixed effects plus the exogeneity arguments; (2) IV using excise taxes for price and income-tax revenue (via revenue neutrality) for the tax, with F, Sargan and Wu-Hausman results; (3) robustness checks each aimed at a named alternative (income, announcement/storage, structural break, nominal illusion, seasonality, small cars), plus the revenue shortfall as outside confirmation.

1

The question and the gap

Regulators are short of money for monitoring and reluctant to use stringent enforcement, so many have added structured information programs (public disclosure) on top of traditional enforcement (fines and penalties). FLL p. 169

Literature 1: monitoring & enforcement works

Inspections cut water pollution from pulp mills in the US and Quebec (Magat & Viscusi; Laplante & Rilstone) and raise self-reporting; enforcement cut steel-industry non-compliance (Gray & Deily); inspections and enforcement shorten violations (Nadeau); inspections cut pollution in China (Dasgupta et al.). pp. 169–170

Literature 2: disclosure works

Stock prices move on environmental news: up for good performance, down for bad (Hamilton; Konar & Cohen; Lanoie et al.). Disclosure improves performance (Konar & Cohen on the US TRI; Afsah et al. on Indonesia's PROPER). p. 170

The gap: no study had measured both tools within one program, so nobody knew their relative impact. BC is ideal because since July 1990 the Ministry of Environment (MOE) publishes a list of polluters twice a year and keeps prosecuting violators. p. 170

Structured vs. unstructured information (fn. 6): a structured program is a deliberate regulator strategy to reveal performance (the BC list, TRI, PROPER); unstructured information is ad hoc news coverage.

3

BC's list of polluters

First released July 13, 1990 (a first in Canada), then twice a year. Each entry names the firm, location, nature of concern, reason(s) for listing, and how many times it has been listed (“fourth time on the list”). p. 172

Part I: Out of compliance

Significantly out of compliance with permit requirements. Examples: exceeded TSS limits July to September; exceeded BOD 3 of 13 days in November and pH 2 days in December; incomplete monitoring data.

Part II: Of concern

Some technically in compliance, or no permit needed, but of concern by nature. Examples: effluent and the Kitimat River at low flow; landfill leachate near fish streams; sulfur-gas odor; grizzly bears drawn to a landfill.

Why “of concern” was dropped in 1994: firms that were in compliance objected to being listed; the criteria looked subjective and inconsistent across regional offices. From 1995 the list covers only plants significantly out of compliance (Fig. 1). p. 173 The results later back the MOE: of concern listings mostly had no effect.

The market noticed: Lanoie, Laplante & Roy (1998) found listed firms' stock prices fell after publication. p. 170

4

Traditional enforcement: the court route

A fine is the end of a judicial process with its own “somewhat unpredictable” dynamics: if the MOE can't prove a violation beyond reasonable doubt, or the firm shows mitigating circumstances, there is no fine even when the plant really was out of compliance (and on the list). p. 173 In the sample: 126 prosecutions from 1987–1996, only 17 fines (3 under \$1,000), totaling \$582,400. p. 178

Table II: the two tools aren't lockstep. For 4 of 15 plants the list came after a fine, for 4 the fine came after the list, and for 4 others listing was never followed by a fine (one plant was fined in 1995 without ever being listed). That independent variation is what lets the regressions separate the two effects. p. 174

5

The model: more than regulator vs. plant

The traditional paradigm is two players: the regulator sets and enforces rules (ex ante standards or market instruments, ex post liability) and the plant complies. Recent work adds two more: p. 175

  • Communities: richer, better-educated, better-organized ones enforce norms through the political process where regulators exist, and through “informal regulation” via community groups and NGOs where they don't (Pargal & Wheeler; Blackman & Bannister).
  • Markets: investors price in expected penalties and liability; lenders, equipment suppliers and engineering firms avoid known polluters; customers judge reputation.

So the regulator can gain leverage through non-traditional programs that harness communities and markets, like public disclosure. The paper tests the reduced form p. 176

$$\text{Pollution} = f(\text{Regulation},\ \text{Traditional Enforcement},\ \text{Public Disclosure},\ X)$$

Open questions they name: once a regulator can fine, is there still a role for disclosure? Should disclosure complement or replace enforcement? Only Hamilton and Konar & Cohen had done formal econometrics, both on the TRI, where fines and disclosure can't be compared. pp. 175–176

Common exam trap: “The 1990 regulation tightened standards for every plant.” Not quite: it made them uniform. Coastal plants faced a huge tightening, inland BOD stayed at 7.5, and inland TSS loosened from 10 to 11.25. Every plant did have to install secondary treatment, and the maximum fine jumped 60-fold.

Exam framing: “Why is British Columbia a good setting for comparing information and enforcement?” (1) The MOE runs both at once: a public list twice a year and ongoing prosecutions; (2) the two aren't tied together (Table II), so their effects can be separated; (3) the list uses information the MOE already collects, so its extra cost is small; (4) plant-level emissions and limits (kg/day) are available for BOD and TSS, 1987–1996.

  • Estimator: generalized least squares for a cross-sectionally heteroskedastic and timewise autoregressive model (Kmenta), because tests found first-order serial correlation and heteroskedasticity. Adding a trend, plant fixed effects or plant age didn't improve fit (likelihood-ratio tests). p. 179, fns. 22–23
  • Sixteen specifications: Table IV (BOD) specs 1–8 and Table V (TSS) specs 9–16. Within each table: compliance rate then absolute level; contemporaneous then lagged policy variables; region dummies (odd) or bassin dummies (even).
2

What the tables say

Out of compliance listing: works, fast, on both pollutants

Contemporaneous coefficients negative and significant in 7 of 8 cases. BOD: compliance rate improves by about 0.063 and emissions fall 1,187–1,241 kg/day. TSS: compliance improves 0.089–0.095 and emissions fall 1,248–1,279 kg/day. The lagged versions are never significant: with two lists a year (sometimes in January), the reaction shows up in the same year. pp. 179, 184

Of concern listing: mostly nothing

No effect except on the TSS compliance rate in the contemporaneous specs ($-0.055$, $-0.076$). Supports the MOE's 1994 decision to drop the category. p. 184

Regul90: the biggest effect

Negative and significant almost everywhere: compliance improves about 0.16 (BOD) and 0.08 (TSS); emissions fall 3,600–4,640 kg/day (BOD) and 1,330–1,945 kg/day (TSS). Stricter limits plus a 60× higher maximum penalty raise the expected cost of non-compliance, and every plant had to install secondary treatment. p. 184

Prosecutions: no effect. Fines: BOD levels only

Prosecutions are insignificant everywhere. Fines cut BOD absolute emissions by 1,600–1,900 kg/day (10% significance contemporaneous, 5% lagged), but do nothing for BOD compliance or for TSS (except the lagged fine in spec 16, at 10%). p. 184, fn. 24

Lagged dependent variable: strong persistence

Coefficients 0.57–0.73 everywhere: about 65% of this year's pollution is explained by last year's, consistent with slow-changing abatement technology. p. 184

Other controls

Production raises TSS levels (elasticity 0.22–0.36) but slightly improves the BOD compliance rate: bigger mills may have economies of scale in abatement. Location dummies are rarely significant for BOD, often for TSS. Kraft process means worse compliance and higher emissions, more clearly for BOD. p. 185

3

Lists vs. fines: the three observations

ABSBOD, contemporaneous specs 5 and 6 (Table IV): the only place the two effects can be compared directly. Error bars are ±1.96 standard errors backed out from the $t$-statistics.

  1. Breadth. The out-of-compliance listing moves both pollutants, in both levels and compliance rates. Fines only move BOD levels.
  2. Size. Where they are comparable (specs 5 and 6), the fine coefficient (about −1,600) is larger than the listing coefficient (about −1,200), but not significantly: $t = 0.364$ and $0.466$.
  3. Speed. Only the contemporaneous listing variable is significant, suggesting lists give a stronger incentive for a quick fix. Firms may weigh the financial hit from a bad public image more than fines.

Conclusion: the MOE's lists “could have had a relatively stronger impact than the fines as they were applied.” p. 184

4

The policy lesson

Complements, not substitutes. Information can't necessarily replace enforcement; used together they put three kinds of pressure on firms: reputational, financial, judiciary. p. 185

Cheap. The list uses data the MOE already collects for monitoring. Nothing new is gathered; the same information just leaves the regulator's files. The incremental cost is “in all likelihood small” relative to the pollution reductions. p. 185

Two results for policymakers pp. 185–186: (1) clear, strong standards with a significant and credible penalty system send the right signal, and plants cut emissions; (2) public disclosure creates additional and strong incentives. Both belong in the regulator's arsenal.

Common exam trap: a negative coefficient on a compliance-rate regression means better compliance, because COMP measures how far emissions are above the limit. “Out of compliance = $-0.089$ in COMPTSS” means a listing lowered TSS emissions by about 8.9% of the plant's allowable level. Second trap: “fines had a bigger effect than lists.” The point estimate is bigger in one spec, but the difference isn't significant and fines only work on one of four outcomes.

Exam framing: “Compare the effects of public disclosure and fines in Foulon et al.” Hit: the variables (listing counts vs. a fine dummy, contemporaneous and lagged); the numbers (listing: about 1,200 kg/day BOD and 1,250 kg/day TSS; fine: about 1,600 to 1,900 kg/day BOD only; regul90 biggest); the non-significant difference ($t$ about 0.4); speed (only contemporaneous listing matters); and the complements conclusion.

Three shared ideas

The signal carries information beyond the dollars

A carbon tax announces “society has decided emissions cost money, and everyone pays.” A listing announces “this plant is a polluter” to everyone who can punish it. Neither message is carried by a market price or a quiet court fine.

Other agents do the enforcing

R&S: drivers police their own behavior once free-riding ends. FLL: communities, investors and customers police the plant. Both fit Tietenberg's “third wave” and Kotchen's non-traditional channels.

Standard forecasts can be wrong

BC's carbon revenue came in 17% short because demand fell more than price elasticities predicted. The list cost almost nothing yet matched a fine. In both, a model that only counts dollars misses the effect.

Keep the caveats straight. R&S: one province, short run, gasoline only; the annual IV models can't confirm salience; cross-border shopping can't be tested. FLL: only 15 plants; the listing effect is on the edge of significance for BOD compliance; lists and fines are compared directly in only two specifications; “of concern” listings did nothing.

Rivers & Schaufele: guide questions

Answer first, then reveal. Starred questions * are the longer exam-style ones. Each answer has a Plain English toggle; the per-bullet “quiz yourself” cards are at the bottom of this tab.

1

What is tax salience? State the paper's main result with numbers, and explain why standard consumer theory says it shouldn't happen.

Definition. Tax salience is the hypothesis that a price change caused by a tax produces a different demand response than an equal price change caused by the market (supply shocks, crude prices). A tax is “more salient” if demand responds more to it. p. 1

Main result. In the preferred model (Table 2, column 2), a 5¢/L increase in the market price lowers BC's short-run gasoline demand by about 1.8%; a 5¢/L increase in the carbon tax (about \$25/t) lowers it by about 12.5%. The response to the tax is 7.1 times larger, and an F-test rejects equal coefficients at 1%. pp. 2, 13–14

Why theory says no. A consumer facing pump price $q = p + t$ should care only about $q$, so $\partial y^*/\partial p = \partial y^*/\partial t$: a cent is a cent. Here the posted price already includes all taxes, so there isn't even a hidden-tax excuse. pp. 3, 20

Context. Consistent with Li, Linn & Muehlegger (2012), who find gasoline excise taxes have a salience ratio of 8.1, and with Chetty, Looney & Kroft (2009) and Finkelstein (2009). New here: first evidence for an environmental tax, whose only purpose is to cut fuel use. pp. 1–3

If gas goes up a nickel because oil got pricier, BC drivers barely change how much they buy (about 2% less). If gas goes up a nickel because of the carbon tax, they buy about 12% less. Econ 101 says a nickel is a nickel. BC drivers disagreed, by a factor of about 7.

2

Describe the design of the BC carbon tax: timing, rate path and revenue neutrality. Why does revenue neutrality matter, both for the policy and for the paper's identification?

Timing. Hinted at only in late 2007, announced in the February 2008 budget, in force July 1, 2008: North America's first broad carbon tax on all fossil fuels (77% of BC emissions). A surprise to most residents. pp. 3–4

Gradual path. \$10/tCO2e (2.34¢/L) in 2008, up \$5 each July 1 to \$30/t (6.67¢/L) in 2012, to ease adjustment. p. 4; Table 1

Revenue neutrality. All revenue returned: lower personal income tax on the bottom two brackets (average rate on \$100k: 8.74% → 8.02%), corporate rate 12% → 10% in steps, small-business rate cut, low-income quarterly rebate (~\$300/yr for a family of four), and up to \$200 for northern and rural homeowners from 2011. pp. 4–5

Why it matters for policy. (1) Acceptability: a “tax shift,” not a tax increase. (2) Efficiency: cutting distortionary income taxes lowers the marginal cost of public funds (double dividend, Goulder 1995). (3) Commitment: once income taxes were cut the province needed the carbon revenue (\$741M in 2011), so delaying the increases during the recession would mean deficits. p. 5

Why it matters for identification. By design, carbon-tax increases move opposite to personal and corporate income-tax revenue, so those revenues are natural instruments for the carbon tax. pp. 5, 11

*Also useful: it came in revenue-negative (83% of forecast), itself evidence of the larger-than-expected demand response. p. 13

BC started small and raised the tax a bit every summer. Every dollar collected went back to people and businesses as income-tax cuts and rebates, so it was sold as a swap, not a new tax. That swap is handy for the researchers too: when the carbon tax goes up, income-tax money goes down, so they can use one to track the other.

3

Write down the estimating equation. What do the fixed effects absorb, how do you read a coefficient in the log-linear model, and why interact prices and taxes with a BC dummy?

$$\ln y_{it} = \alpha\, p_{it} + \beta\, \tau_{it} + X_{it}\theta + \delta_i + \gamma_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

$y_{it}$: per-adult litres in province $i$, month $t$; $p_{it}$: real price excluding the carbon tax; $\tau_{it}$: real carbon tax (¢/L); $X_{it}$: income, small-car share. pp. 7–8

Fixed effects. $\delta_i$ absorbs time-invariant province traits (geography); $\gamma_t$ absorbs shocks common to all provinces in a month (world oil prices, national business cycle, trade policy). p. 8

Reading coefficients. Log-linear, so $100\beta$ ≈ % change in demand per 1¢ (a semi-elasticity); exactly $e^{\beta}-1$ (fn. 10). $\beta = -0.0247$ → about 2.5% less gasoline per cent of tax. p. 8

BC interaction. BC demand is more price-elastic than the national average. Without $p \times BC$, the tax coefficient picks up BC's extra price sensitivity: the national model gives $-0.0457$ (ratio 33.4); with interactions the BC tax coefficient is $-0.0247$ and the BC price coefficient $-0.0035$ (ratio 7.1). p. 13

*Data details: Jan 1990 to Dec 2011, 2,580 province-months; annual models (216 obs.) for variables only available yearly; HAC (Newey-West) standard errors; first-difference versions as checks. pp. 6, 12

They explain gas use with the price, the tax, and two sets of “catch-all” dummies: one for each province (so BC's mountains don't get blamed on the tax) and one for each month (so a global oil spike doesn't either). Because gas use is in logs, each coefficient is basically “percent change per cent.” They give BC its own price and tax slopes because BC drivers react to prices more than other Canadians.

4

* Identification. Why do the authors believe least squares is already unbiased? What instruments do they use, what must a good instrument satisfy, and what do the first-stage F, Sargan and Wu-Hausman results in Table 3 tell you?

Exogeneity arguments. Gasoline markets are competitive (wholesale set in the West Coast market; thin retail margins); taxes are fully and immediately passed through (Marion & Muehlegger); the carbon tax was fast, surprising, comprehensive and single-purpose. Remaining worries: province-specific omitted factors like bike lanes or uneven recession effects. p. 9

Instruments. Market price ← provincial excise taxes (big share of price; legislated with a long lag, so not driven by short-run demand; Davis & Kilian). Carbon tax ← provincial personal and corporate income-tax revenues (move inversely with the carbon tax because of revenue neutrality; no obvious direct link to gasoline use). p. 11

Requirements (Murray 2006): excluded from the demand equation (argued, not tested); relevant (first-stage F on excluded instruments); valid, uncorrelated with the error (Sargan test, possible only when over-identified). Wu-Hausman asks whether IV differs from OLS by more than noise. pp. 10–11

Table 3. (1) Monthly, price instrumented: F = 435.52 (very strong); exactly identified, so no Sargan; coefficients $-0.0033$ / $-0.0257$, ratio 7.9; equality rejected at 5%; Wu-Hausman 0.12 (p = 0.73), so OLS is consistent and more efficient. (2) Annual, tax instrumented: F = 13.36; Sargan p = 0.98 (valid); but tax coefficient $-0.0054$ is insignificant and equality can't be rejected (ratio 1.3). (3) Annual, both instrumented: Angrist-Pischke F 15.95 and 6.55; Sargan rejects (27.00, p = 0.00); ratio 18.8. pp. 14–16

Their reading. The monthly IV confirms OLS; the annual models are imprecise (IV inflates standard errors) and can't confirm salience statistically. Wu-Hausman never rejects, so the preferred OLS model stands, but “the inability to fully uphold the finding in Table 2 must be noted.” p. 16

They're fairly sure the plain regression is fine, but they double-check with “instruments”: things that push the price or the tax around for reasons unrelated to driving. Excise taxes push the pump price; income-tax revenue drops whenever the carbon tax rises. The monthly double-check gives the same answer (about 8×). The yearly ones are too noisy to prove much. A test says the plain regression was good enough all along.

5

* Robustness. For each alternative explanation, name the check that addresses it and the result: income effects of the tax shift; announcement or storage effects; a structural break in preferences; misreading inflation; expecting the maximum tax; province seasonality; the vehicle stock; cross-border shopping; SCRAP-IT and hybrid subsidies.

Income (Table 4, 1–2). The tax shift raised after-tax income; adding after-tax income (levels and first differences) barely moves the price coefficient and trims the tax effect only a little. Ratio still 5.5. pp. 16–17

Nominal illusion (3). Instrument the real tax with the nominal tax, identifying off the discrete July jumps: $-0.0036$ / $-0.0239$, same story. p. 17

Announcement / storage (4–5). Drop June–July, then May–August. A temporary effect would shrink the tax coefficient toward the price one; it doesn't. Storage capacity is also tiny (about a month). pp. 17–18

Structural break (6). Restrict to 2007–2011: ratio 8.5 at the mean. p. 18

Expecting the \$30 maximum (7). Set the tax at its max throughout: gives an upper bound on the tax elasticity; tax about half the preferred estimate but still about 3× the price effect. pp. 18–19

Province seasonality (8). Province × month fixed effects plus a trend: coefficients in the same neighborhood. Vehicle stock (9–10). Small-car shares have the right sign, little effect. p. 19

Not testable (§5.3). Cross-border shopping: neighbors were already cheaper for years, most residents are far from Alberta, Washington is across an international border. SCRAP-IT (18,000 cars, under 1% of the fleet) and hybrid subsidies (1.1% of 2006 sales) are too small; short-run analysis means little fleet turnover. pp. 23–24

Every “but maybe it was really...” gets its own test. Maybe people just had more money? Control for income. Maybe they filled up before the tax started? Drop those months. Maybe BC was already changing? Look only at recent years. Every version still says the tax hit harder than price, somewhere between 3× and 8.5×. Two stories (driving to Alberta, the car-scrapping program) can't be tested, so they argue those are too small to matter.

6

Why might a carbon tax be more salient than a price change? Explain the resentment-of-free-ridership and relief-of-guilt mechanisms using $u = x + v(y, t)$, the sign each predicts, and how this differs from inattention explanations.

Setup (after Chetty 2009): clean numeraire $x$, gasoline $y$, pump price $q = p + t$, utility $u = x + v(y,t)$, so the tax enters utility directly. Differentiating optimal demand: p. 20

$$\frac{dy^*}{dt} = \left.\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right|_q + \frac{\partial y}{\partial q}\frac{\partial q}{\partial t}$$

A direct effect plus the usual price effect. If the direct effect is zero, $\partial y/\partial p = \partial y/\partial t$.

Resentment of free-ridership ($\left.\partial y/\partial t\right|_q < 0$): an environmentally conscious driver who cuts back lowers the cost of driving for others, who drive more (leakage). Resenting that, she contributes less than she would if free-riding were impossible. A carbon tax makes every driver pay the environmental cost, ending the free-riding and the resentment, so she cuts back beyond the price effect. The tax acts as a focal point / coordinating mechanism. Predicts tax more salient. pp. 20–21

Relief of guilt ($\left.\partial y/\partial t\right|_q > 0$): she drove less from guilt; paying the tax “covers” her damage, so she drives more. Predicts tax less salient. Which dominates is empirical; BC's data point to resentment. pp. 21–22

Versus inattention. Chetty, Looney & Kroft, Finkelstein, and Goldin & Homonoff explain salience by irrationality: inattention or cognitive costs lead to misoptimization. R&S instead invoke non-standard, other-regarding preferences (Congdon, Kling & Mullainathan). Gasoline prices are posted tax-inclusive, so inattention is less plausible here. pp. 3, 19–20

*Versus warm glow / impure public goods (fn. 15): in those models taxes still act only through prices; here the tax itself is an argument of utility.

“Why should I bike to work so my neighbor can enjoy an emptier highway in his truck?” That's resentment. A carbon tax makes the neighbor pay too, so biking no longer feels like being the sucker, and people cut back more than the price alone would cause. The opposite story (“I paid my carbon tax, so I've done my bit”) would make the tax weaker. BC's data look like the first story.

7

* How much CO2 did the tax save, and how much of that is due to salience? What evidence supports external validity, and what are the policy implications?

Three scenarios from the preferred model: (1) no carbon tax (baseline); (2) “equivalent response,” the tax treated like any price increase; (3) “salient carbon tax,” the model's actual prediction. Per-capita litres × population → provincial litres → CO2e at 2.47 kg/L. pp. 22–23

Result. 3.61 MtCO2e saved from gasoline over the first four years; about 85% (~3.1 Mt) comes from salience, an “environmental bonus” that the equivalent-response scenario would miss. By 2011 predicted demand is 13.5 L per person per month below baseline, and it keeps falling while the other two scenarios rise. pp. 2, 22–23

External validity. Designed revenue-neutral, the tax turned out revenue-negative: 83% of forecast revenue, \$510M (16.7%) short over four years, too big for forecast error. Demand fell more than standard elasticities predicted. pp. 13, 25

Policy implications. (1) People don't respond to tax increases the way they respond to supply shocks, so using ordinary gasoline elasticities mis-forecasts emissions cuts and revenue. (2) A new wrinkle in the tax vs. cap-and-trade debate: if consumers see a carbon tax as an environmental price but an allowance price as a supply shock, equal carbon prices may produce different behavior. (3) Evidence that behavioral economics, including non-standard preferences, belongs in tax and environmental policy. pp. 25–26

*Limits: one province, short run, gasoline only (the tax covers all fuels), and the annual IV models don't confirm salience statistically.

About 3.6 million tonnes of CO2 were avoided in four years, and most of that came from the “it's a carbon tax” effect, not the nickel itself. The best reality check: the government collected less money than planned, because people bought less gas than the usual formulas said they would.

F

Foulon, Lanoie & Laplante: guide questions

Answer first, then reveal. Starred questions * are exam-style. Q7 is a synthesis question across both papers.

1

What two empirical literatures does the paper bring together, what gap does it fill, and why is British Columbia a good place to fill it?

Motivation. Regulators lack resources for monitoring and are reluctant to use stringent enforcement, so many now supplement fines and penalties with structured information (public disclosure) programs. p. 169

Literature 1: traditional monitoring and enforcement. Inspections and the threat of inspections cut pulp-mill water pollution (Magat & Viscusi, US; Laplante & Rilstone, Quebec) and increase self-reporting; enforcement cut steel-industry air non-compliance (Gray & Deily); inspections and enforcement shorten violations (Nadeau); inspections cut pollution in China (Dasgupta et al.). pp. 169–170

Literature 2: public disclosure. Capital markets react to environmental information, upward for good news and downward for bad (Hamilton; Konar & Cohen; Lanoie et al.), and disclosure improves performance (Konar & Cohen on the TRI; Afsah et al. on PROPER). p. 170

Gap. No paper studied both in one program, so their relative effectiveness was unknown. With the TRI, for example, disclosure can't be compared with prosecutions. pp. 170, 176

Why BC. Since July 1990 the MOE publishes a list of polluters twice a year and keeps prosecuting violators; the market noticed the lists (listed firms' stock fell); and fines and listings don't line up one-to-one (Table II), so their effects can be separated. pp. 170, 174

Some studies showed inspections and fines work. Others showed naming and shaming works. Nobody had tested both on the same factories at the same time to see which works better. BC was doing both at once, so it was the perfect test case.

2

Describe BC's 1990 regulatory changes and its list of polluters. What did the two sections of the list contain, and why was one of them dropped?

New effluent regulation (Dec 13, 1990). Every plant needs a discharge permit, conditional on secondary wastewater treatment. Location- and process-specific standards were replaced by uniform ones (7.5 kg/ton BOD, 11.25 kg/ton TSS; stricter at Port Alberni), a large tightening for coastal plants (kraft BOD 30 → 7.5). Phased in over 1991–1994. pp. 171–172; Table I

Tougher penalties. Maximum fine under the Waste Management Act rose from \$50,000 to \$3 million. p. 174

The list. First published July 13, 1990 (a first in Canada), then twice a year. Entries give the firm, location, nature of concern, reasons, and number of times listed. p. 172

Part I, out of compliance: significantly violating permit requirements (exceeded TSS limits for months; BOD on 3 of 13 days; incomplete monitoring data). Part II, of concern: operations the MOE worried about even if technically compliant or not requiring a permit (river impacts at low flow, leachate near fish streams, odor, grizzly bears at a landfill). pp. 172–173

Dropped in 1994: complaints from compliant firms about being listed, and criteria seen as subjective and inconsistent across regional offices. From 1995, only out-of-compliance plants appear. p. 173

In 1990 BC did three things at once: made every mill meet the same (much tougher for coastal mills) water-pollution limits with modern treatment, raised the biggest possible fine from \$50k to \$3M, and started publishing a twice-yearly “naughty list.” The list had a “breaking the rules” section and a vaguer “we're worried about you” section; the vague one got scrapped in 1994 after companies complained it was unfair.

3

What model do the authors test? Beyond the regulator and the plant, which agents matter, and through what channels could public disclosure affect a plant's pollution?

Traditional paradigm. The regulator sets and enforces rules: ex ante regulation (standards vs. market-based instruments) and ex post liability. Focus is almost entirely regulator vs. plant. p. 175

Communities. Neighbors influence performance (Blackman & Bannister; Pargal & Wheeler). Richer, more educated, more organized communities push for tighter enforcement through politics, or impose “informal regulation” through community groups and NGOs where regulators are weak. p. 175

Markets. Investors weigh potential losses from penalties and liability; growth and internationalization of stock markets raise investor attention; lenders, equipment suppliers and engineering firms avoid known polluters; customers, suppliers and stockholders judge reputation. p. 175

Implication. The regulator's role need not be limited to designing, monitoring and enforcing standards; it can gain leverage by harnessing communities and markets, for instance through disclosure. p. 175

$$\text{Pollution} = f(\text{Regulation},\ \text{Traditional Enforcement},\ \text{Public Disclosure},\ X)$$

Questions: once courts and fines are available, is there still a role for disclosure? Does it create further incentives? Complement or substitute? p. 176

It's not just the government vs. the factory. Neighbors complain, vote and organize; investors sell the stock; banks and suppliers get nervous; customers walk. Putting a factory's name on a public list hands all of those people a reason to push. The paper asks: pollution depends on the rules, the fines, the list, and other stuff. Which ones actually matter?

4

* Describe the data and variables: the sample, the two ways pollution is measured (and how to read their sign), the policy variables and why some are lagged, the controls, and the estimation method.

Sample. Plant-level annual data, pulp and paper, 1987–1996: 24 plants, minus 4 non-comparable and 5 with incomplete files = 15 plants. All data from the MOE (emissions and limits by special request). Pulp and paper chosen for its long regulatory history and good emissions data. p. 176

Dependent variables, for BOD and TSS: ABS = absolute emissions (kg/day); COMP = (actual − allowable)/allowable, with allowable = standard (kg/ton) × production (ton/day). Negative COMP means under the limit; a negative coefficient means improvement. p. 176, fn. 19

Policy variables. Out of compliance and of concern: number of appearances in that section this year (two lists a year). Prosecution: number of prosecutions. Fine: dummy for a fine of \$1,000+. Regul90: dummy for operating under the new regulation. pp. 177–178

Lags. Policy variables enter contemporaneously or lagged one year, to allow reaction time and avoid simultaneity (pollution driving enforcement). p. 178, fn. 20

Controls. Lagged dependent variable (proxy for pollution-control capital and technology); actual production in ton/day (first time real production is used rather than capacity); bassin (receiving river, 6) or region (7) dummies for local awareness, lobbying, monitoring and ecosystem damage; process dummy (kraft = 1, expected positive). pp. 178–179

Method. GLS for a cross-sectionally heteroskedastic, timewise autoregressive model (Kmenta), since tests found AR(1) errors and heteroskedasticity. Trend, fixed effects and plant age didn't improve fit (LR tests). 16 specifications (Tables IV and V). p. 179

Fifteen mills, ten years. Pollution is measured two ways: raw kilograms per day, and “how far over the limit” (where negative is good). The explanations: times on the list, prosecutions, whether they got fined, and whether the new rules applied yet, plus last year's pollution, how much they produced, where they are, and what kind of mill they are. Some policy variables use last year's value, because a mill needs time to fix things.

5

* Summarize the results in Tables IV and V. Compare the effects of being listed, prosecutions, fines and the new regulation, with magnitudes. Why does only the contemporaneous listing variable matter? What do the three observations comparing fines and lists say?

Out of compliance listing. Contemporaneous coefficient negative and significant in all but one case. BOD: compliance rate improves about 0.063; emissions fall 1,187–1,241 kg/day. TSS: compliance improves 0.089–0.095; emissions fall 1,248–1,279 kg/day. Lagged version not significant, unsurprising with two lists a year (some in January). pp. 179, 184

Of concern listing. No effect, except on the TSS compliance rate with contemporaneous variables; supports dropping it in 1994. p. 184

Regul90. Negative and significant almost everywhere, and the largest: compliance improves ~0.16 (BOD) and ~0.08 (TSS); emissions fall 3,600–4,640 kg/day (BOD) and 1,330–1,945 kg/day (TSS). Stricter limits raise the probability of being caught out of compliance; together with the bigger maximum penalty and mandatory secondary treatment, plants responded strongly (Fig. 2). p. 184

Prosecutions: no effect on either pollutant. Fines: cut BOD absolute emissions by 1,600–1,900 kg/day (contemporaneous at 10%, lagged at 5%); no effect on BOD compliance or TSS (except lagged fine in spec 16). p. 184

Three observations. (1) Listing affects both pollutants in both measures; fines only BOD levels. (2) Where comparable (specs 5–6, ABSBOD, contemporaneous), fine coefficients are larger than listing coefficients but not significantly ($t$ = 0.364, 0.466). (3) Only the contemporaneous listing matters, suggesting lists prompt a quick response; firms may weigh a negative public image more than fines. So lists “could have had a relatively stronger impact than the fines as they were applied.” p. 184

*Controls. Lagged DV 0.57–0.73 (~65% persistence); production raises TSS (elasticity 0.22–0.36) but improves BOD compliance (economies of scale in abatement); location matters more for TSS; kraft means higher pollution. pp. 184–185

The new rules did the most. Getting on the “breaking the rules” list worked too, on both kinds of pollution, and mills reacted the same year. Being prosecuted did nothing by itself. Actually getting fined cut one kind of pollution, by a bit more than the list, but the difference could easily be luck. And whatever a mill polluted last year is the best predictor of this year.

6

What do the authors conclude? Are information strategies substitutes or complements for traditional enforcement, and why is disclosure cheap?

Findings recap. Tightening standards in 1990 had a very significant effect; appearing on the list improved performance; the list's impact was relatively stronger than fines'. p. 185

Complements. Information strategies “cannot necessarily replace” traditional enforcement; used together, they put reputational, financial and judiciary pressure on firms and raise the chance they act. p. 185

Cheap. The list uses information already collected for monitoring; nothing new is gathered, it is simply made public instead of kept in the regulator's hands. The incremental cost is likely small relative to the pollution reduction. p. 185

Two policy results. (1) Clear, strong standards with a significant, credible penalty system send the right signal. (2) Public disclosure creates additional and strong incentives. Both belong in the regulator's arsenal. pp. 185–186

Don't swap fines for lists: use both. Strong rules backed by real penalties do the heavy lifting, and the list adds pressure almost for free, because the government already has the data. It just has to publish it.

7

* Both papers Both papers find that the form of a policy matters beyond its monetary incentive. Explain, using evidence and a mechanism from each, and give one limitation of each.

Standard view. Behavior responds to the size of the incentive: the pump price per litre, or the expected penalty (probability × fine). How the incentive is delivered shouldn't matter.

Rivers & Schaufele. Evidence: the same 5¢ cut demand ~12.5% as a carbon tax but ~1.8% as a market price (ratio ~7; F-test rejects equality; robust to IV and ten checks; revenue shortfall confirms). Mechanism: the tax enters utility directly; resentment of free-ridership disappears once everyone pays, so the tax coordinates behavior. Limitation: one province, short run; annual IV models can't confirm; cross-border shopping untestable.

Foulon, Lanoie & Laplante. Evidence: a costless public listing cut BOD and TSS by ~1,200 kg/day each and improved compliance, while fines (a direct financial penalty) only cut BOD levels and prosecutions did nothing; the fine-vs-list difference isn't significant. Mechanism: disclosure activates communities, investors and customers; the response is fast. Limitation: 15 plants; the listing effect on BOD compliance is marginal; direct comparison in only two specifications.

Common thread. A public signal recruits other forces (other-regarding preferences; community and market pressure) that a price or a quiet fine doesn't. Policy lessons: don't forecast with ordinary price elasticities; add disclosure to standards and penalties rather than relying on penalties alone.

How the message is delivered changes the reaction. A tax labeled “carbon” changes how drivers feel about driving; a price spike doesn't. A mill's name in the newspaper gets neighbors, investors and customers involved; a court fine stays between the mill and the judge. Both papers: same size, different channel, different result.
