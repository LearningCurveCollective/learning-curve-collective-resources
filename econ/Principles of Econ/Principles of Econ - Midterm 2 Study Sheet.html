Cheat Sheet: the eleven things to have automatic

Built from the Midterm 2 practice exam and our session (tax wedge, wage = price × MPL, MRS, the park vote). Each row is one idea the test will hit: the picture, the econ, and the plain-English version. If you can explain every row out loud and draw its picture, you are ready for the free-response section. The Numbers Drill tab is where you make the formulas automatic.

Opportunity cost

EconThe value of the next best alternative you give up when you choose. The opportunity cost of taking a job is the wage of your second-best offer. A student takes a low-wage job with on-the-job training because it builds human capital: a cost now, higher wages later.

Plain EnglishWhat you gave up to get what you chose. If your second-best offer paid \$50k, taking the first one costs you \$50k, whatever it pays.

The tax wedge: revenue box, deadweight triangle

EconA per-unit tax $T$ opens a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep: $P_B - P_S = T$. Quantity falls from $Q^*$ to $Q_T$. Revenue is the box, deadweight loss is the triangle of trades that no longer happen. It does not matter who legally pays the tax: the wedge, the burdens and the DWL come out identical.

$\text{Revenue} = T \times Q_T \qquad \text{DWL} = \tfrac12 \times T \times (Q^* - Q_T) \qquad \text{Consumer burden} = P_B - P^* \quad \text{Producer burden} = P^* - P_S$

Plain EnglishThe tax kills the trades that were only barely worth it. Those lost trades are the deadweight loss; the trades that survive are what the government collects on.

Who pays the tax: elasticity decides

EconThe burdens always add up to $T$, but the more inelastic side bears more of it (steep curve = stuck = pays). More elastic curves also mean a bigger drop in $Q$, so a bigger DWL. DWL grows with the square of the tax: double $T$ and DWL quadruples, while revenue first rises, then falls once the tax is large (Laffer curve).

Plain EnglishWhoever cannot walk away from the deal ends up paying most of the tax, no matter whose name is on the bill.

Externalities and the Pigouvian tax

EconA negative externality means $MSC = MPC + $ external cost. The market sets $D = MPC$ and produces $Q_{mkt}$; the efficient quantity is where $D = MSC$, so the market overproduces. A Pigouvian tax equal to the external cost per unit (the gap $MSC - MPC$ at $Q_{eff}$) makes firms internalize it and lands the market on $Q_{eff}$ with zero DWL. A positive externality flips it: $MSB > MPB$, market underproduces, fix with a subsidy.

$\text{DWL} = \tfrac12 \times (Q_{mkt} - Q_{eff}) \times (MSC - MPC)\ \text{at } Q_{mkt}$

Plain EnglishPollution is a cost somebody else pays, so the factory makes too much. Charge the factory exactly that cost per unit and it chooses the right amount on its own.

Public goods: add up the benefits, then vote

EconPublic good = nonexcludable and nonrival, so free riders mean the market underprovides and government steps in. Efficient size: keep adding units while $\sum$ WTP (everyone's marginal benefit added up) $\ge$ marginal cost. With equal cost shares and majority voting, each person says yes only while their own WTP $\ge$ their share, so the vote follows the median voter, not the total, and can build too little or too much. Common resource = rival but nonexcludable: overused (tragedy of the commons).

Plain EnglishFor the next acre of park, add what everyone would pay for it. Build it if that beats the cost. A vote only counts heads, not how much each person cares.

Cost curves: MC rises, AFC falls, ATC is a U

Econ$MC = \Delta TC/\Delta Q = \Delta VC/\Delta Q$ (fixed cost never changes MC). $ATC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC$. MC rises because of diminishing marginal product; AFC falls as fixed cost is spread; their tug-of-war gives the U-shaped ATC. MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimums (the minimum of ATC is the efficient scale). From a table: MC comes from differences between rows, ATC from dividing by $Q$.

Plain EnglishEach extra unit costs more to make than the last, but the rent gets spread over more units. Average cost falls, bottoms out, then climbs.

Perfect competition: price taker, P = MR, long-run zero profit

EconMany firms, identical goods, free entry: the firm's demand is horizontal at the market price, so $D = AR = MR = P$. Produce where $P = MC$. $\text{Profit} = (P - ATC) \times Q$. Short run: operate if $P \ge AVC$, shut down if $P < AVC$ (you still owe fixed cost either way). Long run: exit if $P < ATC$. Profits attract entry, losses cause exit, until $P = \min ATC$ and economic profit $= 0$. Demand rises: short-run price and profit jump (X), entry shifts supply right, long run returns to $P = \min ATC$ with more firms (Z).

Plain EnglishA tiny firm cannot move the price, so it just makes units while each one costs less than the price. Any profit draws in rivals until the profit is gone.

Monopoly: MR below demand, MR = MC, then up to D

EconOne seller facing the whole downward-sloping demand curve, so $MR < P$: selling one more unit means cutting the price on every unit. Linear demand $P = a - bQ$ gives $MR = a - 2bQ$ (twice the slope, same intercept). Profit max: $MR = MC$ gives $Q_m$, then read $P_m$ off the demand curve, never off MR. $\text{Profit} = (P_m - ATC) \times Q_m$. Efficient output is where $D = MC$; the monopoly DWL is the triangle between $Q_m$ and $Q_{eff}$. $AR = P$ for every firm; $AR = MR$ only in perfect competition.

$\text{DWL}_{mono} = \tfrac12 \times (Q_{eff} - Q_m) \times (P_m - MC_{Q_m})$

Plain EnglishThe monopolist sells fewer units at a higher price. The units people would value above their cost but never get made are the deadweight loss.

Wages: W = P × MPL

EconA competitive firm hires until wage $=$ value of the marginal product of labor $= P \times MPL$. MPL is productivity; the price turns it into dollars. More skill, education and experience (human capital) mean higher MPL and higher wages. Teens have lower MPL than adults, so a single minimum wage binds harder on them, and teen labor demand is more elastic (easy to substitute). Discrimination: an employer who hires an equally productive but underpaid group earns more profit, so competition erodes wage gaps; a gap that tracks real productivity differences (less training) is not discrimination.

Plain EnglishYou get paid what your work sells for. A baker's wage is cakes per hour times the price of a cake.

Indifference curves and the MRS

EconAn indifference curve joins every bundle that gives the same utility: higher curves are better, curves never cross, and they bow toward the origin. MRS $=$ the slope's size $=$ how much $Y$ (cake) you would give up for one more $X$ (donut) and stay equally happy. It falls as you move down the curve: steep where cake is plentiful (A), flat where cake is scarce (C). The budget line has slope $-P_X/P_Y$; the best affordable bundle is where the curve is tangent to it, $MRS = P_X/P_Y$.

Plain EnglishOn your tenth slice of cake you would trade a pile of it for one donut. On your last slice you would barely give up a crumb.

Income effect vs substitution effect

EconWhen the price of $X$ rises: the substitution effect says $X$ is now relatively pricier, so buy less $X$ (always). The income effect says you are effectively poorer: for a normal good that also means less $X$, for an inferior good it means more $X$. Normal good: both effects push the same way, quantity falls for sure. Inferior good: they fight, and only in the rare Giffen case does the income effect win.

Plain EnglishA price hike hits twice: the good looks worse next to its rivals, and your money buys less overall.

Tricks & shortcuts.

  • MR from linear demand: same intercept, double the slope. $P = 20 - Q$ becomes $MR = 20 - 2Q$.
  • Monopoly price: find $Q$ where $MR = MC$, then go up to the demand curve for the price. Never read the price off MR.
  • Every DWL is a triangle: $\tfrac12 \times$ (lost quantity) $\times$ (the gap). Forgot the $\tfrac12$? Your answer is double.
  • Pigouvian tax = the gap between MSC and MPC. If the two lines are parallel, just subtract the intercepts.
  • Steep side pays: whichever curve is more inelastic carries more of the tax.
  • Surplus check: old $CS + PS$ $=$ new $CS + PS +$ revenue $+$ DWL. If it doesn't balance, an area is wrong.

Part II is 2 free-response graph problems with "show all work": a monopolist (price, quantity, profit, DWL, efficient quantity) and a negative externality (equilibrium, CS, PS, DWL, Pigouvian tax, then CS, PS, revenue and DWL with the tax). Taxes, Externalities & Public Goods

Everything in this tab is the same move: draw demand and supply, find where the market lands, find where society would want it to land, and measure the gap as a triangle. Taxes push the market away from efficiency; Pigouvian taxes push it toward efficiency; public goods ask what the efficient size is when no market exists at all.

Play first: drag the tax, watch the wedge

Demand is $P = 20 - Q$ and supply is $P = 4 + Q$ (equilibrium $Q^* = 8$, $P^* = 12$). Slide the tax and watch the revenue box and the DWL triangle. Then flip supply to the flat (elastic) version and watch who pays.

Surplus is area. Memorize the shapes, not just the words

Consumer surplus (CS)

What buyers were willing to pay minus what they actually pay: the triangle below demand, above the price. Linear demand: $CS = \tfrac12 \times Q \times (\text{demand intercept} - P)$.

Producer surplus (PS)

Price received minus what sellers would have accepted (their cost): the triangle above supply, below the price. $PS = \tfrac12 \times Q \times (P - \text{supply intercept})$.

Total surplus and deadweight loss

$TS = CS + PS$ (plus government revenue when there is a tax, minus external cost when there is an externality). The free market with no externality maximizes $TS$. DWL is any fall in total surplus that nobody receives: the triangle between the efficient quantity and the actual quantity.

A. The tax recipe (what we did on the whiteboard)

Procedure
  1. Find the no-tax equilibrium $Q^*, P^*$ (set demand $=$ supply).
  2. Fit the wedge. Buyers pay $P_B$, sellers keep $P_S = P_B - T$. Solve $D(Q) - S(Q) = T$ for $Q_T$. With $D{:}\ P = a - bQ$ and $S{:}\ P = c + dQ$: $Q_T = \dfrac{a - c - T}{b + d}$.
  3. Read the prices off each curve at $Q_T$: $P_B = a - bQ_T$, $P_S = c + dQ_T$.
  4. Revenue $= T \times Q_T$ (the box). If the problem gives revenue and $T$, this is how you back out $Q_T$: on the practice exam, $1{,}220 / 0.25 = 4{,}880$.
  5. DWL $= \tfrac12 \times T \times (Q^* - Q_T)$. Base is the tax, height is the lost quantity (or the other way round; it is a triangle).
  6. Burdens: consumers $P_B - P^*$, producers $P^* - P_S$; they add to $T$. New $CS = \tfrac12 \times Q_T \times (a - P_B)$, new $PS = \tfrac12 \times Q_T \times (P_S - c)$.

Check: old $CS +$ old $PS$ $=$ new $CS +$ new $PS +$ revenue $+$ DWL. If your numbers do not balance, one of the areas is wrong.

B. Externalities

Vocabulary
  • Externality: a cost or benefit that lands on a bystander who is not part of the trade. Negative: pollution, noise, congestion. Positive: vaccines, education, restored historic buildings, technology spillovers.
  • Private vs social. $MSC = MPC + \text{external cost}$. $MSB = MPB + \text{external benefit}$. The market uses the private curves; efficiency needs the social ones.
  • Negative externality: market $Q$ (where $D = MPC$) is above the efficient $Q$ (where $D = MSC$). DWL is the triangle between the two quantities, bounded by $MSC$ above and $D$ below.
  • Positive externality: market $Q$ is below efficient $Q$ (where $MSB = S$). Fix with a subsidy equal to the external benefit.
  • Pigouvian tax: a per-unit tax equal to the external cost, measured as $MSC - MPC$ at the efficient quantity. It shifts the firm's cost curve up onto $MSC$ so the market picks $Q_{eff}$ by itself. Unlike an ordinary tax it removes DWL instead of creating it.
  • Other fixes: regulation (quantity limits), tradable pollution permits (a market in the right to pollute, same result as a tax if the number of permits is set right), and private bargaining under the Coase theorem: if property rights are clear and transaction costs are low, the parties bargain to the efficient outcome on their own, whoever holds the right.
Reading a scenario like the gasoline one

"The 300th gallon has private cost 3.07, social cost 3.33, value to consumers 3.48" tells you: external cost per gallon $= 3.33 - 3.07 = 0.26$; at 300 gallons value $>$ social cost, so the 300th gallon should be produced; the market equilibrium (330 gallons) is where $D$ meets private cost, so the price there must be below 3.48 (demand slopes down from 3.48 at 300) and above 3.07 (supply slopes up from 3.07 at 300). Only \$3.12 fits.

Common exam traps. (1) Using $Q_T$ instead of $Q^* - Q_T$ as the triangle's height: DWL only covers the lost units. (2) Counting tax revenue as a loss: it is a transfer to the government and stays in total surplus; only the triangle is lost. (3) Assuming the side that writes the check bears the tax: elasticity decides, not the law. (4) Measuring the Pigouvian tax at the market quantity when the external cost varies with output: it is the gap at the efficient quantity. (5) In a tax problem, reading $P_B$ off the supply curve or $P_S$ off demand: buyers' price always comes from demand, sellers' from supply.

Exam framing for the externality free response. (a) Set $D = MPC$: $20 - Q = 4 + Q$, so $Q = 8$, $P = 12$. (c) Pigouvian tax $= MSC - MPC = 4$. (d) With the tax the supply curve is $MSC$: $Q = 6$, buyers pay 14, sellers keep 10. $CS = \tfrac12(6)(20 - 14) = 18$, $PS = \tfrac12(6)(10 - 4) = 18$, revenue $= 4 \times 6 = 24$, $DWL = 0$. Costs, Competition & Monopoly

Every firm in this course does the same thing: produce where marginal revenue equals marginal cost. What changes between market structures is what marginal revenue is. For a price taker it is the price. For a monopolist it is less than the price, and that gap is where the deadweight loss comes from.

Play first: solve the monopoly graph one step at a time

This is the free-response figure from the practice exam: $D{:}\ P = 20 - Q$, $MR = 20 - 2Q$, $MC = 2 + Q$, and $ATC = 10$ at the chosen quantity. Click Next step and say the step out loud before you click.

A. Costs: the vocabulary and the table skills

Definitions
  • Fixed cost (FC): does not change with output; paid even at $Q = 0$. Variable cost (VC): rises with output. $TC = FC + VC$.
  • Marginal cost: $MC = \dfrac{\Delta TC}{\Delta Q} = \dfrac{\Delta VC}{\Delta Q}$. Fixed cost drops out, so MC is the same whether you difference TC or VC.
  • Averages: $AFC = FC/Q$ (always falling), $AVC = VC/Q$, $ATC = TC/Q = AFC + AVC$.
  • Shapes: MC eventually rises because of diminishing marginal product (each extra worker adds less output, so each extra unit costs more). ATC is U-shaped: falling AFC wins at first, rising MC wins later. MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimums: whenever the marginal is below the average it drags the average down; above, it pulls it up.
  • Efficient scale: the $Q$ that minimizes ATC. Economies of scale: long-run ATC falls as $Q$ grows; diseconomies: it rises; constant returns: flat.
  • Economic vs accounting profit: economic profit subtracts all opportunity costs, including implicit ones (the owner's forgone salary); accounting profit subtracts only explicit costs. Zero economic profit still means the owner is doing as well as the next best option.

Play: fill the cost table (practice exam Table 14-8)

Work out each hidden column in your head, then reveal it. The MC column is the answer to "what shape is the marginal cost curve?"

MC $= \Delta VC$ between rows: $10, 30, 40, 50, 70, 100$. It rises every step, so the MC curve is upward-sloping (not U-shaped: it never falls). ATC bottoms out at $30$ (at $Q = 1$ and $Q = 2$), and MC at $Q = 2$ is exactly $30$: MC passes through ATC at its minimum, as it must.

Play: the price-taker's decision

A competitive firm with $FC = 32$ and $VC = Q^3 - 6Q^2 + 20Q$ (so $\min AVC = 11$ at $Q = 3$ and $\min ATC = 20$ at $Q = 4$). Slide the market price. The firm picks $Q$ where $P = MC$ on the rising part of MC, then checks whether to operate at all.

B. Perfect competition

Defining features
  • Many buyers and sellers, identical products, free entry and exit. Each firm is a price taker: its own demand curve is horizontal at the market price.
  • Because the price never changes with the firm's output, $P = AR = MR$. Total revenue $= P \times Q$, average revenue $= TR/Q = P$ (true for every firm), marginal revenue $= \Delta TR / \Delta Q = P$ (true only for a price taker).
  • Profit max: produce where $P = MC$ (on the rising section). Profit $= (P - ATC) \times Q$: the rectangle between price and ATC.
Shutdown and exit rules
  • Short run (fixed cost is sunk): operate if $P \ge AVC$ (revenue covers variable cost, so producing loses less than the full fixed cost). Shut down if $P < AVC$. The firm's short-run supply curve is MC above min AVC.
  • Long run (everything is variable): exit if $P < ATC$, enter if $P > ATC$. Long-run supply is MC above min ATC.
  • A sunk cost is already paid and unrecoverable; rational decisions ignore it. That is why the short-run rule compares price to AVC, not ATC.
Long-run adjustment (practice exam Figure 15-7)

Start at W: $P_W = \min ATC$, zero economic profit, each firm at $q_0$. Demand rises to $D_1$. Short run: price jumps to $P_X$, each firm expands to $q_1$ along MC and earns profit (point X). Entry: profit attracts new firms, market supply shifts right to $S_1$, price falls back to $P_W$. Long run: point Z, more firms, same price, each firm back at $q_0$ with zero profit. The long-run market supply is horizontal at $\min ATC$ (if input prices are constant). The "best" answer to the question is the whole story (Z), not the stop along the way (X).

C. Monopoly

Why MR is below price

The monopolist is the whole market, so it faces the downward-sloping demand curve. To sell one more unit it must lower the price on all units. MR $=$ price of the extra unit (the output effect) minus the revenue lost on every earlier unit (the price effect), so $MR < P$, and MR can even go negative. For linear demand $P = a - bQ$: $TR = aQ - bQ^2$ and $MR = a - 2bQ$. Same intercept, twice the slope; MR hits zero at half the demand intercept (that is why MR hits the axis at $Q = 10$ when demand hits it at $Q = 20$).

The four-step solution
  1. Set $MR = MC$ and solve for $Q_m$.
  2. Plug $Q_m$ into the demand curve for $P_m$ (the price customers pay). Never read the price off MR.
  3. Profit $= (P_m - ATC_{Q_m}) \times Q_m$, or $TR - TC$. From a table: compute $(P - ATC) \times Q$ for every row and pick the largest.
  4. Efficient $Q$ is where $D = MC$ (price equals marginal cost). DWL $= \tfrac12 \times (Q_{eff} - Q_m) \times (P_m - MC_{Q_m})$: the triangle between demand and MC from $Q_m$ to $Q_{eff}$.
Sources of monopoly and policy
  • Barriers to entry: a key resource, government-granted rights (patents, copyrights), or a natural monopoly (one firm can serve the market at lower ATC than two, because ATC keeps falling).
  • No supply curve: a monopolist picks a price-quantity point, not a schedule.
  • Policy: antitrust, regulation (price $=$ MC is efficient but a natural monopoly then loses money; price $=$ ATC breaks even), public ownership, or doing nothing. Price discrimination (charging different buyers different prices) raises profit and can reduce DWL, since more units get sold; perfect price discrimination has zero DWL but zero consumer surplus.

Common exam traps. (1) "For all firms MR $=$ P": false; only price takers. "Only competitive firms have AR $=$ P": false; AR $=$ P for everyone. The unique competitive fact is AR $=$ MR. (2) MR is a change in total revenue, not $TR/Q$ (that is AR). (3) Reading the monopoly price at the $MR = MC$ point instead of going up to demand. (4) Calling a strictly rising MC column "U-shaped": U-shaped needs a falling section first. (5) Shutting down when $P < ATC$: in the short run you only shut down when $P < AVC$; between AVC and ATC you operate at a loss smaller than the fixed cost. (6) Treating fixed cost as part of marginal cost.

Exam framing for the monopoly free response. Write the curves first ($D{:}\ P = 20 - Q$, $MR = 20 - 2Q$, $MC = 2 + Q$). (a) $MR = MC$: $20 - 2Q = 2 + Q \Rightarrow Q = 6$; $P = 20 - 6 = 14$. (b) Profit $= (P - ATC)Q = (14 - 10)(6) = 24$. (c) Efficient $Q$: $20 - Q = 2 + Q \Rightarrow Q = 9$; DWL $= \tfrac12 (9 - 6)(14 - 8) = 9$, where 8 is MC (and MR) at $Q = 6$. (d) Socially efficient quantity $= 9$, at $P = 11$. Shade the profit rectangle and the DWL triangle on the graph, and label them.

Labor & Consumer Choice

Two chapters that look unrelated and test the same way. Labor: a wage is a price, and the firm buys workers the way it buys anything, up to the point where the last one is worth exactly what it costs. Consumer choice: a shopper buys up to the point where the last trade-off between two goods matches their prices. Both are "marginal benefit equals marginal cost" in disguise.

Play first: walk along the indifference curve

Every bundle on this curve gives the same happiness: cake $\times$ donuts $= 20$. Drag the point and watch the slope. The MRS is how many slices of cake you would hand over for one more donut without feeling worse off.

A. Consumer choice

Budget constraint
  • The bundles you can afford: $P_X X + P_Y Y = I$. Intercepts $I/P_X$ and $I/P_Y$; slope $= -P_X/P_Y$, the rate the market lets you trade $Y$ for $X$.
  • Income rises: the line shifts out in parallel. $P_X$ rises: the line pivots in on the $X$ axis (the $Y$ intercept does not move).
Indifference curves: four properties
  1. Higher is better (more of both goods), because more is preferred to less. E beats everything on $I_2$.
  2. Downward-sloping: giving up some $Y$ needs more $X$ to stay equally happy.
  3. Never cross: crossing would make you indifferent between a bundle and one that has strictly more of everything.
  4. Bowed inward (convex): the MRS falls as you move down the curve. Extreme cases: perfect substitutes are straight lines (constant MRS); perfect complements are L-shaped (right angles).
MRS and the optimum
  • $MRS = $ size of the slope $= \dfrac{\Delta Y}{\Delta X}$ along the curve $= \dfrac{MU_X}{MU_Y}$: the amount of $Y$ you would give up for one more $X$. On the practice exam figure, A to B is steeper than B to C, so the MRS is greater between A and B. Points on different curves (E vs B, B vs D) are not an MRS comparison at all.
  • Optimum: the highest curve the budget line touches, where the curve is tangent: $MRS = P_X/P_Y$, equivalently $MU_X/P_X = MU_Y/P_Y$ (the last dollar on each good buys the same utility).
Income and substitution effects

Substitution effect: movement along the original indifference curve to the new slope. Income effect: the jump to a new curve. For a price fall every arrow flips. Applications in the chapter: labor supply (a higher wage can lower hours worked if the income effect dominates, giving a backward-bending supply curve) and saving (a higher interest rate has an ambiguous effect on saving for the same reason).

Play: how many workers to hire?

A bakery sells cakes at \$10 each. Each extra baker adds fewer cakes than the last (diminishing MPL). Slide the wage and the price: the firm hires every worker whose $VMPL = P \times MPL$ is at least the wage.

B. Labor markets, wages and discrimination

Demand for labor is derived demand
  • Firms want workers for what they produce. Marginal product of labor (MPL): extra output from one more worker; it falls as more are hired (diminishing marginal product). Value of the marginal product $VMPL = P \times MPL$.
  • Hiring rule: hire until $W = VMPL$. The VMPL curve is the firm's labor demand curve. A higher output price or better technology shifts it right; this is the same decision as $P = MC$ seen from the input side.
  • Labor supply slopes up (a higher wage pulls in more hours and more workers); equilibrium wage $=$ VMPL of the last worker hired. Other inputs (land, capital) are paid their marginal products the same way.
Why wages differ
  • Human capital: education, training, experience raise MPL, so they raise wages (the "more experience, holding education constant" finding). Taking a low-paid trainee job is an investment: opportunity cost now, higher VMPL later.
  • Compensating differentials: unpleasant or risky jobs pay more to attract workers. Ability, effort, chance also matter. Signaling: the alternative view of education, that a degree signals innate ability rather than building skill; either way the wage rises with schooling.
  • Minimum wage and teens: teens have fewer skills and less experience, so lower MPL and a lower market wage; one minimum wage therefore sits further above the teen equilibrium than the adult one and causes more teen unemployment. Teen labor demand is also more elastic (easy to replace with adults or machines), so the quantity drop is bigger. Hence the case for a sub-minimum wage for teens.
Discrimination
  • A wage gap between groups is evidence of discrimination only after controlling for human capital, hours, effort and job type. If one group is "less educated in the field of hardware", lower pay reflects lower VMPL, which weakens the complaint. If the lower-paid group were more productive, that would strengthen it.
  • Competition erodes employer discrimination: a profit-maximizing firm that hires the underpaid, equally productive group gets the same output cheaper. As such firms expand and bid for those workers, the gap shrinks. Discrimination survives when it comes from customers or from government rules, which competition cannot undo.

Common exam traps. (1) Comparing MRS between points on different indifference curves: MRS is a slope along one curve. (2) Saying the MRS is constant along a curve "because utility is constant": utility is constant, the slope is not. (3) Thinking the price increase of a normal good has conflicting effects: both effects reduce consumption; only inferior goods conflict. (4) Mixing up MPL (units of output) with VMPL (dollars): the wage equals the dollar version. (5) Reading "farm girls are more productive" as weakening a discrimination complaint: it strengthens it, because they are paid less despite higher VMPL.

Exam framing. "Why would a single minimum wage distort the teen labor market more than the adult one?" Full-credit answer: wages equal $P \times MPL$; teens have less skill and experience so their MPL and equilibrium wage are lower; the same minimum wage is therefore a bigger step above the teen equilibrium (more surplus labor), and because teen labor demand is more elastic, employers cut more teen hours per dollar of minimum wage. "Which bundle does the consumer prefer?" Full-credit answer: the one on the highest indifference curve; bundles on the same curve are equally good; the chosen one is where the budget line is tangent, $MRS = P_X/P_Y$.

MC 1. The potato chip tax

Tax wedge · DWL · revenue

A \$0.25 tax per bag decreases producer surplus by \$600 per day, generates \$1,220 of revenue per day, and decreases equilibrium quantity by 120 bags per day. Which is true? (a) CS falls by \$645 (b) quantity falls from 6,000 to 5,880 (c) total surplus falls from \$3,000 to \$1,800 (d) DWL is \$15 per day.

(d). List the givens: $T = 0.25$, revenue $= 1{,}220$, $\Delta Q = 120$, $\Delta PS = -600$. DWL $= \tfrac12 \times T \times \Delta Q = \tfrac12 \times 0.25 \times 120 = 15$. Done. To rule out (b): revenue $= T \times Q_T$, so $Q_T = 1{,}220 / 0.25 = 4{,}880$ and $Q^* = 4{,}880 + 120 = 5{,}000$, not 6,000. For (a) and (c): the PS loss is a trapezoid, $600 = (P^* - P_S)(4{,}880 + 60)$, so the producer burden is about \$0.121 and the consumer burden about \$0.129; CS falls by about \$635, not \$645, and total surplus (counting revenue) falls by only the \$15 DWL.

MC 2. MRS on the cake-and-donut figure

Indifference curves · MRS

A, B, C lie on $I_2$ with A high and left, C low and right. Which MRS comparison is correct?

(a) The MRS is greater between A and B than between B and C. MRS is the slope of the indifference curve, and the curve is steepest at the top left where cake is plentiful and donuts scarce. Moving from A to B you give up a lot of cake per donut; from B to C much less (diminishing MRS). (c) is the classic trap: being on the same curve means equal utility, not equal slope. (d) compares points on different curves, which is not an MRS statement.

MC 3. Monopoly profit from a table

Monopoly · profit = (P − ATC) × Q

Price / quantity / ATC rows: (24, 0, none), (18, 5, 14.00), (12, 10, 11.00), (6, 15, 10.67), (0, 20, 11.00). Maximum profit?

(b) \$20. Compute $(P - ATC) \times Q$ for every row: $Q = 5$: $(18 - 14)(5) = 20$; $Q = 10$: $(12 - 11)(10) = 10$; $Q = 15$: $(6 - 10.67)(15) = -70$; $Q = 20$: $(0 - 11)(20) = -220$. Pick the biggest. You found the right formula at $Q = 10$ in our session; the fix was doing it for every row instead of the one that looked best.

MC 4. Which statement about revenue is correct?

MR vs AR vs P

(a) For all firms MR $=$ P. (b) Only for competitive firms does AR $=$ P. (c) MR $=$ TR $\div$ Q. (d) Only for competitive firms does AR $=$ MR.

(d). $AR = TR/Q = P$ for every firm, so (b) is wrong because of the word "only" and (c) is wrong because $TR/Q$ is average, not marginal, revenue. $MR = \Delta TR / \Delta Q$ equals $P$ only when the firm's demand is horizontal, so (a) is wrong and (d) is the one relationship that is unique to competition.

MC 5. The Springfield park vote

Public goods · voting vs efficiency

Cost \$24 per acre, split equally. WTP per acre (Santiago, Amara, Connor): acre 1 (10, 24, 6), acre 2 (8, 18, 5), acre 3 (6, 14, 4), acre 4 (3, 8, 3), acre 5 (1, 6, 2). Largest park a majority votes yes for?

(c) 2 acres. Each resident's share is $24/3 = 8$ per acre and votes yes while own WTP $\ge 8$. Santiago: acres 1, 2. Amara: acres 1 to 4. Connor: never. Two of three say yes for acres 1 and 2 only. The efficient size is different: summed WTP is $40, 31, 24, 14$, so acre 3 ($24 \ge 24$) is still worth building and the efficient park is 3 acres. Voting underbuilds here because it ignores how much Amara cares.

MC 6. The gasoline externality

Externality · private vs social cost

The 300th gallon has private cost \$3.07, social cost \$3.33 and value to consumers \$3.48. Equilibrium quantity is 330. The equilibrium price could be: (a) 3.74 (b) 2.91 (c) 3.12 (d) 2.67.

(c) \$3.12. Equilibrium is where demand meets private cost (supply) at $Q = 330$. Demand slopes down, so at 330 the value is below 3.48; supply slopes up, so at 330 the private cost is above 3.07. The price is between 3.07 and 3.48, and only 3.12 is. Bonus facts: the external cost is $3.33 - 3.07 = 0.26$ per gallon, and because the market ignores it, 330 is more than the efficient quantity.

MC 7. Price of a normal good rises

Income & substitution effects

Which effects push which way?

(b) Both effects encourage buying less. Substitution: the good is relatively more expensive, so swap away from it. Income: the price rise makes you poorer, and for a normal good less income means less of it. They only conflict for inferior goods.

MC 8. Shape of MC from a cost table

Cost curves · MC from differences

FC $= 20$ at every output; VC $= 0, 10, 40, 80, 130, 200, 300$ for $Q = 0$ to $6$. Shape of MC?

(b) Upward-sloping. MC is the difference between consecutive VC rows: $10, 30, 40, 50, 70, 100$. Every step is bigger than the last and none falls, so it is rising throughout, not constant and not U-shaped (a U needs a falling section). Fixed cost is irrelevant to MC.

MC 9. Spade's Hardware and the discrimination complaint

Wages = P × MPL · discrimination

Which statement would weaken the complaint that the store discriminates against farm girls?

(c) Farm girls are generally less educated in the field of hardware. Lower human capital means lower MPL, so a lower wage is what a profit maximizer would pay anyone with that productivity: the gap is explained without discrimination. (a), (b) and (d) all say farm girls are more productive, which would make lower pay look worse, strengthening the complaint.

MC 10. Demand rises in a competitive market (Figure 15-7)

Perfect competition · entry · long run

Market starts in long-run equilibrium at W. Demand shifts from $D_0$ to $D_1$. Result?

(b) An eventual increase in the number of firms and a new long-run equilibrium at Z. X is the short-run stop: price rises to $P_X$, existing firms move up MC to $q_1$ and earn profit. Profit attracts entry, supply shifts to $S_1$, price returns to $P_W = \min ATC$, firms go back to $q_0$ with zero profit, and the market quantity is larger only because there are more firms. (c) and (d) have falling profits with the wrong price story: in the short run profits rise; they fall back only as prices fall with entry.

FR 1. The monopolist's graph

Monopoly · four-step solution

Demand hits \$20 at $Q = 0$ and \$14 at $Q = 6$; MR hits zero at $Q = 10$; MC starts at 2 and passes 8 at $Q = 6$ and 11 at $Q = 9$; ATC $= 10$ at $Q = 6$. Find price and quantity, profit, DWL, and the efficient quantity.

Write the curves: $D{:}\ P = 20 - Q$, $MR = 20 - 2Q$, $MC = 2 + Q$.

(a) $MR = MC$: $20 - 2Q = 2 + Q \Rightarrow 3Q = 18 \Rightarrow Q_m = 6$. Price from demand: $P_m = 20 - 6 = 14$.

(b) Profit $= (P_m - ATC) \times Q_m = (14 - 10) \times 6 = 24$. On the graph: the rectangle between 14 and 10, from 0 to 6.

(c) Efficient quantity first: $D = MC$: $20 - Q = 2 + Q \Rightarrow Q_{eff} = 9$ (price 11). DWL is the triangle between demand and MC from $Q = 6$ to $9$: at $Q = 6$ the gap is $14 - 8 = 6$, at $Q = 9$ it is 0, so $DWL = \tfrac12 \times 3 \times 6 = 9$.

(d) Socially efficient quantity $= 9$, where $P = MC$.

FR 2. The negative externality graph

Externality · Pigouvian tax · CS, PS, GR, DWL

Demand from 20 down through (6, 14) and (8, 12); MPC from 4 up through (6, 10) and (8, 12); MSC from 8 up through (6, 14) and (8, 16).

Write the curves: $D{:}\ P = 20 - Q$, $MPC = 4 + Q$, $MSC = 8 + Q$ (external cost $= 4$ per unit).

(a) Market: $D = MPC$: $20 - Q = 4 + Q \Rightarrow Q = 8$, $P = 12$.

(b) $CS = \tfrac12 \times 8 \times (20 - 12) = 32$. $PS = \tfrac12 \times 8 \times (12 - 4) = 32$. Efficient $Q$: $D = MSC$: $20 - Q = 8 + Q \Rightarrow Q = 6$. DWL is the triangle between MSC and D from $Q = 6$ to $8$: gap at 8 is $16 - 12 = 4$, so $DWL = \tfrac12 \times 2 \times 4 = 4$.

(c) Pigouvian tax $= MSC - MPC = 4$ per unit (measured at $Q = 6$: $14 - 10$).

(d) With the tax, sellers' effective cost curve is $MPC + 4 = MSC$. $Q = 6$; buyers pay $P_B = 14$; sellers keep $P_S = 10$. $CS = \tfrac12 \times 6 \times (20 - 14) = 18$. $PS = \tfrac12 \times 6 \times (10 - 4) = 18$. $GR = 4 \times 6 = 24$. $DWL = 0$ because quantity is now efficient. Check: total surplus before $= 32 + 32 - 32$ (external cost $4 \times 8$) $= 32$; after $= 18 + 18 + 24 - 24 = 36$; the gain of 4 is exactly the DWL removed.

Homework recap from our session

Opportunity cost · human capital · VMPL · discrimination

  • Unpaid or low-paid student jobs: the opportunity cost is the wage from another job; students take them for on-the-job training (human capital), which raises their future wage. Expected effect on later earnings: higher.
  • Two-tier minimum wage: teens have fewer skills and less experience (lower MPL, so lower VMPL and wage); teen labor demand is more elastic, not less. Both reasons support a lower teen minimum.
  • Experience pays more, education held constant: experience raises MPL, so $W = P \times MPL$ rises. Same mental model.
  • "Professors could earn more elsewhere": the opportunity cost of being a professor is the forgone finance (or industry) salary; they accept it because of nonwage benefits (compensating differential in reverse).
  • Merit pay for teachers: teachers whose classes perform poorly would oppose it; being well educated does not make you a good teacher, so "most educated" is not the predictor.
  • Paying one group less for equal work is not profit maximizing in a competitive market: hiring the cheaper, equally productive group raises profit, which bids their wage up over time. Econ predicts competition erodes discrimination.
