One-Page Cheat Sheet

This sheet reviews the macro half of a principles of economics third midterm: data and causality, GDP, prices and inflation, growth, saving and the financial system, and money, banks and jobs. Consumer-choice topics (budget constraints, indifference curves, income and substitution effects) are not covered here. The pill beside each title shows how many of four past third-midterm exams tested that topic. If you can explain each row in your own words, you know the core material.

As you practice, start by identifying the question type. Then decide which definition or equation applies, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

Three types of data 3 of 4 past exams

EconCross-sectional data cover many subjects at one point in time. Time-series data follow one subject across many periods. Panel data follow many subjects across many periods.

Plain EnglishAsk two questions: how many subjects, and how many time periods? Several variables for one country over 30 years is still time-series data.

Correlation is not causation 3 of 4 past exams

EconA confounding variable is an omitted variable related to the variables being studied. Reverse causality means $y$ is causing $x$, not the other way around. An instrumental variable is correlated with the independent variable but does not directly affect the dependent variable.

Plain EnglishBefore you accept that $x$ causes $y$, check whether something else drives both and whether the arrow runs backward. Both problems are more likely with observational data than with a randomized experiment.

GDP and its four parts 4 of 4 past exams

EconGDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period: $Y = C + I + G + NX$. In a closed economy, $NX = 0$. GDP counts by where production happens; GNP counts by who produces it.

Plain EnglishGDP is the country's income for the year, because one person's purchase is another person's income. US nominal GDP is about \$30 trillion per year.

What counts in GDP 2 of 4 past exams

EconGoods are counted when they are produced. Unsold output is inventory investment. Transfer payments such as Social Security are not counted. Spending on imports raises $C$ and lowers $NX$ by the same amount.

Plain EnglishAsk: was something new produced here, this period? If firms cut production and sell from their warehouses, GDP falls.

Nominal GDP, real GDP and the deflator 2 of 4 past exams, 20-point problem

EconNominal GDP uses each year's own prices. Real GDP uses base-year prices for each year, so it changes only when output changes. GDP deflator $= \dfrac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{real GDP}} \times 100$.

Plain EnglishReal GDP tracks how much was made. The deflator tracks prices. A quick check: the deflator equals 100 in the base year.

A price level is not inflation 4 of 4 past exams

EconThe GDP deflator and the CPI are price levels. The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price level: $\dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$. A negative result is deflation.

Plain EnglishFind the index for each year first, then take the percent change. Divide by the starting value: an index that goes from 125 to 150 rose 20%, not 25%.

The CPI: a fixed basket 4 of 4 past exams

EconThe consumer price index prices a fixed basket of goods bought by a typical consumer. The quantities stay the same from year to year; only the prices change. The CPI is the more commonly reported gauge of inflation, and it can convert dollar amounts from one year into another year's dollars.

Plain EnglishSame shopping cart, new price tags. If one good's price rises by less than the CPI, that good became cheaper compared with other goods, so people likely buy more of it.

Real and nominal interest rates 1 of 4 past exams

EconReal interest rate $=$ nominal interest rate $-$ inflation rate.

Plain EnglishThe nominal rate is the number the bank posts. The real rate is how much more you can buy. If the CPI goes from 200 to 214, inflation is 7%, so a 5% nominal rate is a real rate of $-2\%$.

Productivity and growth 4 of 4 past exams

EconProductivity is the quantity of goods and services produced from each unit of labor input. It determines living standards, which are usually measured by real GDP per person. With constant returns to scale, doubling all inputs doubles output and leaves productivity unchanged.

Plain EnglishA country is rich when each worker produces a lot. Growth in real GDP per person $\approx$ nominal GDP growth $-$ inflation $-$ population growth.

Saving and investment 3 of 4 past exams

EconPrivate saving $= Y - T - C$. Public saving $= T - G$. National saving $= Y - C - G$. In a closed economy, national saving equals investment: $S = I$.

Plain EnglishTaxes move saving from households to the government, so they cancel out of national saving. Write the three equations, plug in what you are given, and solve.

Stocks, bonds and loanable funds 3 of 4 past exams

EconA bond is debt; a share of stock is ownership. A bond pays a higher interest rate when its term is longer or its issuer is a greater credit risk. A mutual fund sells shares to the public and buys a mix of stocks and bonds. Saving supplies loanable funds, investment demands them, and the real interest rate balances the two.

Plain EnglishFirms raise money by selling stock or by selling bonds. Someone whose project earns more than the interest rate borrows; someone whose project earns less lends.

Banks, the Fed and the money supply 2 of 4 past exams

EconRequired reserves $= R \times$ deposits, and the bank can lend the rest. Money multiplier $= \dfrac{1}{R}$. When the Fed buys bonds, the money supply rises and the federal funds rate falls. When the Fed sells bonds, the reverse happens.

Plain EnglishDeposit \$100 at a 10% reserve ratio: the bank keeps \$10 and lends \$90. That \$90 is deposited and lent again, so \$100 of new reserves can support up to \$1,000 of deposits.

Who is in the labor force 1 of 4 past exams

EconLabor force $=$ employed $+$ unemployed. Employed includes part-time and self-employed workers and people temporarily absent from a job. Unemployed means no job and a job search in the past four weeks, or waiting to be recalled from a layoff.

Plain EnglishRetirees, full-time students and stay-at-home parents who are not searching are outside the labor force. They are not counted as unemployed.

Numbers to know.

  • US nominal GDP: about \$30 trillion per year. Past exams have used \$30 trillion and \$32 trillion. GDP is a flow (an amount per year).
  • US government debt held by the public: about \$30 trillion, close to one year of GDP. Debt is a stock (a total at one moment).
  • GDP per person: roughly \$90,000 per year, which is \$30 trillion divided by about 340 million people.
  • The GDP deflator and the CPI both equal 100 in the base year.
  • Growth of 2% per year doubles living standards in about 35 years.
  • A reserve ratio of 10% gives a money multiplier of 10.
  • To be counted as unemployed, a person without a job must have searched in the past 4 weeks or be waiting for recall from a layoff.

A picture that helps: a cross-section is like slicing a tree trunk once and looking at all the rings at that moment. A time series follows one thing as it changes. Panel data are several time series stacked together.

Experimental and observational data 1 of 4 past exams

Experimental data

The researcher assigns who gets the treatment. In a randomized controlled trial, subjects are placed in the treatment group or the control group at random, so the two groups differ only in the treatment.

Observational data

The researcher records what happened without assigning anything. Most economic data are observational, and causality problems such as reverse causality are more likely here.

Three causality terms 3 of 4 past exams

The independent variable ($x$) is the proposed cause. The dependent variable ($y$) is the outcome. Two variables that move together are correlated, and correlation alone does not show that $x$ causes $y$.

Confounding variable

An omitted variable that is related to the variables being measured and studied. Example: ice cream sales and sunburns rise together, but hot weather drives both. Leave out the weather and it looks as if ice cream causes sunburn.

Reverse causality

A researcher believes that one variable influences a second variable, when in fact the second variable influences the first. Example: cities with more police officers have more crime. High crime may be the reason a city hires more police.

Instrumental variable

A variable that is correlated with the independent variable but does not directly affect the dependent variable. Researchers use one to isolate a causal effect in observational data. Example: to study how schooling affects wages, a researcher might use distance to the nearest college, which changes how much schooling people get but should not change wages directly.

One more term appears among the answer choices: a dummy variable takes the value 0 or 1 to mark a category, such as 1 for employed and 0 for not employed. It is not a causality problem.

A common mistake to watch for: a dataset with several variables (GDP, inflation and population) can look cross-sectional. Check the subjects and the time periods, not the number of variables. One country over many years is time-series data.

Exam framing. These questions usually give a definition and ask for the term. Match the key phrase:

  • "Omitted variable related to the variables being studied": confounding variable.
  • "The second variable influences the first": reverse causality.
  • "Correlated with the independent variable, no direct effect on the dependent variable": instrumental variable.
  • "Multiple subjects at a given time": cross-sectional data.

What GDP measures 4 of 4 past exams

Gross domestic product is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period. Because one person's spending is another person's income, GDP is also the economy's total income, written $Y$.

$Y = C + I + G + NX$

$C$: consumption

Household spending on goods and services. This is the largest part of GDP.

$I$: investment

Business spending on equipment and buildings, new housing, and changes in inventories. It does not mean buying stocks or bonds.

$G$: government purchases

Government spending on goods and services, including the pay of government workers. Transfer payments are not part of $G$.

$NX$: net exports

Exports minus imports. In a closed economy there is no trade, so $NX = 0$ and $Y = C + I + G$. Many exam problems say "this country does not trade" so that you can drop $NX$.

GDP and GNP 1 of 4 past exams

GDP: where

Counts production inside the country's borders, whoever does the producing.

GNP: who

Gross national product counts production by the country's citizens, wherever they work.

Example: a Canadian citizen who works in Ohio adds to US GDP and to Canadian GNP. She does not add to US GNP.

Worked example. A household receives a \$900 Social Security check. It spends \$250 on a jacket made abroad by a foreign firm, \$500 on a phone made abroad, and \$150 at a local diner. By how much does US GDP rise?

The check is a transfer, so it adds nothing. The jacket and phone are imports, so they add nothing. Only the \$150 of domestic production counts. GDP rises by \$150.

Inventories. Suppose firms cut production this quarter but keep selling by drawing down their warehouses. Production is lower, so GDP falls. The sales from inventory do not offset the drop, because those goods were counted when they were made.

Nominal GDP, real GDP and the deflator 2 of 4 past exams, 20-point problem

Nominal GDP

This year's prices times this year's quantities. It can rise because output rose, because prices rose, or both.

Real GDP

Base-year prices times this year's quantities. Prices are held fixed, so real GDP changes only when output changes. Use it to measure growth and economic well-being.

GDP deflator

$\dfrac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{real GDP}} \times 100$. It is a price level. In the base year, nominal and real GDP are equal, so the deflator is 100.

Flow or stock? 2 of 4 past exams

Flow: an amount per period

GDP, income, saving in a year and the budget deficit are flows. US nominal GDP is about \$30 trillion per year.

Stock: a total at one moment

Government debt, wealth and the capital stock are stocks. US debt held by the public is about \$30 trillion, with no "per year" attached.

The "public" that holds the debt is anyone who owns US Treasury securities: households, firms and foreign governments. On a multiple-choice question about size, check two things: the order of magnitude (trillions, not billions) and whether the answer says "per year."

Common mistakes and what to check:

  • Real GDP uses base-year prices for each year, including later years. If your real GDP column uses each year's own prices, it is the nominal column again.
  • The deflator is a price level, not the inflation rate. Inflation is the percentage change in the deflator from one year to the next.
  • For a percentage change, divide by the old value. From 120 to 180 is $60 \div 120 = 50\%$, not $60 \div 180$.

Exam framing. A full-credit answer to the GDP problem shows the formula, the numbers plugged in, and the result for each year. If the economy has only one good, you can check your work with a shortcut: inflation is the percentage change in that good's price, and real growth is the percentage change in its quantity. In the table above, the price goes from \$6 to \$9 (50%) and the quantity from 12 to 15 (25%), which matches.

Two routes to the inflation rate. The GDP deflator and the CPI are both price levels. Inflation is the percentage change in either one.

How the CPI is built 4 of 4 past exams

The consumer price index measures the overall cost of the goods and services bought by a typical consumer.

  1. Fix the basket. Decide which goods the typical consumer buys and how many of each. These quantities stay the same in each year.
  2. Find the prices of each good in each year.
  3. Compute the cost of the basket in each year.
  4. Choose a base year and compute the index: divide the cost of the basket this year by its cost in the base year, then multiply by 100. $$\text{CPI} = \frac{\text{cost this year}}{\text{cost in base year}} \times 100$$
  5. Compute the inflation rate: the percentage change in the CPI from the year before.

The assumption to remember: the number of each good bought by the typical consumer does not change from year to year. Prices can change; the basket does not.

The two measures usually move closely together. One centers on consumer prices, the other on prices across the whole economy.

Inflation and deflation from an index 1 of 4 past exams

A price index is 80 in year 1, 100 in year 2 and 90 in year 3.

$$\text{Year 1 to 2: } \frac{100 - 80}{80} \times 100 = 25\%$$ $$\text{Year 2 to 3: } \frac{90 - 100}{100} \times 100 = -10\%$$

A negative inflation rate is deflation: 10% deflation from year 2 to year 3. Notice that the first answer is 25%, not 20%. The index rose by 20 points, and 20 points on a starting value of 80 is 25%.

Real and nominal interest rates 1 of 4 past exams

The nominal interest rate is the rate as usually reported: how fast the number of dollars in your account grows. The real interest rate corrects for inflation: how fast your purchasing power grows.

real interest rate $=$ nominal interest rate $-$ inflation rate

Givens. CPI in year 1 $= 200$. CPI in year 2 $= 214$. Nominal interest rate $= 5\%$.

$$\text{inflation} = \frac{214 - 200}{200} \times 100 = 7\%$$ $$\text{real rate} = 5\% - 7\% = -2\%$$

This is a two-step question: find the inflation rate from the CPI first, then subtract. A negative real rate means prices rose faster than the account grew.

Did a good become cheaper or more expensive? 2 of 4 past exams

Compare the percentage change in the good's price with the percentage change in the CPI.

Givens. A haircut goes from \$20 to \$25. A pair of jeans goes from \$40 to \$50. The CPI goes from 100 to 150.

Haircut: $\dfrac{25 - 20}{20} = 25\%$. Jeans: $\dfrac{50 - 40}{40} = 25\%$. CPI: $\dfrac{150 - 100}{100} = 50\%$.

Both prices rose by less than prices in general. Compared with other goods, haircuts and jeans became cheaper, so people likely buy more of both.

Comparing dollars across time 1 of 4 past exams

The CPI can convert a dollar figure from an earlier year into today's dollars:

$$\text{today's dollars} = \text{year } T \text{ dollars} \times \frac{\text{CPI today}}{\text{CPI in year } T}$$

Example: a \$20,000 salary in a year when the CPI was 50 is worth $20{,}000 \times \dfrac{300}{50} = 120{,}000$ dollars when the CPI is 300.

Common mistakes and what to check:

  • Subtracting index points is not the inflation rate. From 200 to 214 is 14 points, and $14 \div 200 = 7\%$.
  • A price that rises can still be a relatively cheaper good. Compare it with the CPI before deciding what people buy.
  • Both the CPI and the GDP deflator can measure inflation. A statement that only one of them can is false.

Exam framing. When a question gives index numbers, your first step is usually the percentage change. When it gives a nominal interest rate and two CPI values, find inflation, then subtract. When it asks which statement about the CPI and the deflator is correct, test each choice against the side-by-side table above.

Productivity and living standards 4 of 4 past exams

Productivity is the quantity of goods and services produced from each unit of labor input. A country's standard of living is usually measured by real GDP per person (GDP per capita): real GDP divided by the population.

Productivity plays the key role in living standards for both individuals and nations. A worker who produces more per hour earns more, and a nation whose workers produce more per hour has more goods and services per person.

What raises productivity not on the 4 past exams

Physical capital per worker (tools and machines), human capital per worker (education and skills), natural resources per worker, and technological knowledge. These are the inputs in the production function $Y = A\,F(L, K, H, N)$.

Returns to scale 1 of 4 past exams

Returns to scale describe what happens to output when all inputs change by the same proportion.

With constant returns, output becomes $2Y$ and labor becomes $2L$, so productivity is $\dfrac{2Y}{2L} = \dfrac{Y}{L}$: the same as before.

Two growth scenarios to know

Scenario A: more retirees, fewer workers 2 of 4 past exams

Over a short time, the number of retired people rises and the number of people working falls. Machines and technology do not change that quickly, so each remaining worker has more capital to work with. Productivity rises. Total output still falls, because fewer people are working, and the population is about the same. Real GDP per person falls.

Scenario B: how much did real GDP per person grow? 1 of 4 past exams

Nominal GDP can grow because of higher prices and more people, not only more output per person. For this course, subtract:

growth in real GDP per person $\approx$ nominal GDP growth $-$ inflation $-$ population growth

Givens. Nominal GDP grew 10%. The GDP deflator rose 5%. Population grew 3%.

$$10\% - 5\% - 3\% = 2\%$$

Real GDP per person grew, but by much less than the 10% headline number. The exact calculation, $\dfrac{1.10}{1.05 \times 1.03} - 1$, gives about 1.7%, so the subtraction is a close approximation.

Why small growth rates matter not on the 4 past exams

Rule of 70: a quantity growing at $g\%$ per year doubles in about $70 \div g$ years. At 2% per year, real GDP per person doubles in about 35 years. For a developed economy, 2% real growth per year is healthy.

The saving equations 3 of 4 past exams

Key. $Y$ is income (GDP). $C$ is consumption. $T$ is taxes. $G$ is government purchases. $I$ is investment. $S$ is national saving.

Private saving $= Y - T - C$

What households have left after paying taxes and buying consumption goods.

Public saving $= T - G$

What the government has left after its purchases. A positive number is a budget surplus. A negative number is a budget deficit.

National saving $= Y - C - G$

Private saving plus public saving. The $T$ terms cancel: $(Y - T - C) + (T - G)$ $= Y - C - G$.

Why saving equals investment in a closed economy. Start from $Y = C + I + G$ and subtract $C$ and $G$ from both sides:

$$Y - C - G = I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = I$$

This is the result that most saving problems on past exams rely on. Once you know national saving, you know investment.

The financial system 2 of 4 past exams

The financial system moves funds from savers to borrowers. Firms can raise funds in two ways.

BondStock

So a large company that wants funds without selling shares of stock can sell bonds instead.

What makes a bond's interest rate higher or lower

  • Term. A longer-term bond usually pays a higher rate, because the holder waits longer and bears more risk.
  • Credit risk. A borrower more likely to default pays a higher rate. A firm in sound financial condition pays a lower rate.

For a financially sound firm selling a long-term bond, the sound finances push the rate lower and the long term pushes it higher.

A mutual fund is an institution that sells shares to the public and uses the proceeds to buy a selection of stocks and bonds. It lets a person with a small amount of money hold a diversified portfolio. Banks and mutual funds are both financial intermediaries.

The market for loanable funds 1 of 4 past exams, 20-point problem

Supply: saving

People who save supply funds. A higher interest rate makes lending more attractive, so the supply curve slopes up.

Demand: investment

People and firms with investment projects demand funds. A higher interest rate makes borrowing more costly, so the demand curve slopes down.

The price in this market is the real interest rate. It adjusts until the quantity of funds supplied equals the quantity demanded.

Borrow or lend? Compare the return on your own project with the interest rate $r$. If the project earns more than $r$, borrowing to invest is worthwhile. If it earns less than $r$, lending earns more than the project would. Both sides gain from the trade.

not on the 4 past exams A larger government budget deficit lowers public saving, so national saving falls. The supply of loanable funds shifts left, the real interest rate rises, and investment falls.

Common mistakes and what to check:

  • Taxes appear in private saving and in public saving, but not in national saving. If your national saving formula contains $T$, recheck it.
  • With fewer workers and the same machines, productivity and real GDP per person move in opposite directions. Productivity is output per worker; GDP per person divides by the whole population.
  • Constant returns to scale means output doubles and productivity stays the same. It does not mean productivity rises.

Exam framing. For a saving problem, write the givens, then the three equations, then substitute. A typical chain: use private saving to find $C$, use public saving to find $G$, add the two kinds of saving to get $S$, and set $I = S$. Finish with a check that $C + I + G$ adds up to $Y$.

How a bank handles a deposit 2 of 4 past exams

Reserves

Deposits that a bank has received but has not loaned out.

Reserve requirement

The minimum fraction of deposits that a bank must hold as reserves, written $R$. Required reserves $= R \times \text{deposits}$. Anything held above that is excess reserves.

Loans

A bank that wants no excess reserves lends out the rest: $\text{loans} = (1 - R) \times \text{deposits}$.

Example 1: a new deposit. The reserve requirement is 8%, the bank wants no excess reserves, and it receives a new deposit of \$500.

$$\text{required reserves rise by } 0.08 \times 500 = 40$$ $$\text{new loans} = 500 - 40 = 460$$

The bank must add \$40 to required reserves and can lend \$460. It cannot lend the whole \$500.

Example 2: work backward from loans. A bank has a 20% reserve requirement, \$8,000 in loans, and has loaned out all it can. How large are its deposits?

Key. $D$ is deposits. Loans are the part of deposits not held as reserves, so $\text{loans} = (1 - 0.20) \times D$.

$$0.80 \times D = 8{,}000 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D = \frac{8{,}000}{0.80} = 10{,}000$$

Check: reserves are $0.20 \times 10{,}000 = 2{,}000$, and $2{,}000 + 8{,}000 = 10{,}000$.

The money multiplier not on the 4 past exams

When a bank lends \$90 of a \$100 deposit, the borrower spends it and the \$90 lands in another bank, which keeps \$9 and lends \$81, and so on. Add up all the deposits and you get a simple result:

$$\text{money multiplier} = \frac{1}{R}$$ $$\text{total deposits} = \text{new reserves} \times \frac{1}{R}$$

With $R = 10\%$, the multiplier is $\dfrac{1}{0.10} = 10$, so \$100 of new reserves can support up to \$1,000 of deposits. This assumes banks lend all of their excess reserves and each loan is deposited again.

A real-world note: in the United States the Fed set reserve requirements to 0% in March 2020. Exam problems still use a positive ratio such as 10%, because the model shows how bank lending adds to the money supply.

The Fed and the federal funds rate 1 of 4 past exams

The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the central bank of the United States. The federal funds rate is the interest rate banks charge one another for short-term loans of reserves. The Fed steers it with open-market operations: buying and selling government bonds.

Why: when the Fed buys a bond, it pays with new reserves. With more reserves in the banking system, banks can borrow them more cheaply, so the rate falls.

Employed, unemployed, or not in the labor force?

Sort each person. Then count the labor force.

Labor force definitions 1 of 4 past exams

Employed

Worked for pay, including part-time work and self-employment. Someone who has a job but was absent because of illness or vacation is still employed.

Unemployed

Has no job, is available for work, and looked for work in the past four weeks. Someone laid off and waiting to be recalled also counts as unemployed.

Not in the labor force

Neither of the above: retirees, full-time students, stay-at-home parents and anyone else who has not searched recently.

labor force $=$ employed $+$ unemployed

not on the 4 past exams Unemployment rate $= \dfrac{\text{unemployed}}{\text{labor force}} \times 100$. Labor-force participation rate $= \dfrac{\text{labor force}}{\text{adult population}} \times 100$.

Common mistakes and what to check:

  • To get deposits from loans, divide by $(1 - R)$. Multiplying loans by $(1 + R)$ gives a number that looks close but is not correct.
  • Buying bonds puts money into the banking system; selling bonds takes it out. If you mix up the direction, ask who ends up holding the cash.
  • A person who is not working is not automatically unemployed. Check for a job search in the past four weeks or a temporary layoff. A part-time worker who wants full-time work is employed.

Exam framing. For a labor-force count, go down the list and label each person E, U or N, then add the E's and U's. For a bank question, write required reserves $= R \times D$ first; most answer choices are that number or a number built from it. For a Fed question, decide which way the rate needs to move, then choose the action from the table.

1. A two-good economy: bread and jam

Nominal GDP · real GDP · deflator · percentage change

2025\$3150\$675

(a) Compute nominal GDP, real GDP and the GDP deflator for each year, using 2023 as the base year. (b) Compute the percentage change in each from the year before, and name the variable that does not change in each year. (c) Did economic well-being rise more in 2024 or in 2025?

Key. Nominal GDP: for each good, multiply this year's price by this year's quantity, then add. Real GDP: for each good, multiply the 2023 price by this year's quantity, then add. Deflator $= \dfrac{\text{nominal}}{\text{real}} \times 100$.

(a)

YearNominal GDPReal GDP (2023 prices)Deflator
2023$2(100) + 4(50) = 400$$2(100) + 4(50) = 400$$\frac{400}{400} \times 100 = 100$

(b) From 2023 to 2024: nominal GDP $\dfrac{600 - 400}{400} = 50\%$, real GDP $50\%$, deflator $0\%$. The deflator does not change, because prices did not change; only quantities rose.

2. Saving and investment in a closed economy

Private, public and national saving · S = I

Suppose GDP is \$10 trillion, taxes are \$2 trillion, private saving is \$1 trillion and public saving is \$0.5 trillion. The economy is closed. Calculate consumption, government purchases, national saving and investment.

Givens (in trillions of dollars). $Y = 10$. $T = 2$. Private saving $= 1$. Public saving $= 0.5$. Closed economy, so $NX = 0$.

Consumption. Private saving $= Y - T - C$, so $1 = 10 - 2 - C$ and $C = 7$.

Government purchases. Public saving $= T - G$, so $0.5 = 2 - G$ and $G = 1.5$.

National saving. $S = \text{private} + \text{public} = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5$. The other route agrees: $S = Y - C - G = 10 - 7 - 1.5 = 1.5$.

Investment. In a closed economy $I = S = 1.5$.

Check. $C + I + G = 7 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 10 = Y$.

3. Three students and a market for loanable funds

Loanable funds · borrow or lend · equilibrium interest rate

Maya, Leo and Sam have each saved \$1,000. Each has an investment project that can take up to \$2,000. The projects pay these returns after one year: Maya 4%, Leo 10%, Sam 25%.

(a) If borrowing and lending are not allowed, how much does each student have a year later? (b) Now the students can borrow and lend among themselves at interest rate $r$. What decides whether a student borrows or lends? (c) What are the quantities of loanable funds supplied and demanded at $r = 6\%$? At $r = 12\%$? (d) What is the equilibrium interest rate, and who borrows and who lends? (e) How much does each student have a year later at the equilibrium rate? Who gains from the market?

(a) Each invests only their own \$1,000. Maya: $1{,}000 \times 1.04 = 1{,}040$. Leo: $1{,}000 \times 1.10 = 1{,}100$. Sam: $1{,}000 \times 1.25 = 1{,}250$.

(b) Each student compares the project's return with $r$. If the return is above $r$, the student wants to borrow and invest more. If the return is below $r$, the student earns more by lending.

(c) At $r = 6\%$: Maya (4%) lends her \$1,000, so the quantity supplied is \$1,000. Leo (10%) and Sam (25%) each want to borrow \$1,000 to reach \$2,000, so the quantity demanded is \$2,000. There is a shortage of funds.

At $r = 12\%$: Maya and Leo both lend, so the quantity supplied is \$2,000. Only Sam borrows, so the quantity demanded is \$1,000. There is a surplus of funds.

(d) The equilibrium rate is $r = 10\%$. Leo's project earns the same as the interest rate, so he is content to neither borrow nor lend. Maya lends \$1,000 and Sam borrows \$1,000, so supply equals demand.

(e) Maya lends at 10%: $1{,}000 \times 1.10 = 1{,}100$, up from \$1,040. Leo: \$1,100, the same as before. Sam invests \$2,000 at 25%, which returns $2{,}000 \times 1.25 = 2{,}500$. He repays his loan with interest, $1{,}000 \times 1.10 = 1{,}100$, and keeps $2{,}500 - 1{,}100 = 1{,}400$, up from \$1,250.

Both the lender (Maya) and the borrower (Sam) gain, and no one is worse off. The market moves funds to the project with the highest return.

4. A mandated benefit in the labor market

Supply and demand for labor · shifts · who is better off

A new law requires employers to give each employee a benefit, such as health insurance, that raises the cost of an employee by \$3 per hour.

(a) What does the mandate do to the demand for labor? (b) If employees value the benefit at exactly its cost, what does it do to the supply of labor? (c) If the wage adjusts freely, what happens to the wage and to employment? Are employers and employees better or worse off?

Key. The vertical axis is the cash wage. The horizontal axis is the quantity of labor. Employers are the demand side; workers are the supply side.

(a) An hour of work now costs the employer the cash wage plus \$3. To hire the same number of workers as before, employers need the cash wage to be \$3 lower. Labor demand shifts down by \$3 at each quantity.

(b) An hour of work now pays the worker the cash wage plus a benefit worth \$3. Workers will supply the same hours for a cash wage \$3 lower. Labor supply also shifts down by \$3.

Both curves shift down by the same amount, so the new intersection sits directly below the old one.

(c) The cash wage falls by \$3 and employment does not change. Employers pay $(W_1 - 3) + 3 = W_1$ per hour in total, the same as before. Employees receive a cash wage of $W_1 - 3$ plus a benefit they value at \$3, also the same as before. Neither group is better or worse off.

If you are asked about part (a) alone, with supply not shifting: the wage falls by less than \$3 and employment falls.

5. One bank, one deposit, and the Fed

Reserves · loans · money multiplier · open-market operations

A bank has \$50,000 in deposits, faces a 10% reserve requirement and holds no excess reserves. (a) How much does it hold in reserves and in loans? (b) A customer deposits \$1,000 of new money. By how much do required reserves rise, and how much more can the bank lend? (c) What is the most the \$1,000 could add to deposits across the whole banking system? (d) The federal funds rate is above the Fed's target. What can the Fed do?

Givens. Deposits $D = 50{,}000$. Reserve requirement $R = 0.10$. No excess reserves.

(a) Reserves $= R \times D = 0.10 \times 50{,}000 = 5{,}000$. Loans $= 50{,}000 - 5{,}000 = 45{,}000$.

(b) Required reserves rise by $0.10 \times 1{,}000 = 100$. The bank can lend the other \$900.

(c) Money multiplier $= \dfrac{1}{R} = \dfrac{1}{0.10} = 10$. Total deposits can rise by up to $1{,}000 \times 10 = 10{,}000$ dollars.

(d) The Fed can buy bonds. Buying bonds adds reserves to the banking system and increases the money supply, which pushes the federal funds rate down toward the target.

6. Reading a growth headline

Nominal vs real · GDP per person

A news report says nominal GDP grew 15% over five years. Over the same period the GDP deflator rose 8% and the population grew 3%. What happened to real GDP per person?

Givens. Nominal GDP growth $= 15\%$. Inflation (deflator) $= 8\%$. Population growth $= 3\%$.

Growth in real GDP per person is approximately:

$$15\% - 8\% - 3\% = 4\%$$

Real GDP per person grew, but by about 4%, far less than 15%. Most of the headline growth came from higher prices and a larger population. The exact figure, $\dfrac{1.15}{1.08 \times 1.03} - 1$, is about 3.4%.
