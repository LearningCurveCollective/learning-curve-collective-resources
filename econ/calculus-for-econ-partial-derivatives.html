Partial derivatives on one page

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative, taken one variable at a time. Every rule you already know still works. The one new idea is that the other variable is held constant while you differentiate.

The idea
MeaningWhat $z_x$ tells you: how $z$ changes when $x$ changes a little and $y$ does not change at all.
ExampleFor $z = 6x + 3y$, $z_x = 6$ and $z_y = 3$.
NotationTwo ways to write it: $z_x$ and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ mean the same thing: the partial derivative of $z$ with respect to $x$.
A question has to say which variable. "Find the partial derivative" on its own does not give you enough to go on.
The procedure
Step 1Pick the variable. Read which variable you are differentiating with respect to. The other one is held constant.
Step 2Swap in $\fz$. Replace the held variable with $\fz$ everywhere it appears. $\fz$ stands for some fixed number.
Step 3Differentiate as usual. Use the rules you already know. $\fz$ behaves like any other number.
Step 4Swap $\fz$ back. Replace $\fz$ with the variable it stood for.
Example$z = 3x^2 + 5xy - 2y^3 + 7$, with respect to $x$. Write $3x^2 + 5\fz x - 2\fz^3 + 7$. Differentiate to get $6x + 5\fz$. So $z_x = 6x + 5y$.
Does a term stay, or become 0?

Ask one question about each term: does it contain the variable I am differentiating with respect to?

NoIt becomes $0$. A term without your variable is a constant.
ExampleWith respect to $x$: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(2y^3\big) = 0$, just as $(16)' = 0$.
YesIt stays, and you differentiate it. A held variable multiplied onto your variable rides along, like a number in front.
ExampleWith respect to $x$: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(5xy\big) = 5y$, just as $(5\cdot 2\cdot x)' = 5\cdot 2$.
Second-order partials
FourDifferentiate a first partial again: $z_{xx}$, $z_{yy}$, $z_{xy}$ and $z_{yx}$.
Read as$z_{xy}$: differentiate with respect to $x$ first, then with respect to $y$.
For the functions in this sheet the two mixed partials are equal: $z_{xy} = z_{yx}$. Use that as a check.

"Holding everything else constant" is the phrase economists use all the time. A partial derivative is that phrase written as mathematics.

1

One variable at a time

So far every function has had one input. For $y = 3x^2 + 5x + 2$ there is one derivative, $y' = 6x + 5$.

Many quantities in economics depend on two inputs at once. Output depends on labour and capital. Utility depends on two goods. Write that as $z = f(x, y)$: $z$ depends on both $x$ and $y$. Here $z$ plays the role that $y$ played before.

You can only take a derivative with respect to one variable at a time. So a function of two variables has two first derivatives, called partial derivatives.

SymbolRead it asWhat is held constant
$z_x$ or $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$the partial derivative of $z$ with respect to $x$$y$
$z_y$ or $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$the partial derivative of $z$ with respect to $y$$x$

The curly $\partial$ is a notation choice. It signals that other variables are being held constant. $z_x$ and $\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ mean the same thing.

There is very little here that is new. If you can differentiate with one variable, you already know the hard part. The work is in keeping track of which variable is moving and which one is being held still.

2

What a derivative measures

A derivative answers one question: if I nudge the input a little, how does the output respond?

Take $y = 6x$, so $y' = 6$. Move $x$ from $2$ to $3$ and $y$ goes from $12$ to $18$. It rises by $6$, which is what the derivative says.

Now give the function two inputs: $z = 6x + 3y$.

  • Increase $x$ by $1$ and leave $y$ alone. $z$ rises by $6$. So $z_x = 6$.
  • Increase $y$ by $1$ and leave $x$ alone. $z$ rises by $3$. So $z_y = 3$.

Add one more term: $z = 6x + 3y + 2xy$. Now $z_x = 6 + 2y$. How much $z$ responds to $x$ depends on the value of $y$ you are holding still. Try it:

Notice two things. Each press moves only one variable, and the change in $z$ matches that partial derivative. And when you change $y$, the value of $z_x$ changes too, because $z_x = 6 + 2y$ still has $y$ in it.

Held constant does not mean gone. $y$ is not moving, but it still has a value, and that value can still matter. It follows along like a number.

3

The picture: one slice of a surface

A function of two variables is a surface. For each pair $(x, y)$ there is a height $z$.

Holding $y$ constant means you may only walk across the surface in the $x$ direction. Your path is a single curve, one slice of the surface, and $z_x$ is the slope of that slice. Holding $x$ constant gives a different slice through the same point, and $z_y$ is its slope. The dashed lines are the tangent lines to the two slices at that point.

Quick Check: Can You Apply It?

Answer each one before you open it.

(a) For $z = 4x + 9y$, what are $z_x$ and $z_y$?

$z_x = 4$ and $z_y = 9$. Each one is how much $z$ rises when that variable goes up by $1$ and the other is left alone.

(b) For $z = 4x + 9y + xy$, $x$ increases by $1$ while $y$ stays at $5$. By how much does $z$ change?

$z_x = 4 + y$. With $y$ held at $5$, that is $9$. So $z$ rises by $9$.

(c) True or false: holding $y$ constant means setting $y = 0$.

False. $y$ keeps whatever value it has. It is not changing, which is different from being zero.

3

Does a term stay, or become 0?

This is the part that takes the most getting used to. If the held variable is a constant, why does it sometimes disappear and sometimes stay?

Added on its own: becomes 0

$2\fz^3$ has no $x$ anywhere in it. It is a constant, like $16$.

$(16)' = 0$   so   $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(2\fz^3\big) = 0$

Multiplied onto your variable: stays

$5\fz x$ is a number times $x$. The number rides along.

$(5\cdot 2\cdot x)' = 5\cdot 2$   so   $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(5\fz x\big) = 5\fz$

One question sorts every term: does this term contain the variable I am differentiating with respect to? If it does not, the term becomes $0$. If it does, differentiate it, and any held variable multiplied in rides along unchanged.

Practice that one decision in the Stays or Becomes 0? tab.

4

Both variables in the same term

Worked example

For $z = x^3y^2$, find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

x

With respect to $x$, hold $y$ constant. Swap in $\fz$: $z = x^3\fz^2$. The $\fz^2$ is a number multiplied on, so it rides along: $3x^2\fz^2$. Swap back: $z_x = 3x^2y^2$.

y

With respect to $y$, hold $x$ constant. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \fz^3y^2$. Now $\fz^3$ rides along: $\fz^3\cdot 2y$. Swap back: $z_y = 2x^3y$.

Nothing happens to the held variable in either case. It is not differentiated. It is copied.

5

Once the steps feel familiar

Writing $\fz$ is the clearest way to learn the method and to show your work. With practice you can skip the swap and treat the other letter as a number in your head.

If the function already uses the letter $K$, as production functions do for capital, choose a different stand-in such as $\fc$. The letter does not matter. What matters is that it reminds you the variable is held still.

6

Common mistakes and what to check

These are common mistakes with first partial derivatives. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1

Dropping a held variable that is multiplied in. With respect to $y$, $5xy$ gives $5x$, not $5$. Check: swap in $\fz$ and the term reads $5\fz y$.

Common mistake 2

Keeping a term that does not contain your variable. With respect to $x$, $-2y^3$ gives $0$. Check: is there an $x$ in the term?

Common mistake 3

Differentiating the held variable as well. With respect to $x$, $x^3y^2$ gives $3x^2y^2$, not $3x^2\cdot 2y$. The $y^2$ is copied, not differentiated.

Common mistake 4

Leaving $\fz$ in the final answer. Swap it back so the answer uses the original variables.

Quick Check: Can You Apply It?

Differentiate each one on paper first, then open the answer.

(a) $z = 2x^2 + 4xy + y^2$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

$z_x$: $2x^2 + 4\fz x + \fz^2$ gives $4x + 4\fz$, so $z_x = 4x + 4y$. $z_y$: $2\fz^2 + 4\fz y + y^2$ gives $4\fz + 2y$, so $z_y = 4x + 2y$.

(b) $z = x^2y^4$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

$z_x$: $x^2\fz^4$ gives $2x\fz^4$, so $z_x = 2xy^4$. $z_y$: $\fz^2y^4$ gives $4\fz^2y^3$, so $z_y = 4x^2y^3$.

(c) $z = 8x - 3y + 5xy^2$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

$z_x$: $8x - 3\fz + 5\fz^2x$ gives $8 + 5\fz^2$, so $z_x = 8 + 5y^2$. $z_y$: $8\fz - 3y + 5\fz y^2$ gives $-3 + 10\fz y$, so $z_y = -3 + 10xy$.

Every rule still works

The chain rule, product rule and quotient rule work the same way they did with one variable. Swap in $\fz$ first, then use whichever rule the function calls for.

FunctionWith respect to $x$, it readsRule to use
$z = xe^{y}$$x\,e^{\fz}$None needed. $e^{\fz}$ is a number in front.
$z = \ln(x^2 + y)$$\ln(x^2 + \fz)$Chain rule: a function inside a function.
$z = xy\,e^{x}$$\fz x\,e^{x}$Product rule: two functions of $x$ multiplied.

2

Chain rule with a partial derivative

The chain rule steps are the same as before: let $u$ be the inside and find $u'$, rewrite with $u$, differentiate and multiply by $u'$, then plug in. The only addition is the $\fz$ swap at the start and the end.

Step 1 of 5
Worked example: the other partial

For the same function, $z = \ln(x^2 + y)$, find $z_y$.

1

Hold $x$ constant. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \ln(\fz^2 + y)$.

2

Name the inside. $u = \fz^2 + y$, so $u' = 1$. The $\fz^2$ gives $0$ and $y$ gives $1$.

3

Rewrite and differentiate. $z = \ln u$, so $z_y = \dfrac{1}{u}\cdot 1$.

4

Plug in, then swap $\fz$ back. $z_y = \dfrac{1}{\fz^2 + y} = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + y}$.

$$\boxed{z_y = \frac{1}{x^2 + y}}$$

Same function, same steps. Only $u'$ changed, because a different variable was moving.

Worked example: e to a power

For $z = e^{3x + 2y}$, find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

x

With respect to $x$. Swap in $\fz$: $z = e^{3x + 2\fz}$. Then $u = 3x + 2\fz$, so $u' = 3$. $z = e^{u}$ gives $e^{u}\cdot 3$. So $z_x = 3e^{3x + 2y}$.

y

With respect to $y$. Swap in $\fz$: $z = e^{3\fz + 2y}$. Then $u = 3\fz + 2y$, so $u' = 2$. So $z_y = 2e^{3x + 2y}$.

The power stays the same in both answers. Only the number that comes down in front changes.

3

Product rule with a partial derivative

Worked example

For $z = xy\,e^{x}$, find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

x

With respect to $x$: a product. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \fz x\,e^{x}$. Two functions of $x$ are multiplied, $\fz x$ and $e^{x}$. Copy it twice: $(\fz x)(e^x) + (\fz x)(e^x)$. Differentiate one piece in each copy: $(\fz)(e^x) + (\fz x)(e^x)$. Swap back: $z_x = ye^x + xye^x$.

y

With respect to $y$: no product at all. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \fz e^{\fz}\,y$. Everything except $y$ is a number in front. So $z_y = xe^{x}$.

$$\boxed{z_x = ye^x(1 + x) \qquad z_y = xe^x}$$

The same function needed the product rule for one partial and no rule at all for the other.

4

Quotient rule with a partial derivative

Worked example

For $z = \dfrac{x}{x + y}$, find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

x

With respect to $x$: a quotient. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \dfrac{x}{x + \fz}$. Top: $f = x$, $f' = 1$. Bottom: $h = x + \fz$, $h' = 1$. Copy the product rule, change to a minus, divide by the bottom squared: $\dfrac{(1)(x + \fz) - (x)(1)}{(x + \fz)^2} = \dfrac{\fz}{(x + \fz)^2}$. Swap back: $z_x = \dfrac{y}{(x + y)^2}$.

y

With respect to $y$: rewrite as a power. Swap in $\fz$: $z = \dfrac{\fz}{\fz + y} = \fz(\fz + y)^{-1}$. The top is now a number, so use the chain rule: $u = \fz + y$, $u' = 1$, giving $-\fz u^{-2}$. Swap back: $z_y = \dfrac{-x}{(x + y)^2}$.

$$\boxed{z_x = \frac{y}{(x + y)^2} \qquad z_y = \frac{-x}{(x + y)^2}}$$

Common mistakes and what to check

These are common mistakes when partial derivatives meet the other rules. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1

Differentiating the whole inside. For $e^{3x + 2y}$ with respect to $x$, $u'$ is $3$, not $3 + 2$. The $2y$ is held constant, so it gives $0$.

Common mistake 2

Using the product rule when one piece is constant. For $xe^{y}$ with respect to $x$, $e^{y}$ is a number in front. The answer is $e^{y}$.

Common mistake 3

Choosing the rule before the swap. Swap in $\fz$ first, then look at what kind of function of your variable is left.

Quick Check: Can You Apply It?

Differentiate each one on paper first, then open the answer.

(a) $z = (2x + 5y)^4$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

With respect to $x$: $u = 2x + 5\fz$, $u' = 2$, so $z_x = 4u^3\cdot 2 = 8(2x + 5y)^3$. With respect to $y$: $u = 2\fz + 5y$, $u' = 5$, so $z_y = 20(2x + 5y)^3$.

(b) $z = e^{xy}$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

With respect to $x$: $z = e^{\fz x}$, $u = \fz x$, $u' = \fz$, so $z_x = ye^{xy}$. With respect to $y$: $u = \fz y$, $u' = \fz$, so $z_y = xe^{xy}$.

(c) $z = y\ln x$. Find $z_x$ and $z_y$.

With respect to $x$: $z = \fz\ln x$ gives $\dfrac{\fz}{x}$, so $z_x = \dfrac{y}{x}$. With respect to $y$: $z = y\ln\fz$, and $\ln\fz$ is a number in front, so $z_y = \ln x$.
1

Differentiate again

A first partial derivative is itself a function of $x$ and $y$, so it can be differentiated again. Each first partial can be differentiated with respect to $x$ or with respect to $y$. That gives four second-order partial derivatives.

SymbolAlso writtenHow to get it
$z_{xx}$$\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x^2}$Differentiate $z_x$ with respect to $x$
$z_{yy}$$\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y^2}$Differentiate $z_y$ with respect to $y$
$z_{xy}$$\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial y\,\partial x}$Differentiate $z_x$ with respect to $y$
$z_{yx}$$\dfrac{\partial^2 z}{\partial x\,\partial y}$Differentiate $z_y$ with respect to $x$

Read the subscripts left to right: $z_{xy}$ means $x$ first, then $y$. Nothing about the method changes. Each step is the same four-step procedure, applied to the first partial instead of to $z$.

Worked example

Step 1 of 6

The mixed partials match. For the smooth functions used in economics courses, differentiating with respect to $x$ then $y$ gives the same result as $y$ then $x$. This result is known as Young's theorem. It gives you a free check: if your $z_{xy}$ and $z_{yx}$ differ, one of the steps needs another look.

3

What the second-order partials tell you

$z_x$ is the slope in the $x$ direction. $z_{xx}$ tells you how that slope changes as $x$ increases.

  • $z_{xx} < 0$: the slice is flattening as $x$ rises. Each extra unit of $x$ adds less than the one before.
  • $z_{xx} > 0$: the slice is getting steeper as $x$ rises.
  • $z_{xy}$: how the slope in the $x$ direction changes when $y$ increases.
4

Common mistakes and what to check

These are common mistakes with second-order partials. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1

Starting again from $z$. A second-order partial is the derivative of a first partial. For $z_{xx}$, differentiate $z_x$.

Common mistake 2

Using the same variable twice for a mixed partial. $z_{xy}$ uses $x$ for the first step and $y$ for the second.

Common mistake 3

Forgetting that the held variable switches. For $z_{xy}$, $y$ is held constant in the first step and $x$ is held constant in the second.

Quick Check: Can You Apply It?

Work each one on paper first, then open the answer.

(a) $z = x^2 + 3xy + y^3$. Find all four second-order partials.

$z_x = 2x + 3y$ and $z_y = 3x + 3y^2$. Then $z_{xx} = 2$, $z_{yy} = 6y$, $z_{xy} = 3$ and $z_{yx} = 3$.

(b) $z = x^2e^{y}$. Find all four second-order partials.

$z_x = 2xe^{y}$ and $z_y = x^2e^{y}$. Then $z_{xx} = 2e^{y}$, $z_{yy} = x^2e^{y}$, $z_{xy} = 2xe^{y}$ and $z_{yx} = 2xe^{y}$.

Worked example: marginal utilities

Worked example

A consumer's utility function is $U = 10x^{0.4}y^{0.6}$. Find $MU_x$ and $MU_y$, and simplify the ratio $\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}$.

x

Hold $y$ constant. $U = 10x^{0.4}\fz^{0.6}$ gives $10(0.4x^{-0.6})\fz^{0.6}$. So $MU_x = 4x^{-0.6}y^{0.6}$.

y

Hold $x$ constant. $U = 10\fz^{0.4}y^{0.6}$ gives $10\fz^{0.4}(0.6y^{-0.4})$. So $MU_y = 6x^{0.4}y^{-0.4}$.

÷

Take the ratio. $\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \dfrac{4x^{-0.6}y^{0.6}}{6x^{0.4}y^{-0.4}} = \dfrac{4}{6}\cdot\dfrac{y^{0.6 + 0.4}}{x^{0.4 + 0.6}}$.

$$\boxed{\frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{2y}{3x}}$$

This ratio is the marginal rate of substitution: how much of good $y$ the consumer would give up for one more unit of good $x$.

4

Small changes in both inputs

What if both inputs change at once? For small changes, add the two effects:

$$\Delta z \approx z_x\,\Delta x + z_y\,\Delta y$$

Each partial derivative is a rate. Multiply each rate by the size of that change, then add. Some courses call this the small changes formula or the incremental changes formula.

Example. For $Q = 10L^{0.5}K^{0.5}$ at $L = 16$, $K = 4$, we found $MP_L = 2.5$ and $MP_K = 10$. Suppose labour rises by $1$ and capital rises by $0.5$.

Estimate: $\Delta Q \approx 2.5(1) + 10(0.5) = 7.5$.

Actual: $Q$ goes from $80$ to $10\sqrt{17}\sqrt{4.5} \approx 87.4643$, a rise of about $7.4643$.

The estimate is close because the changes are small. It becomes less accurate as the changes get larger.

5

Common mistakes and what to check

Common mistake 1

Substituting the numbers before differentiating. If you put $L = 16$ and $K = 4$ in first, $Q$ becomes the number $80$ and there is nothing left to differentiate. Differentiate first, then evaluate.

Common mistake 2

Leaving out what is held constant. "One more unit of labour adds $2.5$" is only true with capital held at $4$. Say so in your interpretation.

Common mistake 3

Using $K$ as the stand-in when $K$ is already a variable. Pick another letter, such as $\fc$, or treat the held variable as a number in your head.

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = 4x^2 + 3\fz x - \fz^2 + 9$ gives $8x + 3\fz$, so $z_x = 8x + 3y$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = 4\fz^2 + 3\fz y - y^2 + 9$ gives $3\fz - 2y$, so $z_y = 3x - 2y$.

2

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = 5x^3 - 2x^2y + 7y$

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = 5x^3 - 2\fz x^2 + 7\fz$ gives $15x^2 - 4\fz x$, so $z_x = 15x^2 - 4xy$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = 5\fz^3 - 2\fz^2y + 7y$ gives $-2\fz^2 + 7$, so $z_y = -2x^2 + 7$.

3

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = x^2y^3$

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = \fz^3x^2$ gives $2\fz^3x$, so $z_x = 2xy^3$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = \fz^2y^3$ gives $3\fz^2y^2$, so $z_y = 3x^2y^2$.

4

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = 6x + 4y - xy + 10$

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = 6x + 4\fz - \fz x + 10$ gives $6 - \fz$, so $z_x = 6 - y$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = 6\fz + 4y - \fz y + 10$ gives $4 - \fz$, so $z_y = 4 - x$.

5

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = 3x^4y - 5xy^2 + 2y^3$

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = 3\fz x^4 - 5\fz^2x + 2\fz^3$ gives $12\fz x^3 - 5\fz^2$, so $z_x = 12x^3y - 5y^2$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = 3\fz^4y - 5\fz y^2 + 2y^3$ gives $3\fz^4 - 10\fz y + 6y^2$, so $z_y = 3x^4 - 10xy + 6y^2$.

6

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = \sqrt{x}\,y^2$

Rewrite, then swap

Rewrite the root as a power first: $z = x^{0.5}y^2$.

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = \fz^2x^{0.5}$ gives $0.5\fz^2x^{-0.5}$, so $z_x = 0.5x^{-0.5}y^2$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = \fz^{0.5}y^2$ gives $2\fz^{0.5}y$, so $z_y = 2x^{0.5}y$.

7

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = \dfrac{12}{x} + \dfrac{x}{y}$

Rewrite, then swap

Rewrite as powers first: $z = 12x^{-1} + xy^{-1}$.

$z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $z = 12x^{-1} + \fz^{-1}x$ gives $-12x^{-2} + \fz^{-1}$, so $z_x = -\dfrac{12}{x^2} + \dfrac{1}{y}$.

$z_y$: hold $x$ constant. $z = 12\fz^{-1} + \fz y^{-1}$ gives $-\fz y^{-2}$, so $z_y = -\dfrac{x}{y^2}$.

8

Find $Q_L$ and $Q_K$. $Q = 8L + 3K + 2LK$

Swap, differentiate, swap back

$K$ is a variable here, so use $\fc$ as the stand-in.

$Q_L$: hold $K$ constant. $Q = 8L + 3\fc + 2\fc L$ gives $8 + 2\fc$, so $Q_L = 8 + 2K$.

$Q_K$: hold $L$ constant. $Q = 8\fc + 3K + 2\fc K$ gives $3 + 2\fc$, so $Q_K = 3 + 2L$.

2

With the chain, product and quotient rules

1

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = e^{3x + 2y}$

Chain rule

$z_x$. $z = e^{3x + 2\fz}$. $u = 3x + 2\fz$, so $u' = 3$. Then $e^{u}\cdot 3$ gives $z_x = 3e^{3x + 2y}$.

$z_y$. $z = e^{3\fz + 2y}$. $u = 3\fz + 2y$, so $u' = 2$. Then $z_y = 2e^{3x + 2y}$.

2

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = \ln(x + y^2)$

Chain rule

$z_x$. $z = \ln(x + \fz^2)$. $u = x + \fz^2$, so $u' = 1$. Then $\dfrac{1}{u}\cdot 1$ gives $z_x = \dfrac{1}{x + y^2}$.

$z_y$. $z = \ln(\fz + y^2)$. $u = \fz + y^2$, so $u' = 2y$. Then $z_y = \dfrac{2y}{x + y^2}$.

3

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = (2x + 5y)^4$

Chain rule

$z_x$. $u = 2x + 5\fz$, so $u' = 2$. Then $4u^3\cdot 2$ gives $z_x = 8(2x + 5y)^3$.

$z_y$. $u = 2\fz + 5y$, so $u' = 5$. Then $4u^3\cdot 5$ gives $z_y = 20(2x + 5y)^3$.

4

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = xe^{y}$

A number in front

$z_x$. $z = x\,e^{\fz}$. $e^{\fz}$ is a number in front of $x$, so $z_x = e^{y}$.

$z_y$. $z = \fz e^{y}$. $\fz$ is a number in front of $e^{y}$, so $z_y = xe^{y}$.

No product rule is needed for either one. In each case one piece is a constant.

5

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = x^2e^{xy}$

Product rule with chain rule inside

$z_x$. $z = x^2e^{\fz x}$: two functions of $x$ multiplied. Copy it twice: $(x^2)(e^{\fz x}) + (x^2)(e^{\fz x})$.

Differentiate one piece in each copy: $(2x)(e^{\fz x}) + (x^2)(\fz e^{\fz x})$. So $z_x = 2xe^{xy} + x^2ye^{xy}$.

$z_y$. $z = \fz^2e^{\fz y}$. $\fz^2$ is a number in front. For $e^{\fz y}$: $u = \fz y$, $u' = \fz$. So $\fz^2\cdot\fz e^{\fz y}$ gives $z_y = x^3e^{xy}$.

6

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = \dfrac{x}{x + y}$

Quotient rule, then a rewrite

$z_x$. $z = \dfrac{x}{x + \fz}$. Top: $f = x$, $f' = 1$. Bottom: $h = x + \fz$, $h' = 1$.

$\dfrac{(1)(x + \fz) - (x)(1)}{(x + \fz)^2} = \dfrac{\fz}{(x + \fz)^2}$, so $z_x = \dfrac{y}{(x + y)^2}$.

$z_y$. $z = \fz(\fz + y)^{-1}$. $u = \fz + y$, $u' = 1$. Then $-\fz u^{-2}$ gives $z_y = \dfrac{-x}{(x + y)^2}$.

7

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = y\ln x$

A number in front

$z_x$. $z = \fz\ln x$ gives $\dfrac{\fz}{x}$, so $z_x = \dfrac{y}{x}$.

$z_y$. $z = y\ln\fz$. $\ln\fz$ is a number in front of $y$, so $z_y = \ln x$.

8

Find $z_x$ and $z_y$. $z = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$

Rewrite, then chain rule

Rewrite as $z = (x^2 + y^2)^{0.5}$.

$z_x$. $u = x^2 + \fz^2$, so $u' = 2x$. Then $0.5u^{-0.5}\cdot 2x$ gives $z_x = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$.

$z_y$. $u = \fz^2 + y^2$, so $u' = 2y$. Then $z_y = \dfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}$.

3

Second-order partial derivatives

1

Find all four second-order partials. $z = x^3 + 4x^2y - 2y^2$

Differentiate the first partials

First partials: $z_x = 3x^2 + 8xy$ and $z_y = 4x^2 - 4y$.

From $z_x$: $z_{xx} = 6x + 8y$ and $z_{xy} = 8x$.

From $z_y$: $z_{yy} = -4$ and $z_{yx} = 8x$.

Check: $z_{xy} = z_{yx}$.

2

Find all four second-order partials. $z = x^2y^3$

Differentiate the first partials

First partials: $z_x = 2xy^3$ and $z_y = 3x^2y^2$.

From $z_x$: $z_{xx} = 2y^3$ and $z_{xy} = 6xy^2$.

From $z_y$: $z_{yy} = 6x^2y$ and $z_{yx} = 6xy^2$.

Check: $z_{xy} = z_{yx}$.

3

Find all four second-order partials. $z = e^{2x + y}$

Differentiate the first partials

First partials: $z_x = 2e^{2x + y}$ and $z_y = e^{2x + y}$.

From $z_x$: $z_{xx} = 4e^{2x + y}$ and $z_{xy} = 2e^{2x + y}$.

From $z_y$: $z_{yy} = e^{2x + y}$ and $z_{yx} = 2e^{2x + y}$.

Check: $z_{xy} = z_{yx}$.

4

Find all four second-order partials. $z = x\ln y$

Differentiate the first partials

First partials: $z_x = \ln y$ and $z_y = \dfrac{x}{y} = xy^{-1}$.

From $z_x$: $z_{xx} = 0$ and $z_{xy} = \dfrac{1}{y}$.

From $z_y$: $z_{yy} = -xy^{-2} = -\dfrac{x}{y^2}$ and $z_{yx} = \dfrac{1}{y}$.

Check: $z_{xy} = z_{yx}$.

The problems in section 1 are mixed, and their order changes each time the page loads. Each one asks for a specific partial derivative, so start by identifying which variable is moving and which is held constant. Then decide which rule the function needs, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

1

Find the partial derivative

1

Find $z_y$. $z = 7x^2y - 3y + 2$

First partial

Hold $x$ constant: $z = 7\fz^2y - 3y + 2$ gives $7\fz^2 - 3$.

$z_y = 7x^2 - 3$.

2

Find $z_x$. $z = 4x^3 - 9y^2 + xy$

First partial

Hold $y$ constant: $z = 4x^3 - 9\fz^2 + \fz x$ gives $12x^2 - 0 + \fz$.

$z_x = 12x^2 + y$.

3

Find $z_x$. $z = e^{x^2y}$

Chain rule

Hold $y$ constant: $z = e^{\fz x^2}$. $u = \fz x^2$, so $u' = 2\fz x$.

$e^{u}\cdot 2\fz x$ gives $z_x = 2xye^{x^2y}$.

4

Find $z_y$. $z = \ln(3x + y^2)$

Chain rule

Hold $x$ constant: $z = \ln(3\fz + y^2)$. $u = 3\fz + y^2$, so $u' = 2y$.

$\dfrac{1}{u}\cdot 2y$ gives $z_y = \dfrac{2y}{3x + y^2}$.

5

Find $z_x$. $z = (x^2 + y)^5$

Chain rule

Hold $y$ constant: $u = x^2 + \fz$, so $u' = 2x$.

$5u^4\cdot 2x$ gives $z_x = 10x(x^2 + y)^4$.

6

Find $z_x$. $z = \dfrac{xy}{x + y}$

Quotient rule

Hold $y$ constant: $z = \dfrac{\fz x}{x + \fz}$. Top: $f = \fz x$, $f' = \fz$. Bottom: $h = x + \fz$, $h' = 1$.

$\dfrac{(\fz)(x + \fz) - (\fz x)(1)}{(x + \fz)^2} = \dfrac{\fz^2}{(x + \fz)^2}$, so $z_x = \dfrac{y^2}{(x + y)^2}$.

7

Find $U_x$. $U = x^{0.5}y^{0.5}$

First partial

Hold $y$ constant: $U = \fz^{0.5}x^{0.5}$ gives $0.5\fz^{0.5}x^{-0.5}$.

$U_x = 0.5x^{-0.5}y^{0.5}$. In economics this is the marginal utility of good $x$.

8

Find $z_{xy}$. $z = 5x^2y^3 + 2x$

Second-order partial

First, $z_x$: hold $y$ constant. $5\fz^3x^2 + 2x$ gives $z_x = 10xy^3 + 2$.

Then differentiate $z_x$ with respect to $y$, holding $x$ constant: $10\fz y^3 + 2$ gives $z_{xy} = 30xy^2$.

9

Find $Q_L$. $Q = 6L^2K - L^3$

First partial

$K$ is a variable here, so use $\fc$ as the stand-in: $Q = 6\fc L^2 - L^3$ gives $12\fc L - 3L^2$.

$Q_L = 12LK - 3L^2$. This is the marginal product of labour.

10

Find $z_{xx}$. $z = ye^{-x}$

Second-order partial

Hold $y$ constant both times. $z = \fz e^{-x}$ gives $z_x = -ye^{-x}$.

Differentiate again with respect to $x$: $z_{xx} = ye^{-x}$.

2

Economics applications

1

A firm's production function is $Q = 8L^{0.75}K^{0.25}$.

  1. Find $MP_L$ and $MP_K$.
  2. Evaluate both at $L = 16$, $K = 81$.
  3. Say in words what each number means.

Marginal products

(a) Hold $K$ constant: $8L^{0.75}\fc^{0.25}$ gives $MP_L = 6L^{-0.25}K^{0.25}$. Hold $L$ constant: $8\fc^{0.75}K^{0.25}$ gives $MP_K = 2L^{0.75}K^{-0.75}$.

(b) $16^{0.25} = 2$ and $81^{0.25} = 3$. So $MP_L = 6\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot 3 = 9$ and $MP_K = 2\cdot 8\cdot\dfrac{1}{27} = \dfrac{16}{27} \approx 0.5926$.

(c) With capital held at $81$, one more unit of labour adds about $9$ units of output. With labour held at $16$, one more unit of capital adds about $0.5926$ units.

2

The demand for a good is $Q = 200 - 5P + 0.03Y - 0.0001PY$, where $P$ is its price and $Y$ is income.

  1. Find $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial P}$ and $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial Y}$.
  2. Evaluate both at $P = 50$, $Y = 20000$, and say what each one means.

First partials, then interpret

(a) Hold $Y$ constant: $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial P} = -5 - 0.0001Y$. Hold $P$ constant: $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial Y} = 0.03 - 0.0001P$.

(b) $\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial P} = -5 - 0.0001(20000) = -7$. With income held at $20000$, a price rise of one unit lowers quantity demanded by about $7$.

$\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial Y} = 0.03 - 0.0001(50) = 0.025$. With price held at $50$, one more unit of income raises quantity demanded by about $0.025$.

Notice that the price effect depends on the income level being held constant. Held constant does not mean irrelevant.

3

A firm sells two products, and its profit is $\pi = 40x + 60y - 2x^2 - 3y^2 - xy$, where $x$ and $y$ are the quantities sold.

  1. Find $\pi_x$ and $\pi_y$.
  2. Find all four second-order partials, and check the mixed partials.

First and second-order partials

(a) Hold $y$ constant: $\pi_x = 40 - 4x - y$. Hold $x$ constant: $\pi_y = 60 - 6y - x$.

(b) From $\pi_x$: $\pi_{xx} = -4$ and $\pi_{xy} = -1$. From $\pi_y$: $\pi_{yy} = -6$ and $\pi_{yx} = -1$.

Check: $\pi_{xy} = \pi_{yx} = -1$.

In words, $\pi_x$ is the extra profit from selling one more unit of the first product, with sales of the second held constant.

4

For the production function $Q = 6L^{0.5}K^{0.5}$ at $L = 25$, $K = 9$:

  1. Find $MP_L$ and $MP_K$ at that point.
  2. Estimate the change in output if labour rises by $0.5$ and capital rises by $0.2$.

Marginal products, then small changes

(a) $MP_L = 3L^{-0.5}K^{0.5} = 3\cdot\dfrac{1}{5}\cdot 3 = 1.8$. $MP_K = 3L^{0.5}K^{-0.5} = 3\cdot 5\cdot\dfrac{1}{3} = 5$.

(b) $\Delta Q \approx MP_L\,\Delta L + MP_K\,\Delta K = 1.8(0.5) + 5(0.2) = 1.9$.

For comparison, the actual change is $6\sqrt{25.5}\sqrt{9.2} - 90 \approx 1.8999$. The estimate is close because the changes are small.
