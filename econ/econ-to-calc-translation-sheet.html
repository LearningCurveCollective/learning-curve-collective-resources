Economics problems often come down to a few familiar calculus techniques. The trick is recognising what the question is asking you to find. Use this sheet to connect common economics questions to the calculus you'll need to solve them.

1

The four moves

1

Build

Start by writing the function you need. Depending on the question, that might be revenue, cost, profit, or something else.

2

Differentiate

Find the first derivative. This tells you how the function is changing.

3

Set to zero

Set the first derivative equal to zero and solve for the critical points.

4

Classify

Use the second derivative to check whether each point is a maximum or minimum. Negative means maximum; positive means minimum.

Once you have an answer, reread the question. It may want a price, a profit or a range, not only $Q$.

6

Before you finish: a few things to check

Check 1

Don't stop at quantity. If the question asks for price or profit, you're not quite finished. Substitute your quantity back into the appropriate function.

Check 2

Check which turning point you've found. Setting the first derivative to zero finds possible maxima and minima. Use the second derivative to tell them apart.

Check 3

Check for a change in curvature. A second derivative of zero doesn't automatically mean there's a point of inflection. Make sure the second derivative changes sign.

Check 4

Revenue and profit aren't the same thing. Maximum revenue occurs where $MR = 0$. Maximum profit occurs where $MR = MC$.

Check 5

Break-even doesn't require differentiation. Set profit equal to zero, or equivalently, total revenue equal to total cost.

Check 6

Rearrange demand before finding revenue. If demand is given as $Q$ in terms of $P$, solve for $P$ first. That lets you write revenue as a function of $Q$.

1

Profit: build, differentiate, solve, classify

“Maximise profit” and “$MR = MC$” are two ways of writing the same equation, because $\pi' = MR - MC$. This example uses the same method as the monopolist worked example in your textbook, with different numbers.

Worked example 1: profit for a monopolist

Worked example

A monopolist faces the demand function $P = 240 - 6Q$ and has total cost $TC = Q^3 - 24Q^2 + 300Q + 50$.

  1. Write down the equations for $TR$ and $\pi$.
  2. Find the outputs at which profit is maximised and minimised.
  3. Confirm that $MR = MC$ at maximum profit.
  4. Find the price charged and the maximum profit.
  5. State the range of $Q$ for which profit is increasing.

Try translating each part yourself before you open the steps.

0

The plan. We'll start by building the profit function, then find where its derivative equals zero. The second derivative will tell us which output gives maximum profit and which gives minimum profit. From there, we can work out price, profit, and where profit is increasing.

1

Build TR. Revenue is price times quantity. Since we have a demand function, substitute it for price. $$TR = P \times Q = (240 - 6Q)Q = 240Q - 6Q^2$$

2

Build profit. Profit is revenue minus cost. Be careful with the brackets here: every term in the cost function gets subtracted. $$\begin{aligned}\pi &= 240Q - 6Q^2 - (Q^3 - 24Q^2 + 300Q + 50) \\ &= -Q^3 + 18Q^2 - 60Q - 50\end{aligned}$$

3

Differentiate. Now take the derivative of profit with respect to $Q$. $$\pi' = \frac{d\pi}{dQ} = -3Q^2 + 36Q - 60$$

4

Set to zero and solve. Set the derivative equal to zero. Dividing through by $-3$ makes the equation easier to factorise. $$Q^2 - 12Q + 20 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad (Q - 2)(Q - 10) = 0$$ So $Q = 2$ or $Q = 10$.

5

Classify. We have two critical points, but only one gives maximum profit. Use the second derivative to distinguish between them: $\pi'' = -6Q + 36$. At $Q = 2$, $\pi'' = 24 > 0$, so profit is at a minimum. At $Q = 10$, $\pi'' = -24 < 0$, so profit is at a maximum.

6

Confirm MR = MC. There's another way to check our answer. Since profit is $TR - TC$, its derivative is $MR - MC$. Setting that equal to zero gives us $MR = MC$: an additional unit brings in as much as it costs. Here $MR = TR' = 240 - 12Q$ and $MC = TC' = 3Q^2 - 48Q + 300$, and at $Q = 10$ both equal $120$. Notice that $MR$ also equals $MC$ at the minimum-profit output ($Q = 2$, where both equal $216$). That's why we still need the second derivative to identify the maximum.

7

Answer in the units asked for. We've found the quantity, but the question also asks for price and profit. Substitute the profit-maximising quantity into the demand and profit functions: $P = 240 - 6(10) = 180$ and $\pi(10) = -1000 + 1800 - 600 - 50 = 150$. At the other critical point, $\pi(2) = -8 + 72 - 120 - 50 = -106$, a loss.

8

Where is profit increasing? Profit increases wherever its first derivative is positive. Here, the derivative $\pi' = -3(Q - 2)(Q - 10)$ is a downward-opening parabola, so it's positive between its two roots: $2 < Q < 10$.

$$\boxed{Q = 10, \quad P = 180, \quad \pi_{\max} = 150}$$

Two markets, two prices

Price discrimination is the same idea used twice: set $MR = MC$ in each market separately.

Worked example 2: price discrimination in two markets

Worked example

A producer can charge different prices in two markets, A and B. The demand functions are $Q_A = 40 - 2P_A$ and $Q_B = 60 - 4P_B$. The cost function is $TC = 30 + 5Q$, where $Q = Q_A + Q_B$.

  1. What price should be charged in each market to maximise profit?
  2. Calculate the total cost and the profit when profit is maximised.

Try translating each part yourself before you open the steps.

0

The plan. We'll work with each market separately to find its profit-maximising quantity. Since the demand equations give us $Q$ in terms of $P$, our first step is to rearrange them.

1

Rearrange each demand function. Solve each equation for price so we can write total revenue as a function of quantity. $$\begin{aligned}Q_A = 40 - 2P_A \quad&\Rightarrow\quad P_A = 20 - 0.5Q_A \\ Q_B = 60 - 4P_B \quad&\Rightarrow\quad P_B = 15 - 0.25Q_B\end{aligned}$$

2

Build TR for each market. Multiply price by quantity in each market to get the two revenue functions. $$\begin{aligned}TR_A &= P_A Q_A = 20Q_A - 0.5Q_A^2 \\ TR_B &= P_B Q_B = 15Q_B - 0.25Q_B^2\end{aligned}$$

3

Differentiate. Take the derivative of each revenue function to find marginal revenue. Marginal cost comes from differentiating total cost: $MC = 5$. $$\begin{aligned}MR_A &= 20 - Q_A \\ MR_B &= 15 - 0.5Q_B\end{aligned}$$

4

Set MR = MC in each market. For maximum profit, set marginal revenue equal to marginal cost in each market and solve for quantity. $$\begin{aligned}20 - Q_A = 5 \quad&\Rightarrow\quad Q_A = 15 \\ 15 - 0.5Q_B = 5 \quad&\Rightarrow\quad Q_B = 20\end{aligned}$$ The second derivatives are negative ($-1$ and $-0.5$), confirming that these quantities maximise profit.

5

Answer in the units asked for. Substitute the quantities into their demand equations to find the prices: $P_A = 20 - 0.5(15) = 12.5$ and $P_B = 15 - 0.25(20) = 10$. Then use total output, $Q = 35$, to calculate cost, revenue, and profit: $TC = 30 + 5(35) = 205$, $TR = 12.5(15) + 10(20) = 387.5$ and $\pi = 387.5 - 205 = 182.5$.

$$\boxed{P_A = 12.5, \quad P_B = 10, \quad TC = 205, \quad \pi = 182.5}$$

How to graph a cubic: the plan

A cubic has the form $f(Q) = aQ^3 + bQ^2 + cQ + d$. To graph one, find four things in this order, check how the graph finishes, then join the points. Example A below is $f(Q) = Q^3 - 12Q^2 + 36Q + 5$. Example B is the cost curve $TC = Q^3 - 12Q^2 + 60Q + 100$.

Step 1Y-intercept: calculate $f(0)$. This is where the graph starts.
Example A$f(0) = 5$, so the graph starts at $(0, 5)$
Example B$TC(0) = 100$: a total cost curve starts at fixed cost
Step 2Critical points: set $f'(Q) = 0$. These are the possible minima and maxima.
Example A$f' = 3Q^2 - 24Q + 36 = 3(Q - 2)(Q - 6)$, so $Q = 2$ or $Q = 6$. The heights are $f(2) = 37$ and $f(6) = 5$.
Example B$TC' = 3Q^2 - 24Q + 60$ is never zero, because $24^2 - 4(3)(60) = -144$ is negative. This cost curve has no turning points: it rises the whole way.
The derivative of a cubic is a quadratic, so a cubic has two critical points, one, or none.
Step 3Classify the critical points: evaluate $f''(Q)$ at each one. Positive means minimum; negative means maximum.
Example A$f'' = 6Q - 24$. Since $f''(2) = -12$, there's a maximum at $(2, 37)$. Since $f''(6) = 12$, there's a minimum at $(6, 5)$.
Step 4Inflection point: set $f''(Q) = 0$. This is where the concavity changes.
Example A$6Q - 24 = 0$ gives $Q = 4$, and $f(4) = 21$. The inflection point is $(4, 21)$.
Example B$TC'' = 6Q - 24 = 0$ gives $Q = 4$ too. That's where marginal cost reaches its minimum, $MC(4) = 12$.
Step 5Check the ends: look at the sign of $a$. If $a$ is positive, the cubic eventually rises to the right. If $a$ is negative, it eventually falls.
Example A$a = 1$, so the graph climbs again after its minimum: $f(8) = 37$
Example B$y = 12x^2 - x^3$ from worked example 3 has $a = -1$, so it falls after its maximum
Step 6Plot and join: plot each point with its height, then draw one smooth curve through them. The curve bends one way before the inflection point and the other way after it.
Example AUp from $(0, 5)$ to the maximum $(2, 37)$, down through $(4, 21)$ to the minimum $(6, 5)$, then up again
A useful shortcut for cubics
When a cubic has two turning points, the inflection point is exactly halfway between them. In Example A, $$\frac{2 + 6}{2} = 4$$ and the height is halfway too: $\dfrac{37 + 5}{2} = 21$. Use it to check your answer to Step 4.
Three things to check as you draw
Check 1Find the heights. A critical point is a point on the graph, so you need $f(Q)$ at each one before you can plot it.
Check 2Roots are a separate job. If the question asks for them, solve $f(Q) = 0$ and mark where the curve meets the horizontal axis. Example A is $Q(Q - 6)^2 + 5$, which stays above zero for $Q \ge 0$, so it has no roots there.
Check 3A second derivative of zero at a critical point. The test in Step 3 can't decide that case. Look at the sign of $f'$ on each side: if it doesn't change, the graph flattens there without turning, as $y = x^3$ does at $x = 0$.

Read it as a graph

Worked example 3: understanding a cubic and its derivatives

Worked example

Consider the function $y = 12x^2 - x^3$ for $x \ge 0$.

  1. Find the roots.
  2. Find the turning points and classify each as a minimum or a maximum.
  3. Find the point of inflection.
  4. Sketch $y$. Underneath it, sketch $y'$ and $y''$, and explain how the three graphs relate.

Try translating each part yourself before you open the steps.

0

The plan. To sketch this cubic, we'll find its roots, turning points, and point of inflection. Those points give us the basic shape of the graph. Then we'll compare the original function with its first and second derivatives.

1

Roots. Set the function equal to zero and factorise. These are the points where the graph meets the $x$-axis. $$y = x^2(12 - x) = 0$$ So $x = 0$ or $x = 12$.

2

Turning points. Turning points occur where the first derivative equals zero. Solve for $x$, then substitute back into the original function to find the coordinates. $$y' = 24x - 3x^2 = 3x(8 - x) = 0$$ So $x = 0$ or $x = 8$, with $y(0) = 0$ and $y(8) = 768 - 512 = 256$.

3

Classify. Use the second derivative to check each turning point. A positive value means a minimum; a negative value means a maximum. Here $y'' = 24 - 6x$. At $x = 0$, $y'' = 24 > 0$: a minimum at $(0, 0)$. At $x = 8$, $y'' = -24 < 0$: a maximum at $(8, 256)$.

4

Point of inflection. The point of inflection is where the graph changes from bending upward to bending downward, or vice versa. Set the second derivative equal to zero, then check that its sign actually changes. $$y'' = 24 - 6x = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 4$$ On either side, $y''(3) = 6 > 0$ and $y''(5) = -6 < 0$, so the sign does change. Since $y(4) = 192 - 64 = 128$, the POI is $(4, 128)$.

5

Sketch and compare. Now we have the key points we need: $(0, 0)$, $(4, 128)$, $(8, 256)$ and $(12, 0)$. Plot them, sketch the curve through them, and use the second derivative to check which way the graph bends: upward until $x = 4$, downward after it.

How the three graphs relate

  • When $y$ reaches a turning point ($x = 0$ and $x = 8$), its slope is zero. That's where $y'$ crosses the $x$-axis.
  • When $y$ is increasing ($0 < x < 8$), $y'$ is positive. When $y$ is decreasing ($x > 8$), $y'$ is negative.
  • At the point of inflection ($x = 4$), $y$ changes curvature. Here, that corresponds to the maximum of $y'$, which is $48$, and the point where $y''$ crosses zero.
  • When $y''$ is positive ($x < 4$), the slope of $y$ is increasing and the graph bends upward. When $y''$ is negative ($x > 4$), the slope is decreasing and the graph bends downward.

Read it as economics

The same cubic, read as a production function

Concept

Now suppose the same cubic represents total production, $Q = 12L^2 - L^3$, with $L$ measuring labour. We can use exactly the same calculus as before. The difference is how we interpret the results in economic terms.

Economics questionCalculusAnswer
Marginal product of labour$MP_L = \dfrac{dQ}{dL}$$MP_L = 24L - 3L^2$
Where do diminishing returns set in?$MP_L$ is at its maximum: $\dfrac{d^2Q}{dL^2} = 0$$L = 4$, where $MP_L = 48$. This is the POI of $Q$.
Maximum output$MP_L = 0$$L = 8$, where $Q = 256$
Average product of labour$AP_L = \dfrac{Q}{L}$$AP_L = 12L - L^2$
Maximum average product$\dfrac{d(AP_L)}{dL} = 12 - 2L = 0$$L = 6$, where $AP_L = 36$
Check: MPL = APL at maximum APLEvaluate $MP_L$ at $L = 6$$MP_L = 144 - 108 = 36$, equal to $AP_L$

Three regions of the curve

  • $0 < L < 4$: output is increasing, and each additional unit of labour adds more than the previous one.
  • $4 < L < 8$: output is still increasing, but each additional unit of labour adds less than the previous one. This is diminishing marginal returns.
  • $L > 8$: marginal product is negative, so adding more labour actually reduces total output.
The same reasoning applies to utility. We usually assume that consuming more of a good increases total utility, but that each additional unit adds a little less than the one before. In calculus terms, we're looking for a function that's increasing but bending downward: a positive first derivative and a negative second derivative. On this cubic, that is the region $4 < x < 8$.

Average and marginal cost

Worked example 4: minimum AVC and where MC crosses it

Worked example

A firm has total cost $TC = Q^3 - 12Q^2 + 60Q + 100$.

  1. Write down the equations for $AVC$ and $MC$.
  2. Find the output at which $AVC$ is a minimum.
  3. Calculate $MC$ and $AVC$ at that output. What do you conclude?

Try translating each part yourself before you open the steps.

0

The plan. We want to find the quantity that minimises average variable cost. First, separate variable cost from fixed cost. Then divide variable cost by quantity and use derivatives to find the minimum.

1

Separate TVC from TFC. Fixed cost is the constant term: it doesn't change with output. The terms involving $Q$ make up variable cost. $$TVC = Q^3 - 12Q^2 + 60Q, \qquad TFC = 100$$

2

Build AVC. Average variable cost is total variable cost divided by quantity. $$AVC = \frac{TVC}{Q} = Q^2 - 12Q + 60$$

3

Differentiate and set to zero. Take the derivative of $AVC$ and set it equal to zero to find the critical quantity. $$AVC' = 2Q - 12 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q = 6$$

4

Classify. Check the second derivative to confirm that we've found a minimum: $AVC'' = 2 > 0$. Then substitute the quantity back into $AVC$: $AVC(6) = 36 - 72 + 60 = 24$.

5

Compare with MC. Find marginal cost by differentiating total cost, then evaluate it at the quantity we found. $$MC = \frac{d(TC)}{dQ} = 3Q^2 - 24Q + 60$$ At $Q = 6$, $MC = 108 - 144 + 60 = 24$. Notice that $MC$ and $AVC$ are equal.

$$\boxed{AVC \text{ is a minimum at } Q = 6, \text{ where } MC = AVC = 24}$$

Why MC and AVC meet at the minimum

Here's why this works. When marginal cost is below average variable cost, the next unit costs less than the current average, so it pulls the average down. When marginal cost is above average variable cost, the next unit costs more than the current average, so it pushes the average up.

The point where $MC = AVC$ is where that changes. That's why marginal cost crosses average variable cost at its minimum.

The same idea applies to marginal cost and average total cost. And if the problem uses letters instead of numbers, the process is exactly the same: treat the coefficients as constants and work through the algebra.

1

A firm faces the demand function $P = 100 - 2Q$ and has total cost $TC = 3Q^2 + 20Q + 50$. Find the profit-maximising output, the price, and the maximum profit.

Maximise profit

  1. Build: $TR = 100Q - 2Q^2$, so $\pi = 100Q - 2Q^2 - (3Q^2 + 20Q + 50) = -5Q^2 + 80Q - 50$.
  2. Differentiate and set to zero: $\pi' = -10Q + 80 = 0$, so $Q = 8$.
  3. Classify: $\pi'' = -10 < 0$, a maximum.
  4. Answer: $P = 100 - 2(8) = 84$ and $\pi(8) = -320 + 640 - 50 = 270$.
2

A firm has total cost $TC = Q^3 - 9Q^2 + 40Q + 20$. Find the point of inflection of $TC$, and say what it tells you about $MC$.

Point of inflection

  1. $MC = TC' = 3Q^2 - 18Q + 40$ and $TC'' = 6Q - 18$.
  2. Set $TC'' = 0$: $Q = 3$. Sign check: $TC''(2) = -6$ and $TC''(4) = 6$, so the sign changes.
  3. $TC(3) = 27 - 81 + 120 + 20 = 86$. The POI is $(3, 86)$.
  4. The POI of $TC$ is the turning point of $MC$: marginal cost is at its minimum, $MC(3) = 13$, at $Q = 3$.
3

Let $U = 9x^2 - x^3$ for $x \ge 0$. Find the range of $x$ over which utility is rising while marginal utility is diminishing.

Diminishing marginal utility

  1. Rising means $U' > 0$: $U' = 18x - 3x^2 = 3x(6 - x) > 0$ for $0 < x < 6$.
  2. Diminishing marginal utility means $U'' < 0$: $U'' = 18 - 6x < 0$ for $x > 3$.
  3. Both hold for $3 < x < 6$.
4

A firm has total cost $TC = 2Q^2 + 5Q + 72$. Find the output at which average cost is a minimum, and confirm that $MC = AC$ there.

Minimise average cost

  1. Build: $AC = \dfrac{TC}{Q} = 2Q + 5 + \dfrac{72}{Q}$.
  2. Differentiate and set to zero: $AC' = 2 - \dfrac{72}{Q^2} = 0$, so $Q^2 = 36$ and $Q = 6$ (output is positive).
  3. Classify: $AC'' = \dfrac{144}{Q^3} > 0$ for $Q > 0$, a minimum.
  4. $AC(6) = 12 + 5 + 12 = 29$ and $MC = 4Q + 5 = 29$ at $Q = 6$. They match.
5

A firm's total revenue is $TR = 60Q - 2Q^2$. Find the output at which revenue is maximised, the maximum revenue, and the price at that output.

Maximise revenue

  1. Maximum revenue means $MR = 0$: $MR = 60 - 4Q = 0$, so $Q = 15$.
  2. Classify: $TR'' = -4 < 0$, a maximum. $TR(15) = 900 - 450 = 450$.
  3. Price is average revenue: $P = \dfrac{TR}{Q} = 60 - 2Q = 30$.
6

The graph of $y'$ is a downward-opening parabola that crosses the $x$-axis at $x = 1$ and $x = 5$. State where $y$ has its minimum, its maximum and its point of inflection.

Reading a derivative graph

  1. At $x = 1$, $y'$ changes from negative to positive: $y$ stops falling and starts rising, a minimum.
  2. At $x = 5$, $y'$ changes from positive to negative: a maximum.
  3. The POI of $y$ is where $y'$ has its own turning point. The vertex of the parabola is halfway between its roots, at $x = 3$.

Find the critical points of $f(Q) = Q^3 - 6Q^2 + 12Q + 3$ and decide whether the graph turns there.

A flat point that doesn't turn

  1. $f' = 3Q^2 - 12Q + 12 = 3(Q - 2)^2$, so there is one critical point, at $Q = 2$.
  2. $f''(2) = 12 - 12 = 0$, so the second derivative test can't decide.
  3. $f' = 3(Q - 2)^2$ is positive on both sides of $Q = 2$, so the graph is rising before and after it.
  4. The graph flattens at $(2, 11)$ without turning. That point is the inflection point.
