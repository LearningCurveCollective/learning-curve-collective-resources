How responsive? The units problem, solved

Unit 2 told you which direction $x^*$ moves when $p_x$ changes. The next question is how much — and the slope $\dfrac{dx}{dp}$ is a terrible answer, because it depends on units: measure gasoline in liters instead of gallons and the “same” demand curve suddenly has a slope four times bigger. Economists fix this by measuring responsiveness in percentages:

$$\varepsilon \;=\; \frac{\%\Delta x}{\%\Delta p} \;=\; \frac{dx}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{x}$$

Read it as: when price rises 1%, quantity changes by $\varepsilon$%. For downward-sloping demand $\varepsilon < 0$; we classify by its size:

  • $|\varepsilon| > 1$: elastic — buyers are jumpy; a 1% price rise scares off more than 1% of quantity.
  • $|\varepsilon| < 1$: inelastic — buyers shrug; quantity barely reacts (think gasoline, insulin).
  • $|\varepsilon| = 1$: unit elastic — the knife edge, and (as you'll see) the revenue-maximizing spot.

One straight line, every elasticity: $x = 24 - 2p$. Drag the price up and down the line and watch $\varepsilon$ sweep from $0$ (bottom) toward $-\infty$ (top) — same slope everywhere, totally different elasticity.

Two ways to compute: the point formula $\varepsilon = \tfrac{dx}{dp}\cdot\tfrac{p}{x}$ when you have the demand function; the arc (midpoint) formula $\varepsilon = \dfrac{\Delta x / \bar{x}}{\Delta p / \bar{p}}$ when you only have two observations. Same idea, calculus vs. data.

Plan of attack. Along a Line works the linear case and the midpoint rule. Constant Elasticity introduces the demand curve built from an elasticity, $x = Ap^{\varepsilon}$ — with a Cobb–Douglas payoff. Revenue & MR is the cash register: when do price changes raise revenue, and the $MR = p\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)$ formula that runs all of monopoly later. Income & Cross reuses Unit 2's signatures as elasticities. Then the Lab, practice, and the cheat sheet.

1

Elasticity along a straight line

The shape

$$x = a - bp \;\Longrightarrow\; \varepsilon(p) = \frac{-bp}{a - bp} \qquad \text{with } \varepsilon = -1 \text{ exactly at the midpoint } p = \frac{a}{2b}$$

The slope contributes a constant $-b$; the ratio $\tfrac{p}{x}$ does all the moving. High on the curve, $p$ is big and $x$ small $\Rightarrow$ elastic. Low on the curve, the reverse $\Rightarrow$ inelastic. The halfway point is always the unit-elastic pivot.

Worked example

Given
  • Demand $x = 24 - 2p$
  • Find $\varepsilon$ at $p = 3, 6, 9$ and classify each point. Then use the midpoint (arc) formula between $p = 4$ and $p = 6$ if quantities there are $x = 16$ and $x = 12$.
1

Point formula. $\dfrac{dx}{dp} = -2$, so $\varepsilon = \dfrac{-2p}{24 - 2p}$:

$$p=3:\ \varepsilon = \frac{-6}{18} = -\tfrac{1}{3} \text{ (inelastic)}, \quad p=6:\ \frac{-12}{12} = -1 \text{ (unit)}, \quad p=9:\ \frac{-18}{6} = -3 \text{ (elastic)}$$

2

Midpoint check. $a = 24$, $b = 2$: unit elasticity at $p = \tfrac{24}{4} = 6$ ✓ — matches the computation.

3

Arc formula between $(4, 16)$ and $(6, 12)$: $\bar{p} = 5$, $\bar{x} = 14$:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{arc}} = \frac{\Delta x/\bar{x}}{\Delta p/\bar{p}} = \frac{-4/14}{2/5} = -\frac{5}{7} \approx -0.71$$

An average elasticity over the stretch — sensibly between the point values at its endpoints ($-\tfrac{1}{2}$ at $p=4$ and $-1$ at $p=6$).

Your turn

Given
  • Demand $x = 40 - 4p$. Find $\varepsilon$ at $p = 2, 5, 8$, classify each, and state the revenue-maximizing price without computing revenue.

$\varepsilon = \dfrac{-4p}{40-4p} = \dfrac{-p}{10-p}$: at $p=2$, $-\tfrac{1}{4}$ (inelastic); at $p=5$, $-1$ (unit); at $p=8$, $-4$ (elastic).

Revenue peaks where $\varepsilon = -1$: $\boxed{p = 5}$ — the midpoint $\tfrac{a}{2b} = \tfrac{40}{8}$.

1

Demand built from an elasticity

The shape

$$x = A\,p^{\varepsilon} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dx}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{x} = \varepsilon A p^{\varepsilon - 1}\cdot\frac{p}{Ap^{\varepsilon}} = \varepsilon \qquad \text{(the same number at every price)}$$

Power-function demand wears its elasticity as its exponent — constant everywhere. Taking logs makes it a straight line, $\ln x = \ln A + \varepsilon \ln p$, which is exactly why empirical economists run regressions in logs: the slope coefficient is the elasticity.

Worked example — the Cobb–Douglas payoff

Given
  • The demand you derived in Unit 2.1: $x^*(p_x, m) = \dfrac{2m}{5p_x}$ (from $U = x^2y^3$)
  • Find its own-price elasticity, at every price.
1

Spot the power function. $x^* = \left(\tfrac{2m}{5}\right)p_x^{-1}$ — this is $Ap^{\varepsilon}$ with $A = \tfrac{2m}{5}$ and $\varepsilon = -1$.

2

Conclude. $\varepsilon = -1$ at every price and every income: Cobb–Douglas demand is always unit elastic. Consequence: spending on $x$ is $p_x x^* = \tfrac{2m}{5}$ — constant. A Cobb–Douglas consumer commits a fixed budget share and price changes only rescale the quantity, never the spending. (That's the share rule, rediscovered through elasticity.)

Your turn

Given
  • Demand $x = 100\,p^{-2}$. Find $\varepsilon$, classify, and predict what happens to revenue when price rises.

$\varepsilon = -2$ everywhere — elastic at every price. Elastic $\Rightarrow$ a price rise loses revenue. Check: $R = p \cdot 100p^{-2} = \dfrac{100}{p}$, strictly falling in $p$ ✓.

$$\boxed{\varepsilon = -2 \text{ everywhere; revenue falls as } p \text{ rises}}$$

1

The cash register: elasticity decides what a price change does to revenue

The idea

Revenue is $R = p \cdot x(p)$: a price change moves both factors in opposite directions, and elasticity referees the fight. Differentiate:

$$\frac{dR}{dp} = x + p\frac{dx}{dp} = x\left(1 + \varepsilon\right) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \begin{cases} |\varepsilon| < 1: & p\uparrow \text{ raises revenue} \\ |\varepsilon| = 1: & \text{revenue at its peak} \\ |\varepsilon| > 1: & p\uparrow ~\text{lowers revenue} \end{cases}$$

And the version every monopoly problem will use — revenue per extra unit sold rather than per price change:

$$MR \;=\; p\left(1 - \frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)$$

Worked example

Given
  • Demand $x = 24 - 2p$
  • Find the revenue-maximizing price, and evaluate $MR$ at $p = 9$, $p = 6$, and $p = 3$.
1

Revenue peak. Either maximize $R(p) = p(24-2p) = 24p - 2p^2$ with the FOC ($24 - 4p = 0 \Rightarrow p = 6$), or skip the calculus: revenue peaks where $\varepsilon = -1$, the line's midpoint $p = 6$. Peak revenue: $6 \times 12 = 72$.

2

MR at three points. Using $\varepsilon = -3, -1, -\tfrac{1}{3}$ from the Along a Line tab:

$$p=9:\ MR = 9\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}\right) = 6, \qquad p=6:\ MR = 6(1 - 1) = 0, \qquad p=3:\ MR = 3(1 - 3) = -6$$

Negative $MR$ on the inelastic stretch: selling one more unit reduces revenue. Remember this — it's why a monopolist will never choose to operate on the inelastic part of demand.

Real-world sighting: “good harvest, poor farmers.” Food demand is inelastic, so a bumper crop (quantity up, price down) slides the market down the inelastic region — and total farm revenue falls. Inelastic demand makes abundance a curse for sellers.

Your turn

Given
  • Demand $x = 30 - 3p$. Find the revenue-maximizing price, and $MR$ at $p = 8$.

Peak at the midpoint $p = \tfrac{30}{6} = 5$. At $p = 8$: $x = 6$, $\varepsilon = \tfrac{-24}{6} = -4$, so $MR = 8\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = 6$.

$$\boxed{p^{R\max} = 5, \qquad MR(8) = 6}$$

Same trick, other variables

Unit 2 recall first — the direction chart. The classification you already have answers a direction question: “when income rises, does he buy more or less of it?” Only the sign of $\tfrac{dx}{dm}$ matters:

SignNameMeaning
$\dfrac{dx}{dm} > 0$Normalincome up $\Rightarrow$ buy more of it
$\dfrac{dx}{dm} < 0$Inferiorincome up $\Rightarrow$ buy less of it (ramen, bus rides)

(Same idea for the other price: $\tfrac{dx}{dp_y} > 0$ meant substitutes, $\tfrac{dx}{dp_y} < 0$ complements.) That chart is all sign, no size — it can't say how much more or less. That's the elasticity chart's job.

Any “percent response to a percent change” is an elasticity. Two more matter constantly — and both are Unit 2 signatures wearing new clothes:

$$\text{income elasticity: } \eta = \frac{dx}{dm}\cdot\frac{m}{x} \qquad\qquad \text{cross-price elasticity: } \varepsilon_{xy} = \frac{dx}{dp_y}\cdot\frac{p_y}{x}$$
Sign / sizeNameUnit 2 callback
$\eta > 1$Luxury (normal, and then some)budget share rises with income
$0 < \eta < 1$Necessity (normal)bought at every income, but share falls
$\eta < 0$Inferiorthe income effect that fought substitution
$\varepsilon_{xy} > 0$Substitutespricier rival $\Rightarrow$ more $x$ (the moving cliff)
$\varepsilon_{xy} < 0$Complementspricier partner $\Rightarrow$ less $x$
$\varepsilon_{xy} = 0$IndependentCobb–Douglas's zero cross-price

Familiar benchmarks: Cobb–Douglas has $\eta = 1$ exactly (demand proportional to $m$ — every good a “borderline necessity”) and $\varepsilon_{xy} = 0$. Quasilinear has $\eta = 0$ for $x$ (the income effect you proved was zero). And the budget identity that answered “can all goods be inferior?” has an elasticity version: the share-weighted average of income elasticities equals $1$ — so not everything can be a luxury, and not everything inferior.

Two charts, two different questions. The Unit 2 chart asks which way? (a sign); the elasticity chart asks by what percent? (a unit-free size). They never disagree, because $\eta$ is just $\tfrac{dx}{dm}$ multiplied by $\tfrac{m}{x}$ — and $m$, $x$ are both positive, so multiplying by $\tfrac{m}{x} > 0$ can never flip the sign. Here's the overlap laid side by side:

Unit 2 verdict (direction)Elasticity verdict (size)What the elasticity adds
Normal  ($\tfrac{dx}{dm} > 0$)$\eta > 0$a split direction can't see: necessity ($0 < \eta < 1$) vs luxury ($\eta > 1$)
Inferior  ($\tfrac{dx}{dm} < 0$)$\eta < 0$same verdict, now with a percent attached
Substitutes  ($\tfrac{dx}{dp_y} > 0$)$\varepsilon_{xy} > 0$how hard a rival's price hike pushes buyers to $x$
Complements  ($\tfrac{dx}{dp_y} < 0$)$\varepsilon_{xy} < 0$how hard a partner's price hike drags $x$ down

So the only genuinely new line in the elasticity chart is $\eta = 1$ — the necessity/luxury border, where the budget share stops falling and starts rising. Everything else is the Unit 2 chart with a magnitude bolted on.

Worked example

Given
  • Complements demand from Unit 2.1: $x^* = \dfrac{2m}{2p_x + 3p_y}$, at $p_x = 3$, $p_y = 2$, $m = 60$
  • Find the income elasticity $\eta$ and the sign of the cross-price elasticity $\varepsilon_{xy}$.
1

Income elasticity. $x^*$ is proportional to $m$ (the prices only build the constant), so doubling $m$ doubles $x^*$: $\eta = 1$ exactly. Formally: $\dfrac{dx}{dm} = \dfrac{2}{2p_x+3p_y} = \dfrac{x}{m}$, so $\dfrac{dx}{dm}\cdot\dfrac{m}{x} = 1$.

2

Cross-price. $p_y$ sits in the denominator, so $\dfrac{dx}{dp_y} < 0 \Rightarrow \varepsilon_{xy} < 0$: complements, as the utility function promised. (At these numbers: $x^* = \tfrac{120}{12} = 10$; a $1\%$ rise in $p_y$ costs about $\tfrac{3p_y}{2p_x+3p_y} = \tfrac{1}{2}\%$ of $x$, so $\varepsilon_{xy} = -\tfrac{1}{2}$.)

Practice

Routine: write the demand function, compute $\tfrac{dx}{dp}$, multiply by $\tfrac{p}{x}$, then interpret. Classify before you conclude anything about revenue. Each problem is split into parts — work each part on paper before you check it.

1

Point elasticity. Demand is $x = 60 - 5p$. Compute $\varepsilon$ at $p = 2$, $p = 6$, and $p = 10$, and classify each.

a Write down the point-elasticity formula in calculus form. Hint: $\varepsilon$ has no units.

$\varepsilon = \dfrac{dx}{dp}\cdot\dfrac{p}{x}$. Elasticity is unitless, so the ratio must be the one that cancels the units of $\tfrac{dx}{dp}$ — that forces $\tfrac{p}{x}$, not $\tfrac{x}{p}$.

b Compute $\dfrac{dx}{dp}$. Does your answer depend on $p$?

$\dfrac{dx}{dp} = -5$ at every price — a straight line has constant slope. (Which is exactly why the slope alone can't be the elasticity: we need three different answers out of one number.)

c Write $\varepsilon$ as a function of $p$: substitute $x = 60 - 5p$ into the formula and simplify.

$\varepsilon(p) = -5\cdot\dfrac{p}{60-5p} = \dfrac{-5p}{60-5p} = \dfrac{-p}{12-p}$.

d Evaluate $\varepsilon$ at $p = 2, 6, 10$ and classify each point. Check: at what price should this line hit $\varepsilon = -1$?

$p=2$: $\tfrac{-2}{10} = -\tfrac{1}{5}$ (inelastic); $\;p=6$: $\tfrac{-6}{6} = -1$ (unit); $\;p=10$: $\tfrac{-10}{2} = -5$ (elastic). Check: the midpoint $\tfrac{a}{2b} = \tfrac{60}{10} = 6$ — unit elastic exactly there ✓.

2

Arc elasticity. Price rises from $4$ to $6$ and quantity falls from $20$ to $12$. Compute the arc (midpoint) elasticity.

a Why doesn't the point formula work here? Write down the formula that does.

There's no demand function to differentiate — just two observed points. Use the arc (midpoint) formula: $\varepsilon_{\text{arc}} = \dfrac{\Delta x / \bar{x}}{\Delta p / \bar{p}}$, with the averages as the base.

b Compute the midpoints $\bar{p}$ and $\bar{x}$.

$\bar{p} = \tfrac{4+6}{2} = 5$, $\quad \bar{x} = \tfrac{20+12}{2} = 16$.

c Compute the two percentage changes (watch the signs).

$\%\Delta x = \dfrac{-8}{16} = -0.5$  and  $\%\Delta p = \dfrac{+2}{5} = +0.4$.

d Compute $\varepsilon_{\text{arc}}$ and classify. Then compute revenue at both points to confirm the classification.

$\varepsilon_{\text{arc}} = \dfrac{-0.5}{0.4} = \boxed{-1.25}$ — elastic over this stretch, so the price rise should lose revenue. Check: $R$ went from $4\cdot 20 = 80$ to $6\cdot 12 = 72$ ✓.

3

Constant elasticity. Demand is $x = 100\,p^{-2}$. Find $\varepsilon$, then verify your revenue prediction by computing $R(p)$ explicitly.

a What type of function is this demand curve? What does that type tell you about $\varepsilon$?

It's a power function, $x = Ap^{\varepsilon}$ — the constant-elasticity family. The shortcut: the exponent on $p$ is the elasticity, the same at every price.

b Read off $\varepsilon$ and classify the demand.

$\varepsilon = -2$ everywhere: $|\varepsilon| = 2 > 1$, so demand is elastic at every price.

c If the seller raises the price, does revenue rise or fall? Why?

Elastic demand: a $1\%$ price rise costs about $2\%$ of quantity — the quantity loss beats the price gain, so revenue falls.

d Compute $R(p) = p \cdot x(p)$ and confirm your answer from part (c).

$R(p) = p\cdot 100p^{-2} = \dfrac{100}{p}$ — strictly decreasing in $p$ ✓. Every price rise loses revenue, exactly as $\varepsilon = -2$ promised.

4

Cobb–Douglas, all three elasticities. From $U = xy^3$: $x^* = \dfrac{m}{4p_x}$. Find the own-price elasticity, the income elasticity, and the cross-price elasticity with respect to $p_y$.

a Rewrite $x^*$ so that every variable appears as a power.

$x^* = \tfrac{1}{4}\, m^{+1}\, p_x^{-1}$ — and notice $p_y$ doesn't appear at all.

b Find the own-price elasticity $\varepsilon$. Hint: problem 3's shortcut.

$\varepsilon = -1$: unit elastic at every price. (That's why Cobb–Douglas spending on each good never moves — the revenue box has constant area.)

c Find the income elasticity $\eta$ and classify the good.

$\eta = +1$: demand is exactly proportional to income. Normal — and sitting right on the necessity/luxury border.

d Find $\varepsilon_{xy}$. Hint: look back at part (a) — where is $p_y$?

$p_y$ absent $\Rightarrow \dfrac{dx}{dp_y} = 0 \Rightarrow \varepsilon_{xy} = 0$: independent. The Cobb–Douglas triple $(-1, +1, 0)$ — worth memorizing.

5

Revenue & MR. Demand is $x = 30 - 3p$. Find the revenue-maximizing price and quantity, peak revenue, and $MR$ at $p = 8$ and at $p = 2$.

a Find the revenue-maximizing price without computing any revenue. Hint: where on a straight line is $\varepsilon = -1$?

At the midpoint, where $\varepsilon = -1$: $\;p^{R\max} = \dfrac{a}{2b} = \dfrac{30}{6} = 5$.

b Find the quantity there and the peak revenue.

$x = 30 - 15 = 15$, $\quad R = 5 \cdot 15 = 75$.

c Compute $\varepsilon$ at $p = 8$ and at $p = 2$, and classify each.

At $p=8$: $x = 6$, $\varepsilon = \dfrac{-24}{6} = -4$ (elastic). At $p=2$: $x = 24$, $\varepsilon = \dfrac{-6}{24} = -\tfrac{1}{4}$ (inelastic).

d Compute $MR = p\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)$ at both prices. What does the sign of each answer tell you?

$MR(8) = 8\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{4}\right) = 6 > 0$: elastic side, an extra unit adds revenue. $\;MR(2) = 2\left(1 - 4\right) = -6 < 0$.

Negative $MR$ at $p = 2$: on the inelastic stretch, selling more units lowers revenue — you give up too much price to move each extra unit.

6

Signs & classification. Three demand functions from Unit 2, all at the same numbers: $p_x = 3$, $p_y = 2$, $m = 60$. For each, find the elasticity (or its sign) and classify.

a Quasilinear: $x^* = \dfrac{9p_y^2}{p_x^2}$. Compute $x^*$ at the given numbers, then find the income elasticity $\eta$. Hint: did you use $m$ anywhere?

$x^* = \dfrac{9\cdot 4}{9} = 4$ — and $m$ never entered the calculation. $m$ absent $\Rightarrow \dfrac{dx}{dm} = 0 \Rightarrow \eta = 0$: the zero income effect you proved in Unit 2, sitting exactly on the normal/inferior border.

b Complements: $x^* = \dfrac{2m}{2p_x + 3p_y}$. Compute $x^*$ at the given numbers. Now raise $p_y$ from $2$ to $4$ and recompute. What is the sign of $\varepsilon_{xy}$?

At $p_y = 2$: $x^* = \dfrac{120}{6+6} = 10$. At $p_y = 4$: $x^* = \dfrac{120}{6+12} = \dfrac{120}{18} \approx 6.7$. A pricier $y$ dragged $x$ down: $\dfrac{dx}{dp_y} < 0 \Rightarrow \varepsilon_{xy} < 0$. Complements, as the utility function promised.

c Substitutes below the cliff: $x^* = \dfrac{m}{p_x}$. Compute $x^*$ at the given numbers. Now raise $p_y$ from $2$ to $3$: does $x^*$ change? What is $\varepsilon_{xy}$ on this stretch, and where does the substitutes behavior actually show up?

$x^* = \dfrac{60}{3} = 20$ — and it stays $20$ when $p_y$ moves to $3$, because $p_y$ isn't in the formula: $\varepsilon_{xy} = 0$ locally. But a big enough rise in $p_y$ can move the cliff and switch the corner: the substitutes signature lives at the threshold, not on the smooth stretch. (Subtle — and a favorite trick question.)

7

Reasoning. A streaming service is deciding whether a price cut will raise revenue.

a What single number settles the question?

The own-price elasticity of demand at the current price. Not the slope, not total subscribers — the unit-free $\varepsilon$ right where they're standing.

b State the decision rule, then explain in one sentence why it works.

A price cut raises revenue iff demand is elastic there ($|\varepsilon| > 1$): the percentage of customers gained exceeds the percentage of price given up, so the revenue box grows.

c Why can a straight-line demand curve never have the same $\varepsilon$ at two different prices? Hint: what happens to the ratio $\tfrac{p}{x}$ as you slide down the line?

On a line, $\varepsilon = -b\cdot\tfrac{p}{x}$ with $b$ fixed — and sliding down the curve, $p$ falls while $x$ rises, so $\tfrac{p}{x}$ strictly falls. $\varepsilon$ changes monotonically, so no value ever repeats: each elasticity happens exactly once.

★

Elasticity at a glance

Formulas.

$$\varepsilon = \frac{dx}{dp}\cdot\frac{p}{x} \qquad \varepsilon_{\text{arc}} = \frac{\Delta x/\bar{x}}{\Delta p/\bar{p}} \qquad \eta = \frac{dx}{dm}\cdot\frac{m}{x} \qquad \varepsilon_{xy} = \frac{dx}{dp_y}\cdot\frac{p_y}{x}$$
$$\frac{dR}{dp} = x(1+\varepsilon) \qquad\qquad MR = p\left(1 - \frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}\right)$$

Classification.

Own-priceNamePrice rise does what to revenue?
$|\varepsilon| > 1$ElasticLowers it ($MR > 0$)
$|\varepsilon| = 1$Unit elasticNothing — revenue at its peak ($MR = 0$)
$|\varepsilon| < 1$InelasticRaises it ($MR < 0$)
Other elasticitiesClassification
$\eta > 1$ / $0<\eta<1$ / $\eta < 0$Luxury / necessity / inferior
$\varepsilon_{xy} > 0$ / $< 0$ / $= 0$Substitutes / complements / independent

Special curves & benchmarks.

  • Linear $x = a - bp$: $\varepsilon = \dfrac{-bp}{a-bp}$ — sweeps from $0$ to $-\infty$; unit elastic at the midpoint $p = \tfrac{a}{2b}$, which is also the revenue peak.
  • Power $x = Ap^{r}$: $\varepsilon = r$ at every price; in logs it's a straight line with slope $r$.
  • Cobb–Douglas demand: the triple $(\varepsilon, \eta, \varepsilon_{xy}) = (-1, +1, 0)$; spending on each good is a fixed share of $m$.
  • Quasilinear demand: $\eta = 0$; complements: $\varepsilon_{xy} < 0$; substitutes: $\varepsilon_{xy} > 0$ (at the cliff).
  • Budget discipline: share-weighted income elasticities average to exactly $1$ — not everything can be a luxury; not everything can be inferior.
  • The bridge to Unit 4+: $MR = p(1 - \tfrac{1}{|\varepsilon|})$ — monopoly pricing is this formula plus $MR = MC$.
