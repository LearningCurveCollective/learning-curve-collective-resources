What this worksheet covers

Engineering studies say there are billions of dollars of energy-efficiency investments that pay for themselves — and people don't make them. That's the energy efficiency gap (or “paradox”). This paper asks three things: is the gap real or mis-measured? If real, is it a market failure or a behavioral failure? And what policy fits each answer?

Reading: Kenneth Gillingham & Karen Palmer (2014), “Bridging the Energy Efficiency Gap: Policy Insights from Economic Theory and Empirical Evidence,” Review of Environmental Economics and Policy, 8(1): 18–38 (RFF DP 13-02-REV).

The paper's thesis in one line: the gap has multiple explanations — some measurement error, some genuine market failure, some behavioral — and they differ by user and use, so policy should be tailored to the specific failure. Behavioral welfare economics isn't ready to justify big policies, but cheap nudges (like OPower's social-norms mailers) are worth pursuing.

The key: the gap is an NPV claim

Key — variables in the efficiency investment decision:

$\Delta C$  =  upfront cost premium for the efficient product over the standard one

$S$  =  annual energy-cost savings (engineering estimate: energy saved × price)

$T$  =  product lifetime in years

$r$  =  the discount rate used to value future savings

$r^*$  =  the implicit discount rate — the $r$ at which $NPV = 0$; if consumers reject the investment they “behave as if” $r > r^*$

$$NPV = -\Delta C + \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{S}{(1+r)^t} \qquad\qquad \text{Gap: } NPV > 0 \text{ at a reasonable } r, \text{ yet the investment isn't made}$$

Everything in the paper is a fight over this equation. Reasons the gap may be small say the engineer's $S$ is too high or $\Delta C$ is too low. Market failures say the equation is right but something blocks the purchase. Behavioral failures say the consumer's brain doesn't run the equation.

The externality (CO$_2$, energy security) is deliberately kept outside the gap (fn. 4): it's a reason to want more efficiency than the market delivers, but it's the first of Jaffe–Newell–Stavins's two failures, fixed by a Pigouvian price, not by anything on this page.

Common trap: a high implicit discount rate is evidence, not an explanation. “Consumers behave as if $r = 25\%$” is consistent with every lens above — mis-measured savings, a credit constraint, or present bias all produce it. The whole paper is about telling those apart.

1

Define the energy efficiency gap. Why is it important to understand it?

Definition. The energy efficiency gap (or “paradox”) is the failure of consumers and firms to make energy-saving investments that appear to have positive net present value — the present value of future energy savings exceeds the upfront cost, yet the investment isn't made. In the broader Jaffe & Stavins (1994) sense: energy-efficient products diffuse more slowly than would be socially optimal.

Evidence usually cited for it: consumers behave as if they apply very high implicit discount rates to future savings (Hausman 1979; Train 1985); vehicle buyers appear to “undervalue” future fuel savings (Allcott & Wozny 2012; Helfand & Wolverton 2011); and engineering studies (McKinsey 2009) find huge “negative-cost” abatement — 835 Mt CO$_2$e by 2030 at a net saving of $45 billion.

Why it matters. (1) Policy rests on it. Absent a carbon price, efficiency standards and incentives are the main climate tool in most countries, and their regulatory analyses (e.g., NHTSA's fuel-economy rule) claim fuel savings exceed costs — free CO$_2$ reductions. If the gap is mis-measured, those benefits are overstated. (2) The explanation dictates the fix. If the gap is measurement error, no policy is needed; if it's a market failure, fix the failure; if it's behavioral, nudges or standards may be justified on efficiency grounds. (3) It's the door to behavioral economics in environmental policy — the question of whether observed choices reveal true preferences.

The gap is money left on the table: an efficient fridge costs $300 more but saves $60 a year for ten years, and people still buy the cheap one. Economists find that puzzling — it looks like people are turning down a 15% return. It matters because governments justify efficiency rules by saying they pay for themselves; if that's wrong, the rules cost more than advertised. And why people leave the money there decides what, if anything, to do about it.

2

Discuss each of the five reasons why the energy efficiency gap may be small.

The premise: many economists trust consumer choices over engineering calculations. If the engineer omits costs or models the decision wrongly, the net benefits are overstated and the gap shrinks or vanishes (Metcalf & Hassett 1999; Smith & Moore 2010). Five ways that happens:

1. Hidden costs. Program administration, the time cost of finding and installing a reliable efficient product, and — most important — opportunity costs: lower-quality energy service (harsher light, a smaller or slower car), or scarce managerial attention diverted from bigger projects. Anderson & Newell (2004): roughly half of energy-audit recommendations with very short paybacks were rejected, and managers cited economic reasons (mostly opportunity costs) for up to 93% of rejections. Hard to measure; case by case.

2. Consumer heterogeneity. A product that pays off for the average user may not for a particular one — different usage, preferences, or borrowing costs. The summer-home air conditioner used three weeks a year shouldn't be the efficient model. Bento, Li & Roth (2012): heterogeneity in preferences biases studies toward finding undervaluation, because people who value fuel savings self-select into efficient products (selection bias).

3. Uncertainty. Efficiency investments are irreversible and energy prices fluctuate; if prices fall, the return falls. Hassett & Metcalf (1993) / Metcalf (1994): accounting for uncertainty raises the required return four- or five-fold — an option value of waiting (Dixit & Pindyck). Caveats: Sanstad et al. say option value can't explain all the high implicit rates; Baker (2012) shows the result breaks with multiple efficiency levels. Still, risk is a common reason firms reject audit recommendations.

4. Overestimated energy savings. Engineering models ignore interactions (efficient lighting reduces the heat a furnace would have to replace… or increases it), and assume perfect installation and maintenance. Dubin, Miedema & Chandran (1986), Florida RCT: engineering estimates overstated returns by 8–13%.

5. Rebound effect. Engineering estimates assume energy-service demand is constant, but cheaper usage means more usage. Two-sided: the extra use raises welfare (so engineering estimates understate efficient diffusion), but if rebound is ignored, estimates of cost-effective energy savings are biased upward.

Five ways the engineer's spreadsheet flatters the deal. Hidden costs: the LED light is uglier, the efficient car is slower, and you spent a weekend researching it. Heterogeneity: the math works for the average person, not for you if you barely use the thing. Uncertainty: gas might get cheaper, and you can't un-buy the car — waiting has value. Overestimated savings: lab conditions vs. your leaky house. Rebound: you'll run the efficient A/C more, so the bill drops less than promised. Add these up and the “free money” may not be free.

3

Discuss each of the five market-failure explanations for the gap. How do they relate to the market failures in Jaffe, Newell & Stavins's “Tale of Two Market Failures”?

The premise: even after the measurement corrections, several genuine market failures say the gap is real — the consumer is rational, but the market keeps the deal from happening.

1. Imperfect (and asymmetric) information. Buyers can't verify the savings; sellers may know more but can't credibly convey it — a lemons-type failure. It also amplifies perceived risk, helping explain why firms cite “project risk” when rejecting audit recommendations.

2. Principal–agent (split incentives). One party decides, another pays or benefits. Landlord pays heat, tenant sets the thermostat; or landlord picks the appliances, tenant pays the bills but can't observe efficiency at lease signing. Evidence of higher energy use / lower efficiency in rentals (Davis 2012; Gillingham, Harding & Rapson 2012), though the losses were relatively small. Also inside organizations (whoever pays the bills isn't whoever buys equipment) — widely discussed, never quantified.

3. Credit (liquidity) constraints. High upfront costs plus limited borrowing. Credit rationing arises when lenders can't tell good from bad risks; in efficiency, lenders don't know which projects have big savings payoffs (which would lower default risk), so high-payoff projects by poor-credit borrowers go unfunded (Palmer, Walls & Gerarden 2012).

4. Learning-by-using. Early users generate knowledge about how to use a technology that spills over to later adopters, so early adopters have too little incentive relative to the social optimum — a positive externality. Simulated (Mulder et al. 2003) but no empirical evidence for efficiency technologies.

5. Regulatory failures. Regulated electricity prices differ from marginal cost. If price < MC, that discourages efficiency; but consumers face flat, time-invariant prices while MC varies — and for most hours price is above MC (Brennan 2011), so better pricing might reduce demand for efficiency. On net, probably not an important explanation.

Link to Jaffe, Newell & Stavins JNS's “two market failures” are (i) the environmental externality and (ii) failures in the market for technology innovation and diffusion — knowledge spillovers, adoption externalities like learning-by-using, imperfect information, and split incentives. G&P's five are all instances of JNS's second failure: they are the diffusion-side reasons an efficient technology spreads too slowly. G&P deliberately keep JNS's first failure out of the gap (fn. 4): the externality is real and justifies wanting more efficiency, but its benefits are analyzed separately, so it isn't counted as part of the “gap.” JNS's lesson — two failures need two instruments — reappears in G&P's Q5 answer.

Here the consumer is smart and the deal is real — something else gets in the way. Information: you can't tell whether the savings claim is true. Split incentives: the landlord buys the fridge, you pay the electric bill — she has no reason to buy the good one. Credit: you'd love the efficient furnace but can't borrow $3,000. Learning-by-using: whoever goes first figures out the quirks for everyone else and gets no reward for it. Regulated prices: the electricity price is set by a commission, not the market — though this one probably cuts the other way. Jaffe, Newell & Stavins said there are two problems: pollution isn't priced, and good technology spreads too slowly. G&P's five are all about the second problem.

4

Discuss each of the behavioral-economics explanations for the gap: self-control problems, reference dependence (loss aversion), limited attention, framing of choices.

Two distinctions to state first. A behavioral anomaly is any departure from neoclassical assumptions (stable self-interested preferences, full information, correct processing). A behavioral failure is an anomaly that creates a systematic gap between decision utility (what you maximize at the moment of choice) and experienced utility (what you actually get later). Only failures justify policy. To explain the gap, an anomaly must be biased toward buying less efficient products. G&P sort anomalies (following DellaVigna 2009) into nonstandard preferences, nonstandard beliefs, and nonstandard decision-making.

Self-control problems nonstandard preference · a failure by definition Time-inconsistent preferences: you take the long view about distant outcomes, but as the moment approaches your discount rate jumps — the same mechanism behind unkept plans to exercise or quit smoking. Modeled with quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$–$\delta$) discounting (Laibson 1997; O'Donoghue & Rabin 1999) or Gul–Pesendorfer temptation preferences. Efficiency investments are exactly this shape — pay now, benefit later. Tsvetanov & Segerson (2013) find household refrigerator choices consistent with self-control problems.

Reference-dependent preferences / loss aversion nonstandard preference · maybe not a failure Utility depends on outcomes relative to a reference point, and losses loom larger than equal gains (Tversky & Kahneman 1981). Greene, German & Delucchi (2009): a buyer uncertain about fuel prices, actual efficiency, and miles driven weighs the downside so heavily that an expected-positive efficient car gets rejected. But no empirical test, and loss aversion doesn't clearly separate decision from experienced utility, so it may not be a failure. (Gal 2006: inertia can mimic it.)

Limited attention nonstandard decision-making · could be a failure Bounded rationality (Simon 1955): people process only a subset of information and underweight what is less salient. Evidence outside energy: register sales taxes less salient than posted taxes (Chetty et al. 2009); left-digit odometer effects on used-car prices (Lacetera et al. 2012); shipping costs added at checkout (Hossain & Morgan 2006). In energy: Turrentine & Kurani (2007) found car buyers think about fuel savings crudely, not as a present value. Inattention to future fuel costs is the heart of the “undervaluation” argument (Allcott, Mullainathan & Taubinsky 2012) — but not yet demonstrated empirically.

Framing of choices nonstandard decision-making · could be a failure Presentation format changes choices by directing attention to different subsets of the information (Benartzi & Thaler 2002; Duflo et al. 2006). Regulators already act on this: the redesigned EPA fuel-economy label (2010) was built to head off anomalies in how buyers process the numbers.

For completeness (also in the paper): nonstandard beliefs — systematically wrong expectations about savings; Allcott (2012) finds beliefs are inaccurate but not clearly biased, and if people never learn they were wrong it's unclear it's a failure. Suboptimal heuristics — rules of thumb when there are many variants (cars); no energy evidence yet.

Now the deal is real and the market is fine — the problem is the buyer's brain. Self-control: “I'll buy the efficient one next time” — the same voice that says the diet starts Monday. Loss aversion: the $300 premium is a sure loss today; the savings are a maybe — and losses hurt about twice as much as gains feel good, so you pass. Limited attention: the sticker price is in giant font; the ten years of electric bills are a calculation nobody does. Framing: the exact same numbers, shown differently, produce different choices — which is why the EPA redesigned the fuel-economy label. The key test for policy: does the person later regret the choice? If yes, it's a failure worth fixing; if not, it's just how they like to decide.

5

What types of policies address market and behavioral failures, and how should they be designed? How do these recommendations compare with Jaffe, Newell & Stavins? Describe how three different policies have fared empirically.

Design principle: tailor the policy to the specific failure, and assess benefits and costs as fully as possible. There isn't always a clean first-best.

Behavioral failures Policy is warranted only when decision utility ≠ experienced utility. Then the logic is: information first, and if information doesn't work, change the choice setting (a nudge) — libertarian paternalism (Thaler & Sunstein): keep every option available but set defaults, labels, and framing so people make ex-post “good” decisions. Examples: make the efficient option the default; frame choices around losses when loss aversion is the failure; labels and certification for inattention and heuristics; social-norms messaging. The hard part is matching the specific intervention to the specific failure. When neither information nor a nudge is feasible, fall back to second-best tools — standards and subsidies — which misfire under heterogeneity: standards force over-investment by low-use consumers (Hausman & Joskow 1982); subsidies over-pay consumers who already optimize (deadweight loss). Fix: behavioral targeting / “asymmetric paternalism” — aim the policy at the people most likely to misoptimize, big help to them, little harm to everyone else (Allcott, Mullainathan & Taubinsky 2012; Camerer et al. 2003).

vs. Jaffe, Newell & Stavins Same philosophy: one instrument per failure. JNS argued that the environmental externality needs a price and the technology-diffusion failures need their own tools (information, adoption incentives, R&D support) — a portfolio, because a carbon price alone won't fix slow diffusion and technology policy alone won't fix unpriced pollution. G&P keep that structure for market failures (Pigouvian price for the externality; information / financing / subsidy for diffusion failures) and extend it with a third category JNS didn't have: behavioral failures, addressed by nudges and targeting. Both papers warn that blunt standards and subsidies are second-best.

Empirical record of three policies

1. Information strategies. Alone, audits and tips have limited effects on consumption (Stern; Abrahamse et al.) — though industrial audits got about half of recommendations adopted (Anderson & Newell). Labels: EnergyGuide, fuel-economy labels, city building-disclosure rules. Energy Star certification may have positive net benefits but crowds out other energy-saving activity (Houde 2013). The standout is social norms: OPower's peer-comparison mailers cut consumption ~2% on average — equivalent to an 11–20% price increase (Allcott 2011); effects vary with political ideology (Costa & Kahn) and are largest for heavy users (Ayres et al.).

2. Economic incentives. Rebates, tax credits, low-cost loans, feebates (Canada, France). Problems: funded by distortionary taxes; cause rebound; free riders (would have bought anyway) vs. free drivers (bought because the rebate raised awareness). Evidence mixed: utility programs cost more per kWh saved than ex-ante estimates (Loughran & Kulick; Auffhammer et al.; Arimura et al.), though the differences aren't statistically significant; Rivers & Jaccard find no effect of program spending on Canadian demand growth.

3. Efficiency standards. Second-best but the most politically feasible: appliance standards, CAFE, building codes. Appliance-standard evidence is “remarkably thin” — ex-ante engineering models ignoring lost consumer surplus and rebound. CAFE: studies without behavioral failures find large welfare losses; Jacobsen (2013) — a gasoline tax achieves the same at about one-sixth the welfare cost; but with enough behavioral failures CAFE can raise efficiency (Allcott et al.; Parry, Evans & Oates). Building codes: mixed — no effect (Jaffe & Stavins 1995) vs. 3–5% (Aroonruengsawat et al.) and ~4% (Jacobsen & Kotchen) savings; Costa & Kahn find effects only after 1983 in California.

G&P's bottom line: behavioral welfare economics isn't ready to justify current or proposed policies — but where nudges like OPower's are available, “we see no reason not to pursue them.”

Match the tool to the problem. Pollution → price it. Missing information → provide it. Landlord–tenant → help them write a better lease. Can't borrow → lend. Early adopters help everyone → pay them. Brain glitches → first tell people, then rearrange the menu (defaults, labels, loss-framing) so the good choice is the easy one, without banning anything. Only if that fails do you reach for the blunt tools — mandates and rebates — and aim them at the people who actually make mistakes. That's Jaffe, Newell & Stavins's “two problems, two tools” idea with a third problem (behavior) added.

Scorecard: information alone is weak, except the “your neighbors use less” letters, which work about as well as a 15% price hike. Rebates are hit-or-miss and pay a lot of people who'd have bought anyway. Standards are popular but a gas tax does the same job for a sixth of the cost — unless people are making real mistakes, in which case standards start to look better.
