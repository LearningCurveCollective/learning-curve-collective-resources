The key: the abatement market

Stavins's arguments (and the exam graphs) all live in one diagram — the market for emissions. Note the change from Fullerton: the demand curve is now the marginal abatement cost curve.

Key — variables and curves:

$E$  =  emissions (tons of CO$_2$), on the x-axis. Reading right-to-left is abatement.

$E^0$  =  business-as-usual emissions (no policy), where $MAC = 0$

$MAC(E)$  =  marginal abatement cost — cost of cutting one more ton. Rises as emissions fall, so it slopes down in $E$. It doubles as the firms' demand for the right to emit.

$MD(E)$  =  marginal damage of one more ton (the externality). Slopes up. Equivalently, the marginal benefit of abatement.

$E^*$  =  efficient emissions, where $MAC = MD$

$t^*$  =  efficient tax $= MD(E^*) = MAC(E^*)$; also the equilibrium allowance price when the cap is $E^*$

$$\text{Efficiency: } MAC(E^*) = MD(E^*) \qquad\quad \text{Cost-effectiveness: } MAC_1 = MAC_2 = \cdots = MAC_n = p$$

G1

GLS 17 — Explain what negative externalities are.

A negative externality is an unintended cost of production or consumption that falls on a third party who is not compensated — Stavins's phrasing: an unintentional consequence that reduces another agent's profits or utility (Pigou 1920). Pollution is the textbook case.

Why it's a market failure: the emitter faces only its private cost of polluting, not the damage $MD$ to others, so it emits to $E^0$ where $MAC = 0$. Society would prefer $E^*$ where $MAC = MD$. The gap $E^0 - E^*$ is the overshoot, and the triangle between $MD$ and $MAC$ over that range is the deadweight loss.

Arrow's (1969) framing: an externality is a missing market — nobody sells clean air, so its price is zero. Both policy fixes (Pigou's tax, Coase's property rights) work by supplying the missing price.

G2

GLS 17 — Explain the Coasean approach and why property rights are central to the Coasean solution.

The reframe. Coase (1960) sees pollution not as a tax problem but as a property-rights problem: clean air is a resource nobody owns, so nobody can sell it or buy it. If the rights to a resource are clearly defined and tradable, private parties will bargain to the efficient allocation on their own.

The Coase theorem: with (1) well-defined property rights, (2) low transaction costs, and (3) few enough parties to bargain, the efficient outcome $E^*$ emerges regardless of who initially holds the rights. If victims hold the right, the polluter pays for permission to emit up to $E^*$; if the polluter holds the right, victims pay it to cut back to $E^*$. The assignment changes who pays whom — the distribution — not the quantity.

Why property rights are central: without a defined, enforceable, transferable right there is nothing to bargain over. The right is the missing market. This is the direct ancestor of cap and trade: Crocker (1966) and Dales (1968) proposed that the government designate a total allowable quantity, hand out rights adding up to that total, and let sources trade them — a market solution to a property-rights problem, with the regulator only setting the cap.

Limits: for a global pollutant like CO$_2$ with billions of “victims,” pure Coasean bargaining fails on transaction costs — which is exactly why the cap-and-trade design substitutes a government-created market for private negotiation.

1

Explain the theoretical foundation of taxes (negative externalities) and tradable permits (Coasean solution and property rights).

Taxes ← Pigou Pollution is a negative externality. For 40 years after Pigou (1920) the only prescribed fix was to tax the externality: charge each unit of emissions a tax equal to marginal social damage at the efficient level of control, $t^* = MD(E^*)$. Firms then cut emissions until their marginal abatement cost equals the tax, $MAC_i = t^*$. Because every source faces the same $t^*$, marginal abatement costs are equalized across sources — the necessary condition for cost-effectiveness — and the incentive persists over time, driving diffusion and innovation of cheaper abatement technology.

Permits ← Coase Coase (1960) reframed pollution as a public-good / incomplete-property-rights problem. If clean air were property whose rights could be traded, private actors would allocate it cost-effectively. Crocker (1966) and Dales (1968) turned this into transferable discharge permits: the regulator sets the total quantity (the cap), distributes rights summing to that total, and lets sources trade until each one abates to the point where $MAC_i = $ the market allowance price. The environmental constraint is met and marginal costs are again equalized.

$$\text{Tax: fix } p = t^*,\ \text{let } E \text{ adjust} \qquad\qquad \text{Cap: fix } E = E^*,\ \text{let } p \text{ emerge}$$

Shared roots. Both are “missing markets” (Arrow 1969), and in the carbon context both would most likely be applied upstream — on the carbon content of coal, oil, and gas as they enter the economy — covering ~98% of U.S. CO$_2$ with a few thousand compliance entities. A tax prices carbon directly with quantity adjusting; an upstream cap constrains quantity with the price emerging from the allowance market. Stavins's point: derived from different theories, the instruments are nonetheless more similar than different.

2

List the six similarities of taxes and permits and discuss at least two.

The list (§2.1): (1) emission reductions, (2) abatement costs, (3) possibilities for raising revenue, (4) costs to regulated firms (with revenue-raising versions), (5) distributional impacts, (6) competitiveness effects. Table 2 grades #1, #2, #6 perfectly equivalent; #3 nearly equivalent; #4, #5 similar.

1. Emission reductions perfectly equivalent — Absent uncertainty, setting the time path of the tax rate or of the cap achieves the same reductions. Either can be applied upstream, midstream, or downstream; upstream on fossil-fuel carbon content captures up to 98% of U.S. CO$_2$ with only a few thousand compliance entities instead of hundreds of millions of tailpipes and smokestacks.

2. Abatement costs perfectly equivalent — Competitive firms minimize abatement cost + tax bill (or + allowance cost), so each abates until $MAC = t^*$ or $= $ allowance price. Every firm controls at the same marginal cost, so aggregate cost is minimized either way. Innovation incentives are equivalent for a tax and an auctioned cap (Milliman & Prince 1989), and offsets can be bolted onto either.

3. Raising revenue nearly equivalent — A tax raises revenue that can cut distortionary taxes on income and capital, lowering the policy's net social cost (Bovenberg & Goulder 1996). An auction does the same. The gap is implementation: U.S. committee jurisdictions make revenue recycling harder to attach to cap and trade; globally ~70% of auction revenue is earmarked for green spending vs. ~72% of tax revenue recycled or sent to general funds.

4. Costs to regulated firms (revenue-raising versions) similar — Cost depends on revenue use: vs. lump-sum rebates, recycling through payroll / individual income / corporate tax cuts lowers net cost ~15% / 26% / 67% (Goulder & Hafstead 2018). Auctioned cap and trade shows only “slight differences.” Free allocation is costlier (8% above rebates, 200% above corporate-tax recycling) — but a tax can mimic free allocation via inframarginal exemptions.

5. Distributional impacts similar — Auctioned permits look like a tax to firms; a tax with tradable exemptions for the first $X$ tons looks like free allocation. Household effects split into use-side (prices of goods: regressive) and source-side (wages, capital returns: progressive, since carbon-intensive industries are capital-intensive). Source-side dominates, so carbon pricing with rate-cut recycling is progressive overall; lump-sum rebates are more progressive but less cost-effective.

6. Competitiveness / leakage perfectly equivalent — Any serious carbon price shifts comparative advantage in energy-intensive trade-exposed sectors to jurisdictions without one. Identical under both; mitigated by border tax adjustments (tax) or an import allowance requirement plus output-based updating allocation (cap). Leakage through world energy markets (lower fuel prices abroad) survives either way.

§4 summary: tax clearly favored on #8, #3, #2; cap favored on #7 and possibly #1; #4 and #5 turn out not to matter much in the U.S.; neither instrument dominates.

1. Uncertainty — Weitzman (1974): benefit uncertainty alone doesn't matter; cost uncertainty does, through relative slopes. Steep $MAC$ (small abatement changes = big cost swings) favors a price instrument; steep $MD$ (small emission changes = big damage swings) favors a quantity instrument. Newell & Pizer (2003): CO$_2$ is a stock pollutant, so any one period's $MD$ is flat → tax wins. But Stavins (1996) / Karp & Traeger (2018): technological change makes cost and benefit uncertainty positively correlated, which pushes back toward quantities — the case for taxes under uncertainty is “much weaker than commonly believed,” and calibrated models often favor the cap.

2. Price volatility — A tax eliminates short-run price swings by construction; under a cap, prices are endogenous and volatile (recession, weather, fuel prices). Volatility deters capital investment and R&D and erodes political support. Letting emissions of a stock pollutant vary with the business cycle is economically sensible — it happens automatically with a tax, but a cap needs banking and borrowing (turning an annual cap into a cumulative one). Tax favored.

3. Complementary policies — When a renewable portfolio standard or fuel standard targets sources under a cap: (i) zero net emission reduction (100% leakage to other capped sectors — the “waterbed effect”), (ii) higher aggregate cost because $MAC$s are no longer equal, (iii) suppressed allowance prices that dull innovation. California 2019: AB-32 allowances ~$16/ton vs. LCFS credits ~$200/ton. Under a tax the extra policy does cut emissions further (no leakage, no price suppression), though it's still not cost-effective. Tax favored.

7. Linkage — Allowances are a natural unit of exchange, so caps link easily (and linking thickens markets, cuts costs, reduces market power, signals momentum). Linking a tax to a foreign cap is possible but far harder. Cap favored.

8. Complexity — The simplest cap needs allowance tracking, auctions, anti-fraud rules; the simplest tax rides on existing fuel-excise machinery. Tax favored — with the caveat that real tax bills get complicated in the legislature too.

6. Macro factors (for completeness) — Growth: cap holds emissions, price rises; tax holds price, emissions rise. Recession: cap is counter-cyclical (price falls, emissions fixed); tax keeps taxing every residual ton. Inflation: cap's real price is constant; a unit tax erodes. Exogenous technological progress: cap → lower price, same emissions; tax → same price, fewer emissions.

4

Explain, with an example, how specific design elements of either a tax or a permit system make it more similar to — or more different from — the other.

The principle: “differences in specific design elements dominate fundamental differences between the instruments themselves.” Each instrument's signature weakness can be patched with a feature borrowed from the other, sliding it along a policy continuum (Weisbach 2010).

Cap → more tax-like

• Auctioning allowances — reproduces the tax's revenue and its cost profile to firms. Example: RGGI and California moved steadily toward auctioning; with 100% auctioning and the same recycling, household distribution is essentially identical to a tax.

• Price collar (floor + ceiling / safety valve) — caps the price swings that are the cap's main weakness. Example: RGGI's $10/ton safety valve and California's >$50 reserve trigger; California's post-2020 hard ceiling sells unlimited allowances at an escalating price — at that point it behaves like a tax above the ceiling.

• Banking and borrowing — turn an annual cap into a cumulative one, giving the intertemporal flexibility a tax has automatically. Example: banking drove a large share of gains in the SO$_2$ program; its absence produced RECLAIM's spikes and the EU ETS collapse.

Tax → more cap-like

• Tradable inframarginal exemptions — tax only emissions above a threshold; the exempt tons are economically identical to freely allocated allowances (Goulder & Schein 2013), protecting firm profits the way grandfathering does.

• Quantity-adjustment formula / periodic review — if emissions overshoot a target, the tax rate ratchets up automatically (Hafstead, Metcalf & Williams 2017) or on review (Aldy 2018). This imports the cap's environmental certainty.

• Dedicating revenue to mitigation — spend tax revenue on extra abatement when the tax under-delivers on quantity.

Design that makes them more different

• Free allocation pushes a cap away from a tax: no revenue, higher social cost (possibly negative net benefits — Parry, Williams & Goulder 1999), but much more political support.

• No banking, thin offset limits, one-year compliance periods maximize a cap's volatility, the very thing that distinguishes it from a tax.

One-sentence takeaway: a price collar makes a cap a hybrid; a quantity-adjusting formula makes a tax a hybrid — so the dichotomy is really a choice of design elements, and design is “possibly more important than the choice between the two instruments.”

Illustrate in a graph and discuss similarities #1 (emission reductions), #2 (abatement costs), and #4 (costs to regulated firms with revenue-raising instruments).

#1 — Same emissions, either way
#2 — Same (minimized) abatement cost
#4a — Firm's cost under a tax
#4b — Firm's cost under auctioned cap

#1 Emission reductions. Draw $MAC$ and $MD$; mark $E^*$ at the crossing. A tax set at $t^* = MD(E^*)$ is a horizontal line; firms slide down $MAC$ until $MAC = t^*$ — landing at $E^*$. A cap set at $E^*$ is a vertical line; the allowance market clears where $MAC$ hits the cap — at price $t^*$. Same point, reached from different directions. Discuss: this holds absent uncertainty and requires the regulator to set commensurate time paths; both can be applied upstream to cover ~98% of CO$_2$.

#2 Abatement costs. The two-firm box: $MAC_1$ rises from the left, $MAC_2$ rises from the right, total abatement is the width. A single price $p$ — whether a tax or an allowance price — sets $MAC_1 = MAC_2 = p$, which minimizes total cost (equimarginal principle). A uniform standard splits abatement evenly and wastes the shaded triangle. Discuss: firms minimize (abatement cost + tax) or (abatement cost + net allowance purchases); both lead to the same first-order condition, so aggregate cost is identical. Innovation incentives are also equivalent for a tax vs. an auctioned cap.

#4 Costs to regulated firms. Under the tax, a firm pays the abatement triangle (area under $MAC$ from $E^*$ to $E^0$) plus the tax rectangle $t^* \times E^*$ on its remaining emissions. Under an auctioned cap it pays the same triangle plus the same rectangle — now as allowance purchases. The pictures are identical. Discuss: the total burden then hinges on what government does with the rectangle — recycling via payroll / income / corporate tax cuts lowers net cost ~15% / 26% / 67% versus lump-sum rebates, with only “slight differences” between the tax and auctioned-cap cases. If instead allowances are given away, the rectangle stays with firms (their cost drops, society's rises) — but a tax can replicate that with inframarginal exemptions.

6

Illustrate in a graph and discuss differences #1 (performance under uncertainty) and #2 (carbon price volatility).

Difference #1 — Weitzman's relative-slopes rule. The regulator sets policy on the expected $MAC$ (dashed); the true $MAC$ turns out higher. Each panel shows the deadweight loss of each instrument.

Panel A — Steep $MD$, flat $MAC$ → cap wins
Panel B — Flat $MD$, steep $MAC$ → tax wins

Difference #2 — Price volatility. $MAC$ shifts with the business cycle. A cap holds $E$ fixed and lets the price swing; a tax holds the price and lets $E$ swing.

Setting up Weitzman. The regulator sets $t^*$ or $\bar E$ where expected $MAC$ crosses $MD$. Reality: $MAC$ is higher than expected (abatement is harder than thought). Under the tax, firms respond to the fixed $t^*$ by abating less — emissions rise to $E_{tax}$, past the true optimum $E^{*}_{true}$; the DWL is the triangle between $MD$ and true $MAC$ over that range. Under the cap, emissions stay at $\bar E$ (below the true optimum), price spikes, and the DWL is the triangle between true $MAC$ and $MD$ over $[\bar E, E^{*}_{true}]$.

Reading the panels. Panel A: $MD$ is steep, so overshooting the quantity is very costly — the tax's triangle is big, the cap's is small → quantity instrument wins. Panel B: $MAC$ is steep, so being locked into a quantity is very costly — the cap's triangle is big → price instrument wins. Rule: compare $|{\rm slope}\,MAC|$ to $|{\rm slope}\,MD|$; benefit uncertainty alone doesn't affect the ranking.

Applying it to carbon. Newell & Pizer (2003): CO$_2$ is a stock pollutant, so one year's emissions barely move concentrations — the current-period $MD$ is nearly flat (Panel B) → tax. But Stavins (1996) showed that positively correlated benefit and cost uncertainty tilts toward quantities, and Karp & Traeger (2018) argue technological change creates exactly that correlation (cheaper abatement now lowers future stocks and today's marginal damage). Their calibrated model often favors the cap. Net: the ranking is ambiguous and empirical — the case for taxes under uncertainty “is much weaker than commonly believed.” (Stavins files this under differences because of how recent that result is.)

Volatility (short run, separate from long-run uncertainty). Draw $MAC_{boom}$ and $MAC_{recession}$. With a cap at $\bar E$, the allowance price bounces between $p_{hi}$ and $p_{lo}$; with a tax at $t^*$, price is flat and emissions bounce between $E_{lo}$ and $E_{hi}$. Volatility is a real cost: it deters capital investment and R&D and erodes political support. Note the flip side — letting emissions of a stock pollutant fall in a recession is good economics (counter-cyclical), and a tax does it automatically; a cap needs banking/borrowing (or a collar) to mimic it. Tax favored on this criterion.

7

Illustrate in a graph and discuss how hybrid instruments are designed and how the design elements improve on the “weakness” of each instrument.

Hybrid cap — price collar (floor + ceiling)
Hybrid tax — quantity-adjusting rate

Diagnose the weaknesses first. The cap's weakness is price uncertainty (volatility, investment chill). The tax's weakness is quantity uncertainty (no guarantee of hitting the target). A hybrid borrows the other instrument's strength to patch the weakness.

Hybrid cap = price collar. The allowance “supply” becomes a step: horizontal at the floor (an auction reserve price — government sells nothing below it), vertical at the cap, horizontal again at the ceiling (safety valve — government releases reserve allowances at that price). If $MAC$ shifts high, firms buy reserve allowances at the ceiling instead of bidding the price to the sky — emissions exceed the cap a little, but cost is contained. If $MAC$ shifts low, price stops at the floor and some allowances go unsold — emissions fall below the cap. Real examples: RGGI (~$10 safety valve, now a floor + reserve), California (>$50 reserve trigger; post-2020 hard ceiling with unlimited allowances at an escalating price). Tradeoff: the collar sacrifices the environmentalists' favorite feature, quantity certainty — unless the reserve comes from within the long-run budget (borrowing from future caps), which keeps cumulative emissions unchanged and is appropriate for a stock pollutant.

Banking and borrowing are the other tax-like patch: they redefine the cap as cumulative, so firms smooth abatement over time and prices stop spiking (RECLAIM) or collapsing (EU ETS).

Hybrid tax = quantity-adjusting rate. Set a target $E_T$ and a starting rate $t_1$. If realized emissions exceed the target (true $MAC$ higher than expected), a formula or periodic review ratchets the rate up to $t_2$ until $MAC(E_T) = t_2$. Variants: an automatic formula (Hafstead, Metcalf & Williams 2017), scheduled reviews (Aldy 2018), or dedicating revenue to extra mitigation. Tradeoff: some price certainty is given up to buy quantity certainty.

Both patches move the instrument toward the middle of the policy continuum: a collared cap and a self-adjusting tax are near-twins. That's the design-over-instrument thesis in a picture.

8

List, discuss, and (where appropriate) illustrate key lessons from the global experience with taxes and permits.

Cap-and-trade lessons (§3.1) Drawn from the U.S. lead phasedown (1982–87), SO$_2$ trading (1995–2010), NO$_x$ Budget, RECLAIM, RGGI, California AB-32, and the EU ETS.

1. It works. Environmentally effective and cost-effective: SO$_2$ trading cut abatement costs by more than half versus a comparable command-and-control design; the EU ETS has functioned as anticipated after a rocky pilot.

2. The cap must bite. A robust market needs a cap well below business-as-usual (SO$_2$, RECLAIM); a changing economy can make a cap non-binding (RGGI, EU ETS in the 2008–09 recession) — though a low recession price is a virtue, not a bug.

3. Monitoring, reporting, verification + real penalties drive compliance (SO$_2$); rules must be final well before the first compliance period to avoid price volatility (SO$_2$, NO$_x$, EU ETS).

4. Flexibility induces innovation. Avoiding technology and uniform performance standards is one of carbon pricing's biggest payoffs; the induced technological change in past programs would not have happened under rigid rules.

5. Banking is essential. Intertemporal trading delivered a large share of the gains (lead, SO$_2$); its absence caused price spikes (RECLAIM) and collapses (EU ETS). Hence the role for price collars (RGGI, AB-32) — graph: the collar figure in Q7. Over-tight offset limits, by contrast, thin the market.

6. Simplicity and transparent, up-front formulas resist manipulation; not requiring government approval of individual trades keeps transaction costs low (lead, SO$_2$). Transaction costs have been low to trivial in practice.

7. Allocation is the political battleground. Free allocation buys support (SO$_2$, AB-32) but forgoes the efficiency gain from recycling auction revenue into distortionary-tax cuts — and in practice auction revenue gets spent on programs or deficits, not tax cuts (AB-32, RGGI).

8. Leakage is real in narrow programs (power sector, interconnected grids — RGGI, AB-32); free allocation doesn't fix it (EU ETS), output-based updating allocation can (AB-32).

9. “Complementary” policies often conflict with a cap: they relocate rather than reduce emissions, raise costs, and suppress prices (waterbed effect — EU ETS, AB-32; the $16 vs. $200/ton gap). Market stability reserves are the planned fix.

Carbon tax lessons (§3.2)

1. Northern Europe (Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland since 1991–97): taxes came as part of broader energy/fiscal reform, not pure climate policy. Rates are among the world's highest (Sweden ~$120/ton) but riddled with sectoral variation and exemptions (Norwegian coal fully exempt from 2003; Danish manufacturing up to 90% below standard rate) — contrary to the cost-effective prescription of one uniform price. “Fiscal cushioning” via adjustments to pre-existing energy taxes is common for trade-exposed industries. They proved a tax can cut emissions while raising revenue, but the empirical evidence on emissions impacts is thin, and Sweden's heating-fuel revenue eroded as the tax did its job.

2. British Columbia (2008–): the closest thing to the economist's ideal — upstream on fuel carbon content, ~70–75% of emissions, escalating from $7.50 to $38/ton by 2021, originally 100% revenue-neutral by law. Lessons: revenue use drifted from broad rate cuts toward targeted sector relief (now ~50% business tax cuts, 23% personal, 25% lump-sum rebates); distributionally progressive even before revenue use; estimated 5–10% emission reduction with little economic harm — but contested (Pretis 2019) and with unknown leakage.

Hybrid lessons (§3.3) A clear trend toward auctioning (AB-32/AB-398, RGGI, EU ETS). Banking is universal; borrowing exists only implicitly through 1–5 year compliance periods. Safety valves (RGGI $10, California >$50) have become soft price collars; California's post-2020 hard ceiling is the first unlimited-supply ceiling.

Big-picture conclusions (§4)

• Neither instrument dominates on the merits; any ranking depends on the weights across the nine differences and the probability of good design.

• Design > instrument. Many differences fade with implementation decisions; the dichotomy is a continuum.

• Merits and adoptability are linked. Compare realistic versions of both (Hahn & Stavins 1992), and consider weighting net benefits by implementation probability (Goulder 2019). Policies “impeccable from Berkeley or Cambridge” that always die in Washington aren't optimal.

• Taxes are among the least-used climate instruments (20 countries + 2 provinces by 2018) and have been reversed or defeated at advanced stages (France, Switzerland, Australia, Washington State). National cap and trade failed in the U.S. too. Making carbon pricing politically acceptable is the critical step.

The policy continuum

Pure carbon tax Pure cap & trade Tax + quantity-adjusting formula Tax + tradable exemptions Collared cap, 100% auction, banking Auctioned cap, banking Free allocation, no banking

Read the middle of the bar: a collared, auctioned cap and a self-adjusting tax are almost the same instrument. That is why Stavins says design may be “more consequential than the choice between the two.”

30-second recall drill: Six similarities — reductions, costs, revenue, firm costs, distribution, competitiveness. Nine differences — uncertainty, volatility, complementary policies, manipulation, transaction costs, macro, linkage, complexity, politics. Tax wins volatility/complementary/complexity; cap wins linkage and maybe uncertainty; the rest wash. Design > instrument.
