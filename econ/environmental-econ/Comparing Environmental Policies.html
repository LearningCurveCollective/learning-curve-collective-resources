The big idea of the paper: a whole menu of environmental policies — taxes, subsidies, permits, command-and-control, and Coase bargaining — can all be analyzed in one diagram (Figure 1). Under perfect information, every one of them can achieve the same efficient level of pollution and the same net gain to society. What differs is the distribution: who pays, who profits, and who collects the rectangle of “scarcity rents.”

The key: what every symbol means

Key — variables and curves in Figure 1:

$Z$  =  quantity of emissions / pollution (the x-axis), treated as an input to production

$P^0$  =  the firm's fixed private cost per unit of emissions (removal & disposal)

$P'$  =  the full social price of a unit of emissions at the optimum, $P' = P^0 + t^*$

$Z^0$  =  unregulated pollution level (where $MB = PMC$)

$Z'$  =  socially optimal pollution level (where $MB = SMC$)

$PMC$  =  private marginal cost of emissions (flat at $P^0$)

$MED$  =  marginal environmental damage (external cost of one more unit of $Z$)

$SMC$  =  social marginal cost $= PMC + MED$ (upward sloping)

$MB$  =  marginal benefit of pollution (the demand curve for emissions; downward sloping)

$$SMC = PMC + MED \qquad\qquad \text{Optimum: } MB(Z') = SMC(Z') \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z',\; P'$$

1

Identify what is measured on the x-axis of the model. (Section 2, pp. 3 – middle of p. 5)

Answer

The x-axis measures the quantity of pollution — emissions, $Z$ (gaseous, liquid, or solid waste). It is not output of the dirty good.

Fullerton gets there with a clever re-write of the production function. Instead of treating pollution as a by-product on the left-hand side, he solves for output so that emissions appear as an input:

$$Y = F(L,\,K,\,R,\,Z)$$

where $Y$ is dirty-good output, $L$ is labor, $K$ is capital, $R$ is other resources, and $Z$ is emissions. Like any input, emissions have a downward-sloping marginal product — the first units of pollution are crucial to production, and additional units matter less and less. That marginal product is exactly the downward-sloping marginal benefit (demand) curve for pollution in Figure 1. The y-axis is then the price or cost per unit of emissions (dollars per unit of $Z$).

One special case worth stating: if pollution is fixed per unit of output (think: a ton of steel always makes the same emissions), the demand-for-pollution curve can also be read as the demand for the output — and only in that case can a tax on output cut damages effectively. With variable emissions per unit of output, you have to target emissions themselves.

2

Identify and briefly describe the cost and benefit concepts on the y-axis — define and distinguish PMC, SMC, MED. Then discuss how they are used to illustrate the efficient level of what the x-axis measures.

Definitions

$PMC$ — private marginal cost. The cost the firm itself bears to remove and dispose of one more unit of waste. Fullerton assumes this cost is fixed at $P^0$ per unit, so $PMC$ is a flat horizontal line at height $P^0$.

$MED$ — marginal environmental damage. The dollar value of the lost utility to all other individuals from one more unit of emissions — the external cost. It starts small (the first unit of pollution does little harm) and grows as pollution accumulates (when the air is already dirty, one more unit can send people to the hospital).

$SMC$ — social marginal cost. The full cost to society of one more unit of emissions: the firm's cost plus the harm to everyone else.

$$SMC \;=\; PMC \;+\; MED$$

$SMC$ starts just above $PMC$ (small first-unit damage) and slopes upward because $MED$ rises. The gap between $SMC$ and $PMC$ at any $Z$ is the $MED$ of that unit.

Efficient level

The downward-sloping demand curve is the marginal benefit ($MB$) of pollution — what someone is willing to pay for the right to emit one more unit.

Unregulated equilibrium: firms pollute as long as $MB$ exceeds their own private cost, stopping where

$$MB(Z^0) = PMC = P^0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z^0 \text{ (too much pollution)}$$

Social optimum: society gains from pollution as long as its benefits exceed the full social cost, so the efficient level is where

$$MB(Z') = SMC(Z') \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z' \text{ at social price } P'$$

The policy problem, for every instrument in the paper, is the same: cut pollution from $Z^0$ down to $Z'$. Doing so eliminates the units whose social cost exceeds their benefit, generating the net welfare gain of area $5+6$.

3

List and briefly describe the policy instruments compared in the paper. (Table 1 and Section 3)

Answer

Section 3 reviews eight policy types, organized under four headings with two variants each (the rows of Table 1). If your write-up needs seven, list the four headings and their variants and note that 3b — the price-plus-quantity control — is included by Fullerton only as a cautionary “what not to do” case; check with the guide/class if unsure which one to fold together.

1. Pigouvian solutions

1a. Tax on pollution. A tax $t^*$ per unit of emissions equal to marginal external damage at the optimum. It raises the firm's cost of polluting from $P^0$ to $P' = P^0 + t^*$, so firms stop at $Z'$. Government collects revenue $2+3$.

1b. Subsidy for abatement. Government pays firms $t^* = P' - P^0$ for each unit of pollution they cut below $Z^0$. Polluting now carries an opportunity cost (subsidy foregone), so the full cost of a unit of pollution is again $P'$ and firms choose $Z'$. Same incentive, opposite direction of payment — firms end up with profits.

2. Tradeable permits (real-world example: SO$_2$ allowances under the 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments)

2a. Handed out to firms (“grandfathered”). Government issues $Z'$ permits free, typically in proportion to past emissions. Permits trade at $P' - P^0$, so recipients are handed a private profit of area $2+3$.

2b. Sold at auction. Same $Z'$ permits, but government sells them. Firms bid the price up to $P' - P^0$, so government collects $2+3$ — in this model, identical in every respect to the Pigouvian tax.

3. Command and control (CAC)

3a. Quantity restriction (performance standard). A mandate: “pollution shall not exceed $Z'$.” Output is restricted, price rises to $P'$, and firms — not government — capture scarcity rents $2+3$. Equivalent to handing out permits (2a), even if nothing is tradeable.

3b. Quantity and price restriction. Restrict pollution to $Z'$ and forbid price increases above $P^0$. But $(Z', P^0)$ is off the demand curve — excess demand means rationing by coupons (which become de facto permits), queues, or lobbying. In the worst case, rent-seeking wastes the whole rectangle $2+3$. Fullerton includes it to show Congress “cannot repeal the Law of Supply and Demand.”

4. Coase solutions (no government intervention — bargaining, which works only with well-defined property rights, low bargaining costs, and excludability, i.e., few parties)

4a. “Victim” has property rights. Firms must pay victims for the right to pollute, so they face the full $SMC$ and produce $Z'$. Victims collect a negotiated payment $B$, with $3 \le B \le 2+3$.

4b. Polluter has property rights. Victims pay firms $C$ to cut back to $Z'$ (they'll pay up to the damage avoided, $C \le 4+5+6$). Firms “make out like bandits” — but the efficient quantity is reached either way. That's the Coase theorem: the rights assignment changes the distribution, not the efficiency.

Punchline of Section 3: in this perfect-information model, every one of these policies reaches $Z'$ and delivers the same net gain $5+6$. They differ only in who gets rectangle $2+3$: government (tax, auction), firms (subsidy, grandfathered permits, CAC quantity), consumers (price control, at best), or the bargaining parties (Coase).

4

Using Figure 1, give each efficient policy level in terms of $P'$/$P^0$ (or $Z'$/$Z^0$):

a. Efficient tax level (price instrument)
b. Efficient subsidy level (price instrument)
c. Efficient pollution standard (quantity instrument)
d. Efficient permit price (price instrument)

Recall the key: $P^0$ = private cost per unit of emissions; $P'$ = full social price at the optimum; $Z^0$ = unregulated pollution; $Z'$ = optimal pollution.

a. Tax The efficient Pigouvian tax rate is the gap between the two prices:

$$t^* = P' - P^0$$

It equals marginal external damage at the optimum, and it raises the firm's cost of polluting from $P^0$ up to exactly $P'$ — so if the question wants a single label from the figure, the answer is that the tax makes polluters face $P'$.

b. Subsidy Same magnitude, opposite direction:

$$s^* = P' - P^0 \;\; \text{per unit of pollution cut below } Z^0$$

Each unit of pollution now costs the firm $P^0$ in disposal plus $P' - P^0$ in subsidy foregone, so the full cost of polluting is again $P'$, and firms stop at $Z'$.

c. Standard The quantity instrument sets the quantity directly:

$$\bar{Z} = Z' \quad (\text{not } Z^0 \text{ — that's the unregulated level})$$

“Pollution shall not exceed $Z'$.” The market then pushes the implicit (shadow) price of polluting up to $P'$ on its own.

d. Permit price With $Z'$ permits issued, firms bid until the permit price equals what a unit of pollution is worth above its disposal cost:

$$p_{\text{permit}} = P' - P^0 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{total marginal cost of polluting} = P^0 + p_{\text{permit}} = P'$$

(Fullerton's real-world anchor: an SO$_2$ allowance traded around \$150/ton, so a polluter's all-in cost was disposal cost plus \$150.)

Pattern to remember: every price instrument (a, b, d) is set at the gap $P' - P^0$ so the all-in price of polluting lands on $P'$; the quantity instrument (c) is set at $Z'$ and lets the price adjust. Under certainty they're interchangeable — Q5/Q6 explain why uncertainty breaks the tie.

5

From Section 4 (pp. 16–26), briefly describe at least 3 of the other 7 criteria for comparing instruments. (Each criterion is a Section 4 sub-heading; economic efficiency is #1, so the “other 7” are #2–#8.)

All 7 (pick any 3 for your write-up)

2. Administrative efficiency. How cheaply the policy can be run. A tax on easily measured market transactions (e.g., chemical feedstocks, gasoline) piggybacks on existing tax machinery at very low cost; a true emissions tax that requires measuring every source's waste could cost far more to administer.

3. Monitoring and enforcement. A policy is only as good as your ability to measure the pollutant and catch cheaters. SO$_2$ permits work because utilities are large point sources with continuous emissions monitoring; auto tailpipe emissions can only be measured inaccurately and expensively, which is why we tax gasoline instead of emissions.

4. Information and uncertainty. Under certainty, a tax and a permit system are equivalent. Under uncertainty about the curves (Weitzman 1974), they split: a price instrument fixes $P$ and leaves $Z$ uncertain; a quantity instrument fixes $Z$ and leaves the price/abatement cost uncertain. Which is better depends on the relative slopes — steep abatement costs favor the price instrument (avoid runaway compliance costs); steep marginal damages favor the quantity instrument (avoid catastrophic pollution).

5. Political and ethical considerations. A new “tax” may be dead on arrival; CAC mandates are popular partly because their costs to consumers are hidden, and legislators can “guarantee” results. Grandfathered permits win industry support because they hand firms rents. Ethically, some view pollution as a wrong to be stigmatized by law, not merely priced.

6. Equity and distributional effects. Who bears the burden: rich vs. poor (vertical equity — do low-income households spend a larger share on the taxed goods?), similar people treated similarly (horizontal equity), urban vs. rural, young vs. old — plus the “polluter pays” principle, which works prospectively (efficiency) but fails as retroactive fairness.

7. Other distortions. The clean model assumed lump-sum taxes. In reality, pre-existing distortionary taxes, monopoly power, and trade barriers interact with environmental policy (the “double dividend” debate). Policies that raise revenue (tax, auctioned permits) can fund cuts in other distorting taxes; policies that hand out rents cannot.

8. Flexibility and dynamic adjustments. Can the policy adapt as science, measurement, and conditions change — and does it reward innovation? Market-based incentives keep paying firms to find cheaper abatement; a technology mandate freezes the status quo. Transitions also carry real costs (layoffs, retraining, capital losses) that the static model ignores.

6

Be able to describe tradeoffs between some criteria.

Four ready-to-use examples

Economic efficiency vs. administrative / monitoring ease — the gasoline tax. The ideal instrument taxes emissions, which would reward cleaner cars, better maintenance, and gentler driving. But tailpipe emissions are costly to measure, so we tax gasoline instead: cheap to administer and enforce, but only loosely tied to actual emissions. We deliberately give up efficiency to gain administrability — a balance that could shift as measurement technology improves.

Cost certainty vs. environmental certainty — prices vs. quantities. A tax caps the marginal cost of compliance but leaves the amount of pollution uncertain; a permit cap guarantees the quantity but leaves compliance costs uncertain. If damages are gradual (hazier views at the Grand Canyon), fix the price. If damages have a catastrophic threshold (deaths in a temperature inversion), fix the quantity. You cannot have both certainties with one instrument.

Political feasibility vs. distribution (and revenue). The policies easiest to pass — grandfathered permits, CAC quantity limits — are exactly the ones that hand rectangle $2+3$ to firms as rents, building a powerful industry–environmentalist coalition while consumers quietly pay higher prices. The policies best for the treasury (tax, auctioned permits) collect $2+3$ as revenue that could offset other taxes, but “tax” is politically toxic. Feasibility is often bought with the rents.

Flexibility / innovation vs. certainty of compliance. Incentive policies (taxes, permits) keep rewarding firms for inventing cheaper abatement, but the outcome depends on firms' responses. A technology mandate is easy to verify — inspect whether the equipment is installed — but gives zero incentive to do better than the mandate, and locks in today's technology.

Frame for the exam: Section 3 shows the instruments are efficiency-equivalent on the blackboard; Section 4 shows why they are not equivalent in the world. Every real policy choice trades one criterion against another — there is no instrument that wins on all eight.

Cheat sheet: who matches whom

The one line to remember

Fullerton's own framing — the only differences among these efficient policies are distributional, coming down to “who gets the scarcity rents”: rectangle $2+3$ may end up as firms' profits, as government tax revenue, or as consumer surplus. (Section 3, following the price-control discussion)

If John can restate that sentence and then point to which box below each policy falls into, he has the paper.

All eight policies deliver the same net gain, so the fastest way to organize them is by where rectangle $2+3$ ends up. Policies in the same box below produce identical distributional rows in Table 1.

$$\text{Follow the rectangle: } \; 2+3 = (P'-P^0)\times Z'$$

Where does $2+3$ go?

→ Government Identical twins #1:  1a Pigouvian tax  $\equiv$  2b auctioned permits

Every Table 1 cell matches: consumers $-(2{+}3{+}4)$, firms $0$, gov't $+(2{+}3)$, victims $+(4{+}5{+}6)$. A tax of $P'-P^0$ per unit and an auction of $Z'$ permits are the same policy under certainty — the revenue-raising pair.

→ Firms Identical twins #2:  2a grandfathered permits  $\equiv$  3a CAC quantity restriction

Both restrict quantity to $Z'$ and hand firms the scarcity rents $2{+}3$; gov't gets nothing. Tradeability changes nothing — a strict quota is a permit handout in disguise. The politically popular pair.

Cousin, not twin — 1b abatement subsidy: firms also capture $2{+}3$, plus the subsidy $4{+}5$, and it's the only row where gov't revenue is negative, $-(4{+}5)$. Firms' best row; treasury's worst.

→ Consumers (or nobody) One of a kind:  3b quantity + price restriction

The only row where consumers can keep $2{+}3$ (losing just area $4$) — if rationing is clean. With coupons the holders capture it; with queues or lobbying, rent-seeking burns it and nobody gets it. The only policy whose net effect can fall below $5{+}6$.

→ The bargaining table The Coase family:  4a victims' rights  ↔  4b polluter's rights

Same structure, mirror-image payments: under 4a firms pay victims $B$ (with $3 \le B \le 2{+}3$), under 4b victims pay firms $C$ (with $C \le 4{+}5{+}6$). The rights assignment slides money across the table without moving $Z'$ — Coase's whole point.

30-second recall drill: Government gets it → tax, auction. Firms get it → free permits, quota (and the subsidy pays them extra). Consumers might keep it → price cap. It's negotiated → Coase. That's all eight.

The takeaway sentence for the exam: under perfect information, all of these instruments are efficiency-equivalent — each moves pollution to $Z'$ and earns society $5+6$ — so the fight over which instrument to use is really a fight over who captures rectangle $2+3$: the treasury, the firms, the consumers, or the bargaining table.

How to read the table (the two big equivalences)

Equivalence #1 — tax $=$ auctioned permits (rows 1a & 2b). Every cell matches: consumers pay, government collects $2+3$, victims gain, net $5+6$. Under perfect information these are the same policy wearing different clothes.

Equivalence #2 — grandfathered permits $=$ CAC quantity restriction (rows 2a & 3a). Both restrict quantity to $Z'$ and let firms keep the scarcity rents $2+3$ — tradeability doesn't change who wins in this model.

What never changes: consumers lose $2+3+4$ (except the price-control case), victims gain $4+5+6$, and society nets $5+6$. What changes: the address of rectangle $2+3$. That rectangle — tax revenue vs. firm profits vs. bargaining payments — is why interest groups fight over instruments that economists call “equivalent.”

For class: the professor will derive the entries in these rows on the board — you're expected to understand each policy in relation to the graph, not to reproduce every cell from scratch. If you can explain why consumers lose $2+3+4$ when price rises from $P^0$ to $P'$, and who captures $2+3$ under each row, you're in good shape.
