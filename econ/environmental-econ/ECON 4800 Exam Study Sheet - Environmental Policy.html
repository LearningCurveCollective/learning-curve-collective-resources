Negative externality (GLS). The market equates MPB with MPC and produces $Q_{mkt}$; society wants $MSB = MSC$ at $Q^* < Q_{mkt}$. Units between $Q^*$ and $Q_{mkt}$ cost society more than they are worth: the red triangle is the deadweight loss. A Pigouvian tax equal to MEC at $Q^*$ shifts MPC up to the efficient point.

Positive externality (GLS; JNS learning by using). Adopters ignore the benefit their experience gives later adopters (MEB), so adoption stops at $Q_{mkt} < Q^*$. A subsidy equal to MEB closes the gap. This is the graph for any JNS adoption externality.

Coase. If victims own the right to clean air, start at $Z=0$: the firm pays victims for each unit as long as MB > MD, stopping at $Z^*$. If the polluter owns the right, start at $Z_0$: victims pay the firm to cut back while MD > MB, stopping at $Z^*$. Same $Z^*$; the rights decide who pays.

Fullerton Table 1 as pictures. All eight reach $Z'$ and the same net gain 5+6; only the distribution differs. Tax and auction: 2+3 to government. Handout permits and CAC quantity limit: 2+3 to firms as scarcity rent. Subsidy: firms get 2+3 and the subsidy 4+5. CAC with a price cap: consumers keep 1+2+3 (but someone must ration). Coase: depends on the bargain (B, C).

Efficiency (Stavins). Cutting emissions from $E_0$ costs MAC per ton and avoids MD per ton. Total social cost (abatement cost + remaining damage) is minimized where MD = MAC at $E^*$; the efficient tax is $t^*$ at that height.

Hybrid: price collar. Inside the collar it is a cap (price moves, E = cap). If demand collapses, the floor binds: a tax-like price, fewer emissions than the cap. If demand surges, the ceiling releases allowances: tax-like price, more emissions than the cap.

Cost-effectiveness / similarity #2. With one price t = 3, each firm abates until MAC = t: the cheap firm does 6 tons, the expensive firm 2. Total cost = 12. A uniform standard (4 + 4) hits the same 8 tons but costs 16 because MACs are unequal (2 vs 6): shift a ton from firm 2 to firm 1 and you save 6 − 2 = 4 at the margin.

Tax and permit in a market model (similarities #1, #2, #4). The aggregate MAC is the demand for the right to emit. A tax of \$4 sets the price and firms emit 6; a cap of 6 permits sets the quantity and trading discovers a price of \$4. Same emissions, same MACs, same abatement cost. Firms' cost = abatement triangle + payment rectangle (tax bill or auction); with free permits the rectangle is handed back.

Difference #1: uncertainty (Weitzman). Policy is set off the expected MAC; costs turn out higher. Under a tax, firms abate less and emit to where actual MAC = t (gold DWL). Under a cap, emissions stay put and firms abate too much (brown DWL). A: steep MAC, flat MD → tax loses less. B: steep MD → cap loses less. For CO₂ (a stock pollutant, flat MD) Newell & Pizer favor a tax; Karp & Traeger's positive correlation weakens that.

Difference #2: price volatility (and macro). When the economy booms or slumps, the MAC (demand for permits) shifts. A cap holds emissions and the price swings; a tax holds the price and emissions swing. Cap is counter-cyclical (price falls in recessions); banking/borrowing or a collar dampen the swings.

Micro rebound (GRW). Doubling mpg halves the price per mile, so the budget line pivots out. A→B: substitution effect (miles are relatively cheaper). B→C: income effect (savings make you richer). Miles rise from 88 to 116 (+32%), so gallons fall by less than the engineering estimate: rebound = 32%.

Macro price effect (GRW Figure 1). Efficiency shifts world energy demand from D to D′. At the old price use would fall a−b, but the price falls and others buy more, so use only falls a−c. Rebound = 1 − (a−c)/(a−b): bigger when supply is inelastic and demand is elastic, always below 100%.

Salience (Rivers & Schaufele). A 5¢ rise in the market price moves consumers along D (−1.8%). A 5¢ carbon tax does that and shifts D left (−12.5% total, 7.1×): the tax is more salient than its price effect alone.

Internality (Allcott & Sunstein). Buyers act on decision utility that ignores part of future energy costs, so they buy $Q_{chosen}$ instead of $Q^*$ (experienced utility). The gap between the curves is the bias; a corrective tax or subsidy equal to the average bias of marginal buyers fixes it. Same shape as an externality, but the harm falls on the chooser.

Green clubs (Kotchen), illustrative. A stricter standard means each member provides more and the label is worth more, but fewer firms join. Total public good = members × provision each, so the best standard for the environment is interior: neither the weakest nor the toughest.

Guide QDefine positive and negative externalities, explain why they lead to market failure, and give examples. GLS ch. 17

An externality is a cost or benefit of an economic activity that falls on a third party who is not part of the transaction and is not compensated or charged through the price.

  • Negative externality: a cost imposed on others. Marginal social cost exceeds marginal private cost: $MSC = MPC + MEC$. Examples: a pulp mill discharging BOD into a river (Foulon), CO₂ from driving (Rivers & Schaufele), SO₂ from power plants (Stavins).
  • Positive externality: a benefit others receive for free. $MSB = MPB + MEB$. Examples: knowledge spillovers from R&D, learning by using a new energy technology (JNS), a firm joining a green club that cleans the air for non-members (Kotchen).

Why market failure: buyers and sellers respond only to private costs and benefits. With a negative externality the market produces where $MPB = MPC$, which is more than the efficient $Q^*$ where $MSB = MSC$ (overproduction). With a positive externality it produces less than $Q^*$. Either way, total surplus is not maximized: there is a deadweight loss, so the market fails to allocate resources efficiently. The root cause is a missing market / missing price: nobody owns clean air, so nobody charges for using it (this is why Stavins says both the Pigouvian and Coasean views are "missing markets").

Negative: market at $Q_{mkt}$ where D = MPC. Efficient at $Q^*$ where D = MSC. Each unit from $Q^*$ to $Q_{mkt}$ has MSC > MSB, so the shaded triangle is the deadweight loss. Fix: Pigouvian tax equal to MEC evaluated at $Q^*$.

Positive: market at $Q_{mkt}$ where MPB = MPC; efficient at $Q^*$ where MSB = MPC. Units between them are worth more to society than they cost but are not produced: DWL. Fix: subsidy equal to MEB at $Q^*$.

Guide QWhy is this relevant to environmental economics and to the design of policy solutions?

Almost every environmental problem is an externality or public-good problem, so the diagnosis tells you the cure:

  • Price the externality (Pigou): a tax equal to marginal damage at the optimum, or a permit price that does the same (Stavins, Fullerton 1a, 2b).
  • Create property rights (Coase): define and trade rights to pollute (cap-and-trade), or let victims sue (liability). Tradable permits are a Coasean solution (Stavins); Kotchen calls VIBAs a property-rights approach too.
  • Subsidize positive externalities: R&D and early adoption (JNS: learning by using is "a classic positive externality, which lends itself to a subsidy," as G&P put it).
  • Regulate directly (CAC standards) when measuring emissions is impossible or enforcement is easier (Fullerton's monitoring criterion).

The size and shape of the externality (how steep MD is, whether it is a stock or flow pollutant, how many parties are involved) determine which instrument works best. That is the whole point of Fullerton and Stavins.

Guide QExplain the Coasean approach and the centrality of property rights in the Coasean solution. Fullerton pp. 13 to 15; Stavins p. 4

Coase (1960): if property rights are clearly defined and the parties can bargain cheaply, private negotiation reaches the efficient level of pollution no matter who is given the rights. Government only needs to define and enforce the rights (e.g., through courts).

Three conditions (Fullerton): (1) property rights must be well defined (either victims have the right to be free of the pollutant, or firms have the right to pollute); (2) bargaining costs must be low enough for parties to find each other and agree; (3) exclusion must be feasible so no one can free-ride on the deal. In practice this means few parties.

Why property rights are central: the externality exists because no one owns the environment, so its use has no price. Assigning rights creates the missing market. The assignment does not change efficiency, but it does change distribution: whoever holds the right gets paid. Coase also stressed that externalities are reciprocal (the river is valuable to both the fishery and the logger).

Where it breaks: many victims (air pollution) means high transaction costs and free riding, so government steps in with Pigouvian taxes or permits. Tradable permits are the policy descendant of Coase: government sets the cap and hands out the rights, then trading finds the least-cost allocation (Crocker 1966, Dales 1968).

Victims hold the right: pollution starts at 0. The firm values the first units highly (MB > MD), so it pays victims to accept them; it keeps buying until $Z^*$. Payment is at least the damage area (victims must be compensated) and at most the firm's benefit. In Fullerton's Table 1 row 4a the firm keeps $2+3-B$ with $3 \le B \le 2+3$.

Polluter holds the right: start at $Z_0$. Victims will pay up to MD for each unit cut; the firm accepts as long as the payment beats its MB. They stop at $Z^*$ (row 4b: firm gets $2+3+C$).

Guide QDefine and distinguish PMC, SMC and MED (the y-axis curves). How do they show the efficient level of pollution (x-axis)? pp. 3 to 6

The x-axis is pollution $Z$, treated as an input to production with a downward-sloping marginal product (extra units of emissions are successively less crucial to production).

  • MB (marginal benefit, the demand for pollution): what firms/consumers would pay for the right to emit one more unit. Slopes down.
  • PMC (private marginal cost) $= P^\circ$: the private cost per unit of removing and disposing of the waste. Flat at $P^\circ$.
  • MED (marginal environmental damage): the dollar value of the lost utility to everyone else from one more unit of pollution. Rising: each unit hurts more as the air gets dirtier.
  • SMC $= PMC + MED$: the full cost to society. Starts just above PMC (the first unit does little harm) and slopes up.

Unregulated: polluters stop where $MB = PMC$, at $Z^\circ$. Efficient: $MB = SMC$, at $Z'$. The problem for policy is to cut pollution from $Z^\circ$ to $Z'$. Units between $Z'$ and $Z^\circ$ cost society (SMC) more than they are worth (MB): the welfare gain from cutting them is area 5+6.

Guide QExplain Figure 1 in your own words: efficient tax, subsidy, standard and permit price in terms of $P'$, $P^\circ$, $Z'$, $Z^\circ$.
  • Efficient tax (price instrument): $t_Z = P' - P^\circ$, the MED at $Z'$. It raises the polluter's cost from $P^\circ$ to $P'$, so the after-tax cost per unit is $P'$ and polluters stop at $Z'$.
  • Efficient subsidy (price instrument): also $P' - P^\circ$ per unit of pollution reduced below $Z^\circ$. Polluting one more unit means giving up that subsidy, so the full cost of polluting is again $P'$.
  • Efficient standard (quantity instrument): $Z'$. "Pollution shall not exceed $Z'$." The shadow price of the right to pollute becomes $P' - P^\circ$.
  • Efficient permit price (price that emerges from a quantity instrument): issue $Z'$ permits; they trade at $P' - P^\circ$ per unit (the text's SO₂ example: about \$150/ton), so the total cost of polluting is $P^\circ$ + permit price $= P'$.

One line to remember: every price instrument is $P' - P^\circ$; every quantity instrument is $Z'$; and every policy makes the polluter face $P'$.

Guide QCompare the instruments by welfare impact: similarities and differences, graphically and in prose. pp. 7 to 15

Similarities. All eight cut pollution to $Z'$ and produce the same net gain (5+6). In seven of eight, consumers pay the higher price $P'$ and lose 2+3+4; victims always gain 4+5+6. Firms earn zero profit before and after a tax (competition), so in equilibrium the tax hits consumers, not shareholders.

Differences: who gets rectangle 2+3.

  • Revenue-raising (tax, auction): government gets 2+3 and can cut other taxes. Tax = auction exactly in this model.
  • Rent-creating (handout permits, CAC quantity limit, Coase): firms get 2+3 as a scarcity rent. Handout permits = CAC quantity restriction exactly: even untradeable quotas create rents, because restricting output lets firms charge $P'$ while costs stay $P^\circ$ (a legal cartel).
  • Subsidy: firms get 2+3 plus the payments 4+5, financed by taxpayers. Only incumbents are eligible, so it acts like paying firms to collude; it also may attract entry (a problem the model rules out).
  • Quantity + price limit: consumers keep 2+3 on paper, but excess demand needs rationing; queues or lobbying can waste 2+3.

Politics follows the rents: industry prefers instruments that give it 2+3 (grandfathered permits, quantity limits), which is why environmentalists and industry can form a coalition (Buchanan & Tullock; Maloney & McCormick found cotton-dust standards raised textile stock prices).

Guide QDescribe briefly the criteria for comparing instruments (Section 4 headings). pp. 16 to 26

Your guide says 7; the paper's Section 4 has 8 numbered headings. Learn all 8; if asked for 7, any 7 earns the points.

  1. Economic efficiency: right amount of pollution and least-cost abatement. Market incentives let each firm find its cheapest method; CAC typically costs 6 to 10× the minimum.
  2. Administrative efficiency: minimize government administration and private compliance costs. A tax on chemical feedstocks is easier to run than a tax on hazardous waste; economies of scale mean a tax is only worth it if the externality is big enough.
  3. Monitoring and enforcement: can you measure the pollutant and deter evasion? A per-unit waste tax may push waste toward illegal dumping; checking that a scrubber is installed is easier than counting tons.
  4. Information and uncertainty: firms know their abatement costs better than regulators; Weitzman: steep MAC favors a price, steep MD favors a quantity.
  5. Political and ethical considerations: a new "tax" may be Dead on Arrival; mandates "guarantee" results to voters; some see pollution as a crime to stigmatize, not a fee to pay.
  6. Equity and distributional effects: vertical equity (rich vs poor), horizontal equity (similar people treated alike), polluter-pays principle (prospective works; retroactive does not).
  7. Other distortions: monopoly (second best: a Pigouvian tax on a monopolist can reduce welfare), trade, existing labor taxes (the double-dividend debate).
  8. Flexibility and dynamic adjustment: ability to update rules as information improves; incentives to innovate (taxes/permits yes, technology mandates no); transition costs (job loss, retraining).
Guide QDescribe trade-offs between criteria. §5, pp. 26 to 28
  • Efficiency vs administration / monitoring: the US gasoline tax is simple to collect and enforce, but gas use is only weakly correlated with emissions (vehicle age, maintenance, driving style), so it is less efficient than a true emissions tax. One national rate also ignores that damages are huge in LA and tiny in rural areas; a geographic tax would be hard to administer.
  • Efficiency vs monitoring: requiring a scrubber is easier to verify than a tax per unit of waste.
  • Information vs bargaining costs (Coase): affected parties know more and can re-contract (flexible), but bargaining and litigation are costly and free riding breaks it.
  • Efficiency vs equity: benefits of many programs go to high-income households (wilderness, visibility) while costs can be regressive; redistributing may weaken targeting.
  • Flexibility vs certainty: frequently changing rules lets policy adapt, but uncertainty discourages investment ("changing the rules in the middle of the game") and creates windfall gains/losses (horizontal equity).

Fullerton's closing point: "a reform cannot obtain something for nothing." The framework makes the trade-offs explicit.

Common exam traps. (1) Saying the tax is "more efficient" than handout permits in Fullerton's model: it is not; they differ only in distribution (the revenue matters once you drop the lump-sum-tax assumption: criterion 7). (2) Forgetting the subsidy's opportunity-cost logic: the firm faces $P'$ because polluting means forgoing the subsidy. (3) Thinking CAC quantity limits hurt firms: they create scarcity rents 2+3.

Guide QDefine, differentiate, explain and illustrate efficiency (MD = MAC) and cost-effectiveness (equality of all MACs). §2, pp. 3 to 5

Efficiency answers "how much pollution?" The efficient level minimizes the total of abatement costs plus damages, which happens where marginal damage = marginal abatement cost. Beyond that point, an extra ton of abatement costs more than the damage it avoids.

Cost-effectiveness answers "given a target, is it met at least cost?" It requires every source's MAC to be equal (the equimarginal principle). If two firms have different MACs, shifting a ton of abatement from the high-MAC firm to the low-MAC firm lowers total cost without changing emissions.

Link: a common tax or permit price satisfies cost-effectiveness automatically (each firm abates until MAC = price). It is also efficient only if the price (or cap) is set where MD = MAC. A target set by politics (not at MD = MAC) can be cost-effective without being efficient: Stavins notes the cap is "not necessarily at the efficient level."

Givens: $MAC_1 = 0.5A_1$, $MAC_2 = 1.5A_2$, target $A_1 + A_2 = 8$.

Cost-effective: $0.5A_1 = 1.5A_2 \Rightarrow A_1 = 3A_2$, so $A_2 = 2$, $A_1 = 6$, common MAC $= 3$ (the tax that achieves it). Cost $= \tfrac12(6)(3) + \tfrac12(2)(3) = 9 + 3 = 12$.

Uniform standard (4 each): MACs are 2 and 6, cost $= \tfrac12(4)(2) + \tfrac12(4)(6) = 4 + 12 = 16$. Same emissions, 33% more expensive.

Guide QIllustrate a tax and a permit system using a market model.

Treat the right to emit as a good. Its demand curve is the aggregate MAC (firms will pay up to their MAC to avoid abating a ton). Tax: the government sets a horizontal price line at $t$; firms emit where MAC = $t$. Cap-and-trade: the government sets a vertical supply of permits at the cap $\bar E$; trading finds the price where MAC = $p$. With certainty, a tax of \$4 and a cap of 6 give the identical point.

Guide QExplain the theoretical foundation of taxes (negative externalities) and tradable permits (Coase, property rights). pp. 3 to 4

Taxes: Pigou (1920), the externality view. Pollution is a negative externality; charge each unit a tax equal to marginal social damage at the efficient level so emitters internalize the damage. Each firm abates until MAC = tax, so MACs are equalized (cost-effective) in the short run, and the tax rewards diffusion and innovation of cheaper abatement in the long run.

Permits: Coase (1960), the property-rights view. Pollution is a problem of poorly defined property rights (a public-good problem). Make clean air a tradable property right: Crocker (1966) and Dales (1968) proposed transferable discharge permits. The regulator only sets the total (the cap), distributes rights, and lets trading move them to the lowest-cost abaters, so each source abates until MAC = permit price.

Both are "missing markets" (Arrow 1969): one supplies the missing price, the other the missing property right.

Guide QGraph and discuss more deeply differences #1 and #2. §2.2.1, §2.2.2

#1 Uncertainty (Weitzman 1974). If the regulator is unsure where the MAC lies, a tax fixes the price and lets emissions be wrong; a cap fixes emissions and lets the price (cost) be wrong. Benefit uncertainty alone doesn't matter; cost uncertainty does, through relative slopes:

  • |slope MAC| > |slope MD| → tax (avoids very expensive abatement when costs surprise).
  • |slope MD| > |slope MAC| → cap (avoids catastrophic damage from too much pollution).
  • Correlated uncertainty (Stavins 1996): positive correlation between MB and MC favors the cap.
  • Climate: CO₂ is a stock pollutant, so one year's emissions barely move the stock: MD is flat, favoring a tax (Newell & Pizer 2003; Hoel & Karp). But tech change lowers both current costs and future damages (positive correlation), so the tax advantage is "much weaker than commonly believed" (Karp & Traeger 2018). Result: ambiguous, an empirical question.

#2 Price volatility. Under a cap, emissions are fixed and the allowance price is endogenous, so it swings with economic conditions; under a tax, price is fixed and emissions are endogenous. Volatility deters capital investment and R&D and erodes political support. Since CO₂ is a stock pollutant, letting emissions vary year to year is fine: a tax does it automatically; a cap needs banking and borrowing (a cumulative cap) or a price collar.

Guide QGraph and discuss how hybrid instruments are designed and how they fix each instrument's weakness. §2.3, §3.3

Weakness of a pure cap: price volatility and cost uncertainty. Hybrid fix: a price collar = auction price floor (reserve price; allowances withheld if price falls) + price ceiling / safety valve (extra allowances released from a reserve if price rises). Inside the collar it is a cap; at the edges it is a tax. Banking puts a floor under prices; borrowing adds flexibility.

Cost of the fix: it gives up the cap's selling point, quantity certainty: a ceiling adds allowances, a binding floor means fewer emissions than the cap. Remedy: draw ceiling allowances from a set-aside reserve or future years so cumulative emissions don't change. A finite reserve gives a "soft" ceiling (RGGI, California pre-2020); California post-2020 has a hard ceiling.

Weakness of a pure tax: emissions uncertainty. Fix: an automatic adjustment formula that raises the rate if emissions miss the target, periodic review, or earmarking revenue for mitigation.

So taxes and caps are endpoints of a continuum of design choices.

Experience: RGGI safety valve about \$10/t, California above \$50; both now use collars (floor + reserve trigger price).

Guide QKey lessons from global experience with taxes and permits. §3

Cap-and-trade (lead phasedown, SO₂, NOx, RECLAIM, RGGI, California AB-32, EU ETS):

  • Environmentally effective and cost-effective: SO₂ trading cut costs by more than half vs CAC.
  • Transaction costs low to trivial; a robust market needs a cap well below business as usual.
  • Compliance needs monitoring, reporting, verification + real penalties; set rules early to avoid price volatility.
  • Flexible compliance induced innovation; banking was a large share of gains; no banking → spikes (RECLAIM) or collapses (EU ETS).
  • A changing economy can make a cap non-binding (RGGI, EU ETS) → role for price collars; too-tight offset limits thin the market.
  • Free allocation buys political support; auction revenue tends to fund programs rather than tax cuts.
  • Leakage ranges from none to serious (RGGI); output-based updating allocation can address it.
  • "Complementary" policies under the cap backfire (waterbed effect): AB-32 allowances ~\$16/t vs LCFS ~\$200/t.

Carbon taxes: Nordic taxes (Norway 1991, Sweden 1991 now ~\$120/t, Denmark, Finland) came with tax reform and many exemptions (not one uniform price), little evidence on emissions. British Columbia (2008) is the closest to the economist's ideal: upstream, broad, initially revenue-neutral, progressive overall; cut emissions 5 to 10% by one estimate (disputed).

Hybrids: more auctioning over time; collars and safety valves in RGGI and California. Overall: design dominates instrument choice.

Common exam traps. (1) "Free allowances mean firms don't care about emissions": wrong; each allowance used is an allowance not sold (opportunity cost), so the marginal incentive is identical. (2) In a recession a cap does not cut emissions further; the price falls instead. (3) Under a binding cap, an extra renewable mandate does not reduce total emissions.

Exam framing. For "compare taxes and permits" essays, use Stavins' structure: foundations (Pigou vs Coase) → six similarities (they are equivalent under certainty) → nine differences with the winner of each → hybrids blur them → conclusion: design and political feasibility matter more than the label.

Jaffe, Newell & Stavins: "A Tale of Two Market Failures" paper not in folder

Guide QWhy may technology policy that promotes innovation be insufficient to guarantee diffusion? Define each failure in your own words, explain how it causes market failure, and give an example.

The two headline failures are (1) the environmental externality (pollution is unpriced, so there is little demand for clean technology) and (2) knowledge spillovers (innovators can't capture all the returns to R&D, so there is too little invention). Even with invention fixed, adoption/diffusion runs into further failures:

A. Adoption externalities (adopters create benefits for others they aren't paid for, so adoption is too slow: a positive externality):

  • Learning by doing (supply side): a producer's costs fall with cumulative production, and the know-how leaks to rivals. Early producers bear high costs while competitors share the gains, so too little early production. Example: solar panel or wind turbine costs falling as the industry scales up.
  • Learning by using (demand side): early users discover how well a product works and that information helps later users. Early adopters bear the risk but don't capture the information value. Example: early heat-pump or EV owners revealing real-world reliability.
  • Network externalities: a technology is worth more to each user the more people use it. Each adopter ignores the benefit to others, so adoption stalls (chicken-and-egg), and the economy can lock in to an incumbent. Example: EVs and charging stations; hydrogen cars and refueling.

B. Incomplete / asymmetric information:

  • Long-term uncertainty: future energy prices, future environmental policy, and real performance are uncertain, and information is a public good, so adopters wait or demand very high returns. Sellers often know more than buyers. Example: a utility delays a low-carbon plant because it doesn't know whether a carbon price will exist in ten years.
  • Principal-agent problem: the person choosing the technology is not the person paying the energy bill (or can't observe the other's effort). Example: a landlord installs a cheap, inefficient furnace because tenants pay for heat.

So "invent it and they will come" fails: diffusion itself is distorted.

Put "adopters of the new technology" on the x-axis. MPB is the adopter's own benefit; MSB adds the spillover (learning or network benefit). Adoption stops at $Q_{mkt}$, short of $Q^*$; a subsidy equal to the spillover moves it to $Q^*$.

Gillingham & Palmer: Bridging the Energy Efficiency Gap

Guide QDefine the energy efficiency gap. Why is it important to understand it? G&P pp. 1 to 6

Definition: consumers and firms fail to make energy-saving investments that appear to have a positive net present value; they behave as if they heavily discount, or undervalue, future energy savings (high implicit discount rates; Hausman 1979). Broader version: diffusion of efficient products slower than socially optimal (Jaffe & Stavins 1994). Also called the energy efficiency paradox.

Why it matters: policy (e.g., the fuel economy standards RIA) claims CO₂ cuts at "negative cost" because fuel savings exceed upfront costs. Whether that is real decides whether efficiency standards and subsidies improve welfare or just impose costs. And the reason for the gap decides the right policy: a market failure, a behavioral failure, or no failure at all.

The gap may be overstated because engineering estimates miss: hidden costs (time, lower-quality light, managerial attention; half of audited projects were rejected, Anderson & Newell), consumer heterogeneity (a summer-home AC doesn't pay off), uncertainty (irreversible investment with volatile prices raises the hurdle rate 4 to 5×), overestimated savings (engineering models overstated returns 8 to 13%), and the rebound effect.

Note: environmental externalities are not part of the gap as G&P define it (footnote 4): the gap is about investments that pay off privately.

Guide QDiscuss each behavioral explanation: self-control, reference dependence (loss aversion), limited attention, framing. pp. 8 to 11

Anomaly vs failure: a behavioral anomaly is behavior that departs from standard theory; it is a behavioral failure only if it creates a systematic gap between decision utility (what you maximize when choosing) and experienced utility (what you actually get later). To explain the gap, the bias must push systematically toward less efficient products.

  • Self-control problems (nonstandard preferences): time-inconsistent, quasi-hyperbolic ($\beta$-$\delta$) discounting. You plan to buy the efficient fridge, but when the day comes, the upfront cost looms large. Always a behavioral failure. Evidence: refrigerator choices (Tsvetanov & Segerson 2013).
  • Reference dependence / loss aversion (nonstandard preferences): utility depends on gains and losses relative to a reference point, and losses hurt more. With uncertain fuel prices and savings, the possible loss from an efficient car looms larger than the likely gain (Greene et al. 2009). May not be a failure.
  • Nonstandard beliefs: systematically wrong beliefs about savings; Allcott (2012) finds beliefs wrong but not clearly biased.
  • Limited attention (nonstandard decision-making): people process only salient information and underweight less obvious costs (sales tax at the register, shipping costs, left-digit odometer). Future fuel costs are less salient than the sticker price, so they are undervalued.
  • Framing: how choices are presented changes them (Benartzi & Thaler); hence carefully designed fuel economy labels.
  • (Also suboptimal heuristics: rules of thumb with many product variants.)
Guide QWhat policies address market and behavioral failures, and how should they be designed? How do they compare with JNS? How have three policies fared empirically? pp. 14 to 19

Market failures: imperfect info → information provision; principal-agent → information + contracts that reward landlords who insulate; credit constraints → government financing (hard to target); learning by using → subsidy; regulatory failure → fix the regulation. (Externalities → Pigouvian tax/permit, the clear first best.)

Behavioral failures: act only if decision utility ≠ experienced utility. First choice: information or a change in the choice setting (nudge): defaults, framing in terms of losses, salient labels. If neither works: second-best standards and subsidies, which suffer with heterogeneity (standards force over-investment on light users; subsidies overpay the unbiased). Fix: behavioral targeting / asymmetric paternalism, aiming policy at the people most likely to misoptimize.

Similarity to JNS: both say match a tailored policy to each specific failure, use a portfolio rather than one tool, and both recommend information programs for information failures and subsidies for learning-by-using spillovers.

Empirical evidence on three policy types:

  1. Information strategies: audits alone have limited effects for households, but firms adopted about half of audit recommendations; Energy Star may crowd out other saving (Houde); social-norm reports (OPower) cut use 2%, like an 11 to 20% price increase (Allcott 2011).
  2. Economic incentives (subsidies, rebates, feebates): mixed; free riders (would have bought anyway) vs free drivers; cost per kWh saved higher than utilities' estimates; no effect on Canadian demand growth (Rivers & Jaccard).
  3. Efficiency standards (appliances, CAFE, building codes): second-best but most politically feasible; thin evidence; tight CAFE has large welfare losses, a gasoline tax costs about 1/6 as much (Jacobsen 2013) unless behavioral failures are large; building codes cut residential electricity 3 to 5% (or ~4%; or none in some studies).

Allcott & Sunstein: Regulating Internalities paper not in folder

Guide QDefine internalities and relate them to the behavioral failures in Gillingham & Palmer.

Internalities are "costs we impose on ourselves by taking actions that are not in our own best interest": externalities that individuals impose on their future selves. Formally, the wedge between decision utility and experienced utility.

Link to G&P: each behavioral failure creates an internality. Present bias (self-control) makes you buy the cheap inefficient appliance and pay more for energy later; limited attention to fuel costs or biased beliefs about savings make you undervalue efficiency. The internality is exactly G&P's gap between decision and experienced utility, and it is the behavioral explanation of the energy efficiency gap.

Guide QWhat solutions do the authors offer for internalities, and how do they compare with the externality solutions in Stavins, JNS and Gillingham & Palmer?

Four principles for behaviorally motivated policy:

  1. Prefer taxes and subsidies to mandates/bans: a mandate is strictly worse than the optimal subsidy, because people with legitimate reasons to choose the disfavored option keep their choice.
  2. Target the distortion: aim at the most biased consumers; the optimal subsidy equals the average bias of consumers on the margin. Nudges and information are attractive because they correct the biased without distorting the unbiased.
  3. Minimize losses from regulators' imperfect information about who is biased and by how much.
  4. Maximize net benefits including internalities when choosing stringency (e.g., CAFE, appliance standards), but measure the bias empirically: evidence suggests the average bias is at most moderate.

Guide QEfficiency improvements are CAC, but they change the price of energy per unit of service. How? In what direction? How will hours of AC use (or miles driven) respond?

The consumer doesn't buy energy for its own sake; energy is a derived demand from the service (miles, hours of cooling). The price of the service is

$p_{\text{per mile}} = \dfrac{p_{\text{gas}}}{\text{mpg}}$     $p_{\text{per hour of AC}} = p_{\text{elec}} \times \text{kWh per hour}$

A fuel-economy or appliance standard raises efficiency, so the price of the service falls (\$4 gas at 20 mpg = 20¢/mile; at 40 mpg = 10¢/mile). By the law of demand, a lower price means a larger quantity of the service demanded: more miles driven, more hours of AC. So energy use falls by less than the engineering calculation (which assumes usage stays constant).

Guide QDefine the rebound effect in your own words and explain how and why it occurs. pp. 2 to 3

Rebound effect: the share of the expected (engineering) energy savings from an efficiency improvement that is "taken back" by consumer and market responses. It occurs because efficiency lowers the effective price of the energy service and raises real income, so people consume more of the service (and other goods).

$\text{Rebound} = \dfrac{\text{expected savings} - \text{actual savings}}{\text{expected savings}}$

GRW's example: an AC uses 100 kWh/yr; a more efficient one should save 10 kWh. If behavior adds back 1 kWh, actual savings are 9: rebound $= 1/10 = 10\%$. Backfire (Jevons 1865) is rebound > 100%: energy use actually rises.

Guide QDescribe the substitution and income effects of the price change for an energy efficiency improvement; illustrate with indifference curves and budget lines. pp. 5 to 6

For a zero-cost breakthrough, GRW list four adjustments:

  1. Substitution toward the energy service (now relatively cheaper): drive more miles.
  2. Substitution away from other goods (now relatively more expensive): e.g., fewer bus tickets.
  3. Income effect on the service: lower operating cost raises purchasing power, so more of the service (if normal).
  4. Income effect on other goods: spend the savings on other normal goods (a flight, or books), which use energy too.

Each adjustment raises or lowers total energy use; (1) and (3) are the direct rebound; (4) is the common definition of the indirect rebound.

Givens (illustrative): income \$100/week, other goods \$1, gas \$4/gal, mpg rises 20 → 40, so price per mile falls \$0.20 → \$0.10. Budget line pivots out around the y-intercept.

A → B (substitution): slide along the original indifference curve to the point where its slope equals the new price ratio (dashed line). Miles rise 88 → 105 even with real income held fixed.

B → C (income): shift out to the new budget line; miles rise to 116 (normal good).

Energy: engineering expects gallons to halve, $88/20 = 4.4 \to 88/40 = 2.2$. Actual $= 116/40 = 2.9$. Expected saving 2.2, actual 1.5: rebound $= (116-88)/88 \approx 32\%$.

Guide QDefine the two types of rebound: exogenous / zero-cost breakthrough vs bundled / policy-induced improvement. pp. 3 to 5
  • Zero-cost breakthrough (exogenous): an innovation raises efficiency costlessly, holding all other product attributes the same. The response is a pure rebound driven only by the cheaper service. This is what most studies call "the rebound effect."
  • Policy-induced (bundled) improvement: a policy requires higher efficiency; it may raise the product's price and change other attributes (size, weight, quietness, capacity). The response mixes the efficiency effect with responses to price and attribute changes. Right concept for evaluating a specific policy.

Neither is always better: the zero-cost version is more general (holds confounders constant); the policy version is what matters for a given policy's welfare. Confusing them exaggerates the rebound.

Guide QDirect vs indirect rebound (measurement): describe each and relate it to zero-cost vs policy-induced.

Direct rebound: the change in energy use from the substitution and income effects on demand for the efficient product itself (more driving). Measured by elasticities, which hold other attributes fixed, so it implicitly adopts the zero-cost breakthrough concept. Policy-induced direct rebounds exist too: Davis (2008) new clothes washers, elasticity −0.06 (but larger, gentler machines); Davis et al. (2015) Mexico: new fridges cut use only 7%, new ACs raised use; Gillingham (2013) −0.15 for new California vehicles.

Indirect rebound: energy used when the savings are spent on other goods; most commonly only the income effect on other goods. Sign depends on how energy-intensive the extra spending is. Estimates 5 to 15%; all assume a zero-cost breakthrough (a costly policy would shrink the income effect, so lower indirect rebound).

Guide QRelate rebound to elasticity. Which rebound concept does elasticity measure?

Since the service price is $p_{energy}/\text{efficiency}$, a 1% efficiency gain is a 1% fall in operating cost. So the elasticity of demand for the service with respect to operating cost (or fuel price) gives the direct rebound: $\text{direct rebound} \approx -\varepsilon$. An elasticity of $-0.5$ means 50% of the marginal savings are taken back.

Elasticity measures the direct rebound of a zero-cost breakthrough (other attributes held fixed, other goods ignored). Reliable estimates: short- and medium-run elasticities for gasoline/driving and electricity of −0.05 to −0.40, so direct rebound 5 to 40%, mostly 5 to 25%. Developing countries similar, roughly −0.10 to −0.40.

Guide QWhat issues arise in estimating the rebound? Discuss salience of prices vs efficiency improvements. pp. 8 to 10
  1. Causality / endogeneity: cross-sectional studies can't control unobserved heterogeneity and find too-elastic demand; simultaneity in prices.
  2. Symmetry assumption (salience): using the fuel-price elasticity assumes people respond to efficiency the same as to fuel prices. But fuel prices are more salient (you see them every fill-up and bill), so the fuel-price elasticity may overstate the direct rebound (Gillingham 2011). Other studies find no asymmetry or a bigger response to efficiency (perceived longevity); gasoline taxes appear more salient than fuel prices (Li et al. 2014), the same idea as Rivers & Schaufele.
  3. Time frame: long-run responses (where to live, what car) are bigger but harder to estimate credibly; lagged-dependent-variable OLS needs strong assumptions.
  4. External validity: elasticities vary by place (California counties, Germany vs US); don't transplant.
  5. Other goods ignored: own-good and other-good effects are never estimated jointly; multiple services using the same fuel break the fuel-price = rebound link.
Guide QDescribe the two macroeconomic rebound effects: macro price effect (explain Figure 1) and macro growth effect. pp. 14 to 16

Macro price effect: the economy-wide analog of the direct rebound, working through equilibrium prices. Efficiency (say, US cars) shifts global oil demand left, D → D′. At the old price, use would fall by $a-b$. But lower demand lowers the world oil price, and everyone (other countries, other uses) buys more, so equilibrium use falls only by $a-c$.

$\text{macro price rebound} = 1 - \dfrac{a-c}{a-b} = \dfrac{c-b}{a-b}$

Macro growth effect (the basis of most backfire claims; poorly defined, least evidence) through three channels: (1) sectoral reallocation: more productive energy use makes energy-intensive sectors grow (supply-side analog of substitution); (2) induced innovation (a TFP shock, spillovers across sectors, e.g., lighter aircraft → lighter cars); (3) re-spending freed-up resources, possibly with a fiscal-style multiplier if resources are idle.

GRW's guidance: compute the price effect from elasticities, then do sensitivity analysis on growth (11% or 21%, Barker et al.). Sectoral reallocation is likely small because energy is a complement to other inputs (Leontief → zero rebound; perfect substitutes → possible backfire). Total rebound in most cases ≤ 60% (30% micro + 25% macro price + 5% growth). No convincing evidence of backfire.

Here $a = 6$, $b = 4.5$, $c = 5$: rebound $= 1 - (6-5)/(6-4.5) = 1 - 1/1.5 \approx 33\%$.

Guide QHow is elasticity important for estimating the macro price effect?

The size depends on the relative slopes: inelastic supply and elastic demand give a big rebound (the price falls a lot and buyers respond a lot); elastic supply and inelastic demand give a small one. GRW (citing Borenstein): demand −0.4, supply 1.0 → ~30%; demand −0.6, supply 0.2 → up to 76%. Long-run oil supply is likely elastic, so 20 to 30% for oil. Because demand slopes down and supply slopes up, the macro price effect is always below 100%: backfire cannot come from the price effect alone.

Common exam traps. (1) Rebound is not backfire: 30% rebound still saves 70% of the expected energy. (2) Rebound is not automatically bad: by revealed preference, people value the extra driving; it lowers welfare only if the extra use has big external costs. GRW warn that designing policy to "mitigate the rebound" is likely counterproductive. (3) The elasticity measures only the direct, zero-cost rebound.

Exam framing. Chain for any rebound answer: standard → efficiency up → price of service down (formula) → substitution + income effects → quantity of service up → energy savings smaller than engineering estimate → size measured by elasticity (5 to 40%) → macro price effect adds ~20 to 30% for oil → total rarely above 60%, no backfire.

Kotchen: VIBAs through a public-economics lens

Guide QDefine VIBAs. Which two market failures do they address? How exactly are they "market based"? How do they affect producers' and consumers' decisions? Kotchen pp. 276 to 279

VIBAs = Voluntary- and Information-Based Approaches to environmental management: information disclosure programs (EPA Toxics Release Inventory), eco-labels (400+ in 197 countries), markets for green goods, and voluntary programs where firms meet higher standards in exchange for reputation ("green clubs").

Two market failures:

  1. Incomplete / asymmetric information: consumers can't see how products affect the environment, so they can't match choices to preferences. VIBAs supply the information.
  2. Public-good nature of environmental quality: free riding means environmental protection is underprovided. VIBAs make provision more incentive compatible.

Market based: VIBAs bundle a private benefit with the public good, so the private good's market pays for (subsidizes) the public good: "the jointly produced private benefits of a standard good effectively subsidize the voluntary provision of public goods." This privatizes part of public-good provision, so Kotchen calls it a property-rights approach.

Producers join for reputation/certification that earns a green price premium, preempts regulation, lowers liability and financing costs, and motivates employees. Consumers buy green goods to get a private good plus a contribution to a cause they value, at lower transaction cost than donating separately.

Guide QWhat are impure public goods, and how is a VIBA one? Use joint provision, bundling and the characteristics approach. pp. 279 to 285

Characteristics approach (Lancaster): people get utility from a good's characteristics (a car's safety, comfort, mpg), not the good itself; each good is a bundle of characteristics at a price.

Bundling (IO): selling items as a package; pure bundling (only the bundle) vs mixed bundling (bundle or parts separately). Bundling focuses on separately produced items; the characteristics approach on joint production (a car's size affects its mpg).

Impure public good (Cornes & Sandler): one good that jointly produces a private characteristic and a public characteristic. Shade-grown coffee jointly produces coffee (private X) and biodiversity conservation (public Y). Utility $U(X, Y)$; goods: conventional $c$ (gives X), donation $d$ (gives Y), green good $g$ (gives $\alpha$ X and $\beta$ Y per dollar).

  • $\alpha + \beta < 1$: green good never bought (cheaper to buy $c$ and donate $d$).
  • $\alpha + \beta = 1$: simple bundling = "regular good + donation" (Product RED, 1% for the Planet): no change in choices, absent transaction costs.
  • $\alpha + \beta > 1$: technological advantage; consumers never buy both $c$ and $d$. Introducing $g$ lowers the implicit price of a characteristic.

Surprise: a green good can lower environmental quality. If people buy $g$ + donations, the implicit price of coffee falls; if Y is a gross substitute for X, they cut donations and total biodiversity falls. Because Y is public, this can even lower social welfare despite more choices.

Guide QHow are VIBAs like clubs (define clubs by rivalry and excludability)? How do they differ from clubs in public economics in who provides the public good? pp. 287 to 290

Club good (Buchanan 1965): excludable but non-rival up to a point, then congestible (partly rival). Contrast: private goods (rival, excludable), pure public goods (non-rival, non-excludable). Examples: country clubs, cinemas; Tiebout communities; toll roads (Knight); ITQ fisheries.

VIBAs as clubs: a voluntary program's reputation ("brand") is shared by members and excludable (only certified firms can use the label); the program's standard is the provision rule; and it is congestible: reputation comes from differentiation, so once too many join, membership is worth less.

Difference: a Buchanan club exists to produce benefits for its members. A green club exists to get members to produce positive externalities for non-members (cleaner air and water for society) beyond what regulation requires (Potoski & Prakash). The public good is provided by members (firms, voluntarily) for everyone, not by government and not just for the club. Evaluation must therefore take the economy-wide view, not just the within-club view.

Guide QHow do club sponsors define different priorities for VIBAs? pp. 289 to 290
  • Government agencies: economy-wide view, maximize total social welfare (members' benefits + spillovers to non-members).
  • Industry associations: within-club view, maximize members' benefits (manage congestion, protect reputation).
  • Third-party NGOs: maximize provision of the environmental public good itself, subject to the club staying viable.

Implication (van't Veld & Kotchen 2011): with perfect monitoring, government should leave sponsorship to industry when public-good benefits are low (congestion is the main issue) and to NGOs when public-good benefits are high.

Guide QIn your own words: the trade-off between the membership standard (environmental quality provided), the club's reputation, participation and congestion.

A stricter standard makes each member do more for the environment and makes the label more credible (better reputation), but it makes joining more costly, so fewer firms participate. Total environmental benefit = participants × improvement each, so the toughest standard need not be best. A larger club may have to relax its standard to keep members (congestion erodes the reputation premium), and many members meeting a weak standard can give more or less total benefit. Stricter monitoring works the same way: more credibility, but costlier membership.

Shapes are illustrative (Kotchen states the trade-off in words); the point is that total provision peaks at an interior standard.

Guide QHow is the notion of transaction costs relevant to VIBAs? p. 283

If $\alpha + \beta = 1$, bundling a donation with a product should change nothing, so why is mixed bundling so common? Because donating has transaction costs: time and effort to choose a cause, decide an amount, and make the gift, which deter small donations. Bundling the donation with something you buy anyway removes those costs. Counting transaction costs in the effective price, the green good effectively has $\alpha + \beta > 1$, so it can raise public-good provision. Firms add reputation benefits on top. (Clubs also economize on transaction costs of coordination; Coase-style bargaining fails when they are high.)

Tietenberg: Disclosure Strategies for Pollution Control

Guide QDescribe the four functions of environmental information disclosure mechanisms. Tietenberg §3
  1. Detecting environmental risks: someone must invest to discover the extent and magnitude of risks (emissions, exposure, sensitivity). Who pays and what incentives they face matters.
  2. Assuring reliable information: quality as well as quantity; firms may overstate or omit. Standardized collection methods, large penalties for falsifying, ISO 14000, and a process to verify community complaints.
  3. Disseminating the information: it must reach pollutees in usable form; transparency and access.
  4. Acting on the information: through existing or new channels of pressure.

Foundation: Coase: inefficient pollution means victims' damages exceed control costs, which motivates victims to act. Objections (information is a public good; victim-pays violates polluter-pays) are fixed by making polluters supply the information and by empowering victims in other ways.

Guide QDefine the existing channels and the 2 new potential channels through which stakeholders act on information, with examples. §3.4

Your guide says 4 existing channels; the paper lists five bullets (it treats courts and legislature separately). Know all five.

Existing channels:

  • Product market: consumers choose less harmful or "green" products (pesticide-free produce, chlorine-free paper); big buyers (chains, government) amplify it.
  • Capital market: investors move money to green firms (green mutual funds), for moral reasons or expecting lower cleanup costs; stock prices react to disclosure.
  • Labor market: responsible employers find it easier to hire and keep workers.
  • Judicial system: victims sue for compensatory damages (tort law); oversight suits against enforcement agencies.
  • Legislature: information builds support for new laws when existing law is inadequate.

New channels:

  • Constitutional right to a clean environment (after Stockholm 1972; Colombia, Ecuador, Chile): a fundamental, judicially enforceable right.
  • Public enforcement powers: private enforcement to bring polluters into compliance (not compensation): citizen suits (direct, in court) or complaint actions (file with an authority).

Programs: US TRI (1986): reported releases fell 44%. Indonesia's PROPER: color ratings (black, red, blue, green, gold); half the Black plants upgraded before full disclosure; Philippines' EcoWatch copied it. Green electricity pricing: utilities let customers pay up to 15% more for renewables (56 to 80% say they would).

Foulon, Lanoie & Laplante (2002): Regulation or Information?

Guide QWhy is comparing the effectiveness of "regulation" vs "information" an economic question? Foulon pp. 169 to 171; Guide Qs

Economics is about allocating scarce resources. Regulators have limited budgets and staff (Tietenberg: systems "overburdened"), and inspections, prosecutions and court cases are costly, while publishing a list of polluters costs little ("at little additional cost"). Choosing between instruments, or how to combine them, is a cost-effectiveness question: which instrument buys the most pollution reduction per dollar of enforcement? It is also about incentives: both instruments work by changing firms' expected costs (fines vs reputational, capital-market and community pressure), and economics predicts how firms respond to each.

Guide QHow and why is the answer relevant for environmental policy? What did they find?

Setting: 15 British Columbia pulp and paper plants, 1987 to 1996. Since 1990 the Ministry of Environment publishes a list of firms "out of compliance" or "of concern."

Variables (Table III): 4 dependent variables: compliance rates COMPBOD, COMPTSS $= \frac{\text{actual} - \text{allowable}}{\text{allowable}}$ and absolute emissions ABSBOD, ABSTSS (kg/day). Regulation (3): Regul90 (new stricter 1990 standards), Prosecution (number), Fine (dummy for a fine ≥ \$1000). Information (2): Out of compliance, Of concern (appearances on the lists). Controls: production, river basin, region, kraft process dummy, lagged dependent variable.

Results: stricter standards (Regul90) had a strong effect (compliance improved ~0.16 for BOD, ~0.08 for TSS; BOD down 3,600 to 4,640 kg/day). Appearing on the out-of-compliance list improved both compliance and absolute emissions for both pollutants (BOD down about 1,190 to 1,240 kg/day; TSS compliance up 0.089 to 0.095, TSS down 1,250 to 1,280 kg/day), and only the same-year listing mattered (quick response). Fines mattered only for absolute BOD (about 1,600 to 1,900 kg/day); prosecutions had no effect on either pollutant. Overall the lists had a relatively stronger impact than fines.

Policy relevance: information disclosure creates "additional and strong incentives" for pollution control, but it complements rather than replaces traditional enforcement: "clear and strong standards accompanied with a significant and credible penalty system" plus public disclosure both belong in the regulator's arsenal. Cheap disclosure lets scarce enforcement budgets go further, which matters most for countries with weak regulatory capacity.

Common exam traps. (1) A club good is excludable; a pure public good is not. A green label is a club; the cleaner air it produces is a pure public good. (2) A green good does not automatically help the environment: it can crowd out donations. (3) Foulon's verdict is "complement," not "information is better than regulation."

Exam framing. Thread to use in long essays: Kotchen (why voluntary/information tools can work: joint production, clubs), Tietenberg (the mechanics: four functions, channels of pressure), Foulon (the evidence: lists work, but alongside credible enforcement), Kotchen's own caveat: VIBAs rarely substitute for centralized policy because of free riding.

Guide QDefine salience. Why does it matter for consumer response to market price vs tax changes in the gasoline market? R&S pp. 1 to 3

Salience: how noticeable, visible, or attention-grabbing a price component is. Tax salience is the hypothesis that a tax-induced price change produces a different demand response than an equal market-determined price change. Standard theory says a 5¢ increase is a 5¢ increase, whatever its source, so responses should be identical.

Why it matters: policymakers forecast a carbon tax's effect (emissions cut, revenue) using elasticities estimated from market price variation. If the tax is more salient, those forecasts are wrong. BC result (preferred model, monthly data, province and month-year fixed effects, log-linear): a 5¢/L market price increase cuts gasoline demand 1.8%; a 5¢/L carbon tax (about \$25/tCO₂) cuts it 12.5%: 7.1× as large. BC's tax cut gasoline CO₂ by 3.61 Mt in four years, ~85% of it due to the extra salience. Robust to IV (excise taxes, income tax revenues), dropping summer months, restricting to 2007 to 2011, and vehicle-stock controls.

Also their Figure 1: in July 2008 prices fell in both BC and Ontario; Ontario's demand rose (law of demand), but BC's kept falling. The tax acted as a demand shifter on top of its direct price effect.

Guide QGive the behavioral-economics explanations for why a tax change is more salient than a market price change. R&S §5.1
  • Inattention / cognitive costs (Chetty, Looney & Kroft; Finkelstein): consumers misoptimize or use rules of thumb, so visible price components get more weight. (Less convincing for gasoline, whose posted price already includes taxes, so the authors look elsewhere.)
  • Non-standard (other-regarding) preferences: "resentment of free-ridership." The authors' main story: the tax enters utility directly, not just through price. Without a tax, an environmentally conscious driver who cuts back just lowers costs for others who drive more (leakage), which breeds resentment and discourages contributing. A carbon tax makes everyone pay for their emissions, removing the resentment, so conscious drivers cut back more than the price alone implies. (The opposite "relief of guilt" effect would make the tax less salient; which dominates is empirical.)
  • Perceived permanence and information: a tax is a deliberate, publicized, scheduled policy expected to persist (BC's rose \$5/t each July), while market prices bounce around and are expected to revert; a tax also signals the social cost of carbon and can act as a focal point / coordinating mechanism. (GRW make the same point: Li et al. find gasoline taxes more salient than prices, perhaps due to perceived longevity.)
Guide QWhy does getting the elasticity right matter for (a) choosing fuel efficiency standards vs carbon taxes (GRW + R&S), and (b) a revenue-neutral carbon tax that cuts income taxes (Stavins)?

(a) Standards vs taxes. A standard works through efficiency (lowering the price of driving, so it triggers a rebound); a tax works through raising the price of fuel. The case for each depends on elasticities:

  • If the tax elasticity is much larger than the market-price elasticity (R&S), using ordinary price elasticities understates how much a carbon tax cuts emissions, biasing analysts toward standards. A correct (larger) tax response strengthens the case for taxes, which were already cheaper per ton (gas tax ≈ 1/6 the welfare cost of CAFE).
  • The rebound from a standard is usually measured with the fuel-price elasticity. If fuel prices are more salient than efficiency (GRW), that overstates the standard's rebound; if the response to efficiency is smaller, standards save more energy than rebound-adjusted estimates suggest. Wrong elasticities give the wrong ranking.

(b) Revenue-neutral tax. Revenue $= t \times Q(t)$. To promise income-tax cuts of equal size, the government must forecast $Q$ after the tax, which requires the right elasticity. BC underestimated the response: the tax became revenue-negative, about \$510 million (16.7%) less than forecast over four years (only 83% of expected revenue), so the income-tax cuts were not fully funded. Stavins stresses that recycling revenue through cuts in distortionary taxes is what lowers the policy's net cost (payroll/income/corporate cuts lower costs 15/26/67% vs lump-sum) and affects progressivity, so mis-forecasting revenue undermines both the efficiency gains and the distributional plan.

Common exam traps. (1) In a log-lin model the coefficient is a percent change (times 100), not units. (2) Statistical significance ≠ economic importance: always give the magnitude in meaningful units. (3) "Insignificant" means you can't reject zero, not that the effect is proven zero. (4) Salience is not irrationality by definition: R&S's leading story is other-regarding preferences.

Exam framing. A full-credit interpretation sentence has five parts: direction, magnitude with units, holding other variables constant, significance (t or p), and what it means for the economic question. Example: "Holding prices and fixed effects constant, a 5¢ carbon tax reduces per-capita gasoline use by about 12.5%, significant at 1%, roughly 7 times the response to a 5¢ market price increase, so the tax is more salient than market prices."
