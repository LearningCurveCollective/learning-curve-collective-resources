What this worksheet covers

A command-and-control instrument this time: an energy-efficiency standard (max kWh for an appliance, minimum mpg for a car). The twist is that a standard is a per-unit rule, not a cap on total energy — and making each unit cheaper to run changes how many units people use. That feedback is the rebound effect.

Reading: Kenneth Gillingham, David Rapson & Gernot Wagner (2016), “The Rebound Effect and Energy Efficiency Policy,” Review of Environmental Economics and Policy, 10(1): 68–88.

The paper's thesis in one line: rebound is real, mostly 20–40% at the micro level, almost certainly under 60% all-in, and the “backfire” story (efficiency raises energy use) has little support. And because rebound is consumers enjoying cheaper energy services, it is usually a welfare benefit of a policy, not a cost — so trying to “fix” it is counterproductive.

The key: a standard is a price cut in disguise

Key — variables:

$\eta$  =  energy efficiency of the durable (miles per gallon; hours of cooling per kWh)

$P_E$  =  price of energy (per gallon; per kWh)

$p_S = P_E / \eta$  =  operating cost of the energy service — dollars per mile, per hour of cooling. This is the price the standard changes.

$S$  =  quantity of the energy service consumed (miles driven, hours of A/C)

$E = S / \eta$  =  energy actually used

$\varepsilon$  =  elasticity of demand for the service with respect to its operating cost (negative)

$$\eta \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; p_S = \tfrac{P_E}{\eta} \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; S \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; \text{energy savings} < \tfrac{\Delta\eta}{\eta}\times E_0 \qquad\qquad \text{Direct rebound} \approx -\varepsilon$$

Rebound effect = the share of the engineering-forecast energy savings that gets “taken back” by consumer and market responses. The paper's example: an air conditioner uses 100 kWh/yr; a more efficient model should save 10; if the owner runs it more and uses 1 kWh back, rebound = 1/10 = 10%.

1

Energy-efficiency improvements are a CAC instrument, but essentially change the price of energy per unit (per hour of use, per mile). How? In what direction? How would you expect that price change to affect the number of hours an air conditioner runs (or miles driven)?

How. Consumers don't demand energy for its own sake; they demand energy services — cool rooms, miles traveled. The cost of one unit of service is the operating cost:

$$p_S = \frac{P_E}{\eta} \qquad \text{(price of energy} \div \text{efficiency)}$$

A standard raises $\eta$ (more mpg, fewer kWh per hour of cooling) while $P_E$ is unchanged. So the standard acts exactly like a cut in the price of the service. Under standard neoclassical assumptions, a 25% efficiency gain and a 20% drop in the fuel price are the same thing to the consumer — both lower $p_S$ by the same amount.

Direction: down. The standard makes each mile and each hour of cooling cheaper.

Effect on usage: by the law of demand, a lower price raises quantity demanded — more hours of A/C, more miles driven. That extra usage is the source of the rebound: energy used = $S/\eta$, and because $S$ rises when $\eta$ rises, energy falls by less than the engineering calculation ($E_0 \times \Delta\eta/\eta$) predicts. How much more usage depends on the elasticity of demand for the service (Q6).

Nobody wants gasoline; they want to get somewhere. If your car goes from 25 to 50 mpg, gas still costs the same per gallon, but each mile now costs half as much. A cheaper mile is a price cut, and people buy more of things that get cheaper — so they drive more. The law of demand did nothing unusual here; the standard just changed a price without calling itself a price change.

2

Define the rebound effect of energy-efficiency standards in your own words, and describe how and why it occurs.

Definition. The rebound effect is the share of the expected energy savings from an efficiency improvement that is lost because consumers and markets respond to the improvement. “Expected” means the naive engineering forecast: old energy use $\times$ the percentage gain in efficiency, holding behavior fixed.

$$\text{Rebound} = \frac{\text{forecast savings} - \text{actual savings}}{\text{forecast savings}}$$

Paper's example: air conditioner uses 100 kWh/yr; a more efficient one should cut 10 kWh; behavior takes back 1 kWh; rebound = 10%. A rebound over 100% — energy use goes up — is called backfire (Jevons 1865, the coal question).

How it occurs. The improvement lowers the operating cost of the energy service, so relative prices change (and real income rises). Consumers respond through substitution and income effects (Q3), and markets respond through prices and growth (Q9). Each response uses some energy back.

Why it occurs — the one-line reason: energy demand is derived from demand for services, and a standard makes services cheaper without making anyone want fewer of them. The only way to get the full engineering savings would be for people not to notice that miles and cooling just got cheaper — which they do.

Buy a more fuel-efficient car, drive more. That's the whole thing. The engineer says “double the mpg, halve the gas” — but that assumes you drive the same miles. You don't; driving got cheaper, so you drive a bit more, and some of the gas savings come back. Rebound is just the fraction that comes back: 10% rebound means you kept 90% of the promised savings.

3

Describe in words the substitution effect and the income effect of the price change in Q1, for an appliance or a fuel-economy standard. (The *illustration with indifference curves is on the Exam tab.)

The paper lists four adjustments to a zero-cost breakthrough that lowers the price of the energy service. Grouped:

Substitution effect Holding real purchasing power fixed, the service is now relatively cheaper than everything else, so the consumer (1) substitutes toward the energy service — more miles in the efficient car, more hours of A/C — and (2) substitutes away from other goods that are now relatively more expensive (and toward complements of the service).

Income effect The lower price also raises real income — the same budget buys more. So the consumer (3) buys still more of the energy service, if it's a normal good, and (4) buys more of all other normal goods too.

Car example: 50 mpg instead of 25. Substitution: drive instead of fly, take the car instead of the bus, move the vacation from the train to a road trip. Income: the $600/year saved on gas gets spent — some on yet more driving, some on other things (a flight, a new phone, dinners out), each with its own energy content.

Where they land in the rebound accounting: (1) + (3) on the energy-efficient good = the direct rebound. (4), the income effect on all other goods = the indirect rebound (as the literature usually defines it). (2) is usually assumed away (Q5).

Two separate reasons you drive more after getting a fuel-efficient car. Substitution: driving is now the cheaper way to get places, so you pick it over alternatives more often — same wallet, different choices. Income: you're also just richer, because your gas bill dropped — and richer people buy more of everything, driving included. The first is about relative prices; the second is about having more to spend.

4

Define the two types of rebound effect: exogenous / zero-cost breakthrough and bundled / policy-induced improvement.

Zero-cost breakthrough (exogenous). An innovation raises efficiency costlessly and holds every other attribute of the product fixed — same size, same price, same features, just less energy per unit of service. The consumer and market responses to this are the pure rebound effect: they capture only the reaction to efficiency itself. This is what estimating a price elasticity implicitly measures, and it is the concept most of the literature calls “the rebound effect.”

Policy-induced improvement (bundled). A regulation forces manufacturers to raise efficiency. That is usually costly (higher product price) and often changes other attributes — size, weight, capacity, noise, features. The consumer response now reflects a bundle: the efficiency gain, the attribute changes, and the cost. The rebound is measured as the gap between an engineering forecast and the empirically estimated effect of the whole policy.

Why the distinction matters. The two answer different questions. Zero-cost isolates what efficiency alone does and transfers across settings; policy-induced tells you what a specific policy actually did. Conflating them — the paper's central complaint — exaggerates rebound estimates. Neither is unambiguously better; it depends on the question. And they can differ in sign: attribute upgrades push a policy's rebound up, but the policy's cost mitigates the income and growth effects, so a costly enough policy can produce negative rebound.

Zero-cost breakthrough: a free upgrade that changes nothing but efficiency — same fridge, magically uses less power. Whatever people do next is caused by efficiency alone. Policy-induced: the government mandates a better fridge, and what actually shows up in stores is bigger, quieter, has an ice maker, and costs $200 more. People respond to all of that, not just the efficiency. If you measure their response and call it “the rebound effect,” you've mislabeled a lot of things. Keep the two apart.

5

For measurement, there is a direct and an indirect rebound effect. Describe each, and how each relates to the zero-cost breakthrough vs. policy-induced distinction.

Direct rebound. The change in energy use from the combined substitution and income effects on the energy-efficient product itself — more driving in the efficient car, more hours on the efficient A/C. It is measured by estimating an elasticity of demand with respect to operating cost, so it implicitly uses the zero-cost breakthrough approach: it asks how usage responds on the margin, holding other attributes constant. It ignores what happens to other goods. Typical range: 5–40%, most 5–25%.

Indirect rebound. The change in energy use through the demand for all other goods. The literature is inconsistent, but the most common definition is the income effect on other goods only: the fuel savings get spent, and the sign and size of the rebound depend on whether the marginal spending (a flight? books?) is more or less energy-intensive per dollar than the efficient product was. Usually estimated at 5–15%. It also assumes a zero-cost breakthrough — any cost of the improvement would shrink the income effect and thus the indirect rebound.

Policy-induced estimates measure the direct rebound of the whole bundle: engineering forecast vs. observed change. Davis (2008): households given new clothes washers showed an elasticity of only −0.06, but the washers were larger and gentler, so usage rose through attribute changes, not just price. Davis et al. (2015), Mexico “cash for coolers”: electricity fell just 7% after refrigerator replacement and actually rose after A/C replacement — bigger, quieter units plus an income effect from the cash transfer. Gillingham (2013): −0.15 for new California vehicles chosen after a price policy.

Two gaps the paper flags: nobody estimates direct and indirect jointly with comparable data (a bigger direct rebound should mean a smaller indirect one), and the substitution effects on other goods are essentially always assumed away.

Direct = you use the efficient thing more. Indirect = you spend the savings on other things that also use energy. Direct is easy to measure — watch how usage responds when the cost per mile changes — and that measurement is automatically the “pure” (zero-cost) version because it holds the car fixed. Indirect is about where the saved dollars go, and it only works as a pure number if the upgrade was free; if you paid for the efficient fridge, you have less to spend elsewhere. Policy studies measure the direct effect of the whole package and find that the package matters: new washers got used more because they were nicer, not just cheaper to run.

6

Relate the rebound effect to elasticity. Which rebound concept is measured by elasticity?

The direct rebound effect is measured by elasticity. Because the operating cost of a service is $p_S = P_E/\eta$, a 1% rise in efficiency lowers the operating cost by about 1% — the same as a 1% fall in the fuel price. So the elasticity of service demand with respect to operating cost (or, under symmetry, the fuel-price elasticity) converts directly:

$$\text{Direct rebound} \approx -\,\varepsilon_{S,p_S} \qquad \varepsilon = -0.5 \;\Rightarrow\; 50\% \text{ of marginal savings taken back}$$

Intuition: with $\varepsilon = -0.5$, a 10% cheaper mile means 5% more miles, and 5% more miles uses back half of the 10% energy saving on the margin. Using an elasticity this way is implicitly the zero-cost breakthrough approach (holds attributes fixed).

Elasticities also drive the macro price effect (Q10): the rebound $1 - (a-c)/(a-b)$ depends on the relative slopes of energy supply and demand — inelastic supply and elastic demand mean a bigger rebound. And in the growth effect, the relevant elasticities are elasticities of substitution between energy and other goods/inputs.

Elasticity is just “how much do people change their usage when the price changes.” Since a standard is a price change in disguise, the elasticity tells you the rebound almost for free: an elasticity of −0.25 means a 25% rebound. That's the direct effect — the efficient product being used more. The other rebound channels need different numbers.

7

What are some issues in estimating the rebound effect? Be able to talk about the salience of price instruments versus efficiency improvements.

1. Causality / identification. Many elasticity studies don't meet modern standards — cross-sectional comparisons of high- and low-price places can't control for unobserved differences and tend to find demand far more elastic than panel or quasi-experimental designs. The paper's Table 1 keeps only rigorous short/medium-run estimates.

2. The symmetry assumption and salience. Converting a fuel-price elasticity into a rebound assumes consumers react the same way to a cheaper mile whether it came from cheaper gas or a better engine. Evidence is mixed: Gillingham (2011) finds drivers respond less to efficiency than to fuel prices — fuel prices are more salient (you see them every fill-up; efficiency is buried in a spec sheet). If so, the fuel-price elasticity overstates the rebound. Frondel et al. (2013) find no asymmetry; Linn (2013) finds a larger response to efficiency, perhaps because it's perceived as permanent; Li et al. (2014) find gasoline taxes more salient than price swings, again from perceived longevity. Also, when several services share a fuel, the fuel-price elasticity and the direct rebound aren't the same thing.

3. Time horizon. Short-run responses (trips, routes, which car) are smaller than long-run ones (buy/scrap cars, where to live). Long-run elasticities are larger but rarely estimated credibly (lagged dependent variables, cross-sections).

4. External validity. Every elasticity is for a time and place — driving elasticities vary across California counties, let alone between the U.S., Germany (better transit, pricier gas, more elastic), and developing countries.

5. Conflating concepts. Mixing zero-cost and policy-induced estimates; nobody estimates direct and indirect effects jointly; substitution effects on other goods assumed away; costs of improvements ignored.

The rebound number usually comes from watching how people react to gas prices, then assuming they'd react the same way to a better engine. But do they? Gas prices are in your face on a giant sign; your car's mpg is a number you looked at once. If people notice prices more than efficiency (salience), the gas-price reaction overstates the rebound. Some studies find that; others find people trust efficiency more because it's permanent while prices bounce. On top of that: bad studies overstate elasticities, short-run and long-run answers differ, and a California number may not travel to Germany or Nigeria.

9

Describe the two macroeconomic rebound effects: the macro price effect (explain Figure 1) and the macro growth effect.

Macro price effect (Figure 1 — Picture 3 on the Model tab). The economy-wide analog of the direct rebound, working through the price of energy itself. An efficiency improvement (say, in U.S. cars) shifts the demand curve for oil down and left, $D \to D'$. If the price stayed put, quantity would fall from $a$ to $b$ — the engineering forecast. But lower demand pushes the world oil price down, and at the cheaper price everyone (including the rest of the world) buys more; quantity settles at $c$, between $b$ and $a$:

$$\text{Macro price rebound} = 1 - \frac{a-c}{a-b} = \frac{c-b}{a-b}$$

It is bigger when supply is inelastic (price falls a lot) and demand is elastic (buyers respond a lot). Because demand slopes down and supply slopes up, $c < a$ always — this channel cannot produce backfire on its own.

Macro growth effect. Often cited, poorly defined, and the basis of most backfire claims (Jevons). The premise: efficiency spurs economic growth, and growth uses energy. Three channels: (1) sectoral reallocation — cheaper energy inputs raise returns in energy-intensive sectors, which grow (the supply-side twin of the substitution effect); (2) induced innovation — the policy or breakthrough spills over into other sectors' productivity (lighter aircraft → lighter cars); (3) freed inframarginal resources — money no longer spent on energy gets spent elsewhere and may carry a fiscal-multiplier effect if idle resources exist. The size hinges on elasticities of substitution: backfire needs energy to be a strong substitute for other goods/inputs, but energy is more plausibly a complement (we can't consume it directly), so the authors expect this effect to be small.

Price effect: if Americans buy less oil, the world price of oil dips, and someone else — or Americans themselves — buys a little more at the lower price. Some of the saving leaks back through the market. It can never leak back all the way, because a cheaper price never brings you above where you started. Growth effect: the bigger claim that cheap energy makes the whole economy grow and a growing economy burns more. It's possible in theory, but it needs energy to be something people and firms substitute toward — and mostly energy is a sidekick to other stuff, not a substitute for it. So the authors think it's small.

10

Describe how elasticity matters in estimating the macro price effect.

The macro price rebound is entirely a function of two elasticities — the slopes of the curves in Figure 1:

Supply elasticity of energy. Inelastic supply (steep $S$) means a demand shift drops the price a lot, so quantity claws back a lot → big rebound. Elastic supply (flat $S$) means the price barely moves → small rebound. Oil supply is inelastic in the short run (capacity constraints) but the authors — citing decades of extraction innovation triggered by high prices — believe it is highly elastic in the long run.

Demand elasticity of energy. Elastic demand (flat $D$) means buyers respond strongly to the lower price → big rebound. The gasoline evidence points to inelastic demand (−0.05 to −0.40 short/medium run).

Combining them (Borenstein 2015): demand −0.4 with supply 1.0 → rebound ≈ 30%; demand −0.6 with supply 0.2 → ≈ 76%. Given likely-elastic long-run supply and inelastic demand, the authors' best guess for oil is 20–30%. The direction of each lever: more inelastic supply and more elastic demand both raise the rebound. Try the three supply settings on the Model tab's Picture 3.

Two questions decide it. When demand drops, how far does the price fall? If producers can't easily cut back (inelastic supply), the price falls a lot. When the price falls, how much more do people buy? If buyers are sensitive (elastic demand), a lot. Big price drop × sensitive buyers = big rebound. For oil, buyers aren't very sensitive and producers can adjust over time, so the rebound is probably modest — a quarter or so.

13

Discuss the implications of measuring the rebound effect for policy.

1. Backfire is not supported. The evidence doesn't back claims that efficiency gains get reversed. Total micro rebound is typically 20–40%; the macro price effect is always under 100%; growth rebound is likely small. The authors' subjective ceiling is ~60% total. The continued focus on backfire is “largely unwarranted” and distracts from the welfare question.

2. Measure the right concept for the question. Use the zero-cost breakthrough approach to learn what efficiency alone does (transferable, welfare-interpretable); use the policy-induced approach to evaluate a specific policy's energy outcome. Don't mix them — it exaggerates rebound.

3. Rebound is usually a benefit, not a cost. By revealed preference, the extra usage is consumers enjoying cheaper services. Net welfare falls only if the external costs of the extra energy exceed those private gains. So policies designed to mitigate rebound (van den Bergh 2011; Gloger 2011) are likely counterproductive — the goal is maximizing economic efficiency, not minimizing energy use.

4. Practical recipe for a policy economist. For a zero-cost breakthrough: compute the macro price effect from the best supply/demand elasticities, then run sensitivity analysis over growth-rebound values (e.g., 11% and 21% from Barker et al.). For a specific policy: each one needs its own analysis, because costs and attribute changes can push rebound up (valued attributes) or down — even negative (costly policies).

5. Include rebound in benefit-cost analysis, don't treat it as a deterrent. Policymakers should count the welfare gains and losses from rebound as part of the policy's evaluation, rather than as a reason not to pass efficiency policy.

Rebound is real but it doesn't eat the savings — you keep most of them. And the part that comes back isn't waste: it's people enjoying cheaper cooling and cheaper trips. That's a good thing unless the extra energy is very dirty. So don't design rules to stop people from enjoying their efficient stuff; count the enjoyment as a benefit, count any extra pollution as a cost, and judge the policy on the total. Measure the version of rebound that matches your question, and stop worrying about backfire.

Illustrate the substitution and income effects of an efficiency improvement using indifference curves and budget lines.

Set-up: horizontal axis = energy service $S$ (miles, or hours of cooling); vertical axis = all other goods $Y$. Income $I$. Prices $p_S = P_E/\eta$ and $p_Y = 1$. Budget line: $p_S S + Y = I$, so its horizontal intercept is $I/p_S$ and its slope is $-p_S$.

Step 1 — initial. Draw the budget line $B_0$ and the indifference curve $U_0$ tangent to it at bundle $A$. Read off $S_A$.

Step 2 — the standard hits. $\eta$ rises, so $p_S$ falls. The vertical intercept ($I$, all income on other goods) doesn't move; the horizontal intercept $I/p_S$ moves right. The budget line pivots outward around the vertical intercept to $B_1$. Nothing has been bought yet; this is just the new opportunity set.

Step 3 — substitution effect ($A \to B$). Draw a compensated budget line: parallel to $B_1$ (new relative price) but shifted in until it is tangent to the old indifference curve $U_0$. That tangency is $B$. The consumer, held on the original utility level, slides along $U_0$ toward the now-cheaper service: $S_B > S_A$. Pure relative-price response.

Step 4 — income effect ($B \to C$). Remove the compensation: shift the budget line out to $B_1$ itself and find the tangency with a higher curve $U_1$ at $C$. Because the service is a normal good, $S_C > S_B$; other goods $Y$ also rise (Hicks decomposition).

$$\underbrace{S_C - S_A}_{\text{total (direct rebound)}} \;=\; \underbrace{(S_B - S_A)}_{\text{substitution}} \;+\; \underbrace{(S_C - S_B)}_{\text{income}}$$

Tying it to rebound. The engineering forecast assumes $S$ stays at $S_A$; actual usage is $S_C$. Energy $= S/\eta$, so the horizontal move $S_A \to S_C$ times $1/\eta_{new}$ is the energy taken back — the direct rebound. The rise in $Y$ (other goods) is the seed of the indirect rebound: those goods carry energy too.

What to say about welfare: $C$ sits on a higher indifference curve than $A$. The consumer is better off — that is the paper's point that rebound is, by revealed preference, a private surplus gain.

12*

Discuss the overall welfare effects of energy-efficiency policies, and when they may be negative.

Start from the microeconomics. The substitution and income effects behind the rebound are ordinary responses to a change in relative prices. By revealed preference, consumers end up on a higher indifference curve (bundle $C$ vs. $A$ in Q3) — they enjoy private surplus gains. So the rebound itself is a welfare benefit.

Macro channels also raise welfare. The macro price effect comes from markets reaching new equilibria; sectoral reallocation moves resources to more productive uses; induced innovation raises productivity. Each is welfare-improving on its own terms.

When the net effect can be negative:

• External costs outweigh private gains. The extra energy use carries pollution. If the electricity feeding the more-used air conditioner is particularly dirty, the external damage from the rebound can exceed the consumer surplus from the extra cooling. This is the only route by which rebound reduces welfare — so the net welfare effect is ambiguous and depends on the marginal energy source.

• The policy itself is costly. A policy-induced improvement raises product prices and may force attribute changes consumers don't value (smaller, weaker, pricier). Those costs are borne whether or not there's any rebound, and a sufficiently costly policy can be welfare-reducing on its own — it can even generate negative rebound as reduced income cuts other consumption.

• Policies designed to “mitigate” rebound. Because rebound is mostly people enjoying cheaper services, trying to suppress it (van den Bergh 2011; Gloger 2011) throws away surplus — likely counterproductive from a welfare perspective. The mistake comes from targeting minimum energy use instead of maximum economic efficiency.

Bottom line (Conclusions): a zero-cost breakthrough rebound is likely to both conserve energy and increase welfare; unless it has severe external costs, rebound is a benefit of an efficiency policy, not a cost. Policymakers should count rebound's welfare gains and losses inside the benefit-cost analysis rather than treating it as a deterrent.

Cheat sheet: the one line to remember

The authors' verdict: the literature “provides little support for the so-called backfire hypothesis,” and unless it carries severe external costs, the rebound effect is a benefit of an efficiency policy, not a cost — so trying to mitigate it is counterproductive.

The rebound map — every concept in one tree

Rebound effect — share of forecast energy savings lost to consumer & market responses. Cross-cutting choice: measure a zero-cost breakthrough (pure, transferable) or a policy-induced improvement (bundle: efficiency + attributes + cost).
Microeconomic rebound (Q1–8) — the operating cost $p_S = P_E/\eta$ falls; substitution + income effects. Total ≈ 20–40%.
Direct — sub + income effects on the efficient good itself. Measured by elasticity: rebound ≈ −ε. 5–40%, mostly 5–25%. Same range in developing countries (weaker data).
Indirect — income effect on all other goods (fuel savings get spent). Sign depends on energy intensity of marginal spending. ≈ 5–15%. Assumes zero cost.
Macroeconomic rebound (Q9–11) — markets re-equilibrate; defined for zero-cost breakthroughs.
Price effect — Figure 1: $D \to D'$ lowers energy price; quantity claws back from $b$ to $c$. Rebound $= 1 - (a-c)/(a-b)$. Bigger with inelastic supply, elastic demand. ≈ 20–30% for oil; always < 100%.
Growth effect — sectoral reallocation, induced innovation, freed resources/multiplier. Basis of backfire claims. Needs energy to be a substitute; it's more likely a complement → probably small. Simulation numbers 11%, 21%.
Welfare (Q12–13) — rebound = consumers enjoying cheaper services (revealed preference) → benefit. Negative only if external costs of the extra energy exceed private gains, or the policy itself is costly. Don't design policies to suppress rebound; count it in benefit-cost analysis.
