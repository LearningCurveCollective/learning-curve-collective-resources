What changes when the other guy is thinking too

Every model you have built so far had a single decision-maker facing something impersonal. The consumer optimized against a budget line. The competitive firm optimized against a price it could not move. Even the monopolist optimized against a demand curve — and a demand curve does not react. It just sits there.

Game theory is what you do when the thing you are optimizing against optimizes back. The moment your best move depends on someone else's move, and their best move depends on yours, "maximize profit" is no longer a well-posed problem — you cannot maximize until you know what they do, and they cannot maximize until they know what you do. Game theory is the machinery for cutting that circle.

You have already met one of these. In the duopoly, firm 1's best output depended on $q_2$ and firm 2's best output depended on $q_1$. That circular structure — and the way you broke it, by finding the pair where both firms are simultaneously happy — is the whole subject in miniature. This sheet builds the general theory, and the last tab brings it back home to Cournot.

The three ingredients of a game

A game is not defined by conflict, competition, or fun. It is defined by exactly three pieces of data. Write these three down and you have specified a game completely; leave one out and the problem is not yet well-posed.

1. Players — who is choosing?

The set of decision-makers, written $\{1, 2, \dots, n\}$. Two firms, two prisoners, a kicker and a goalie. Anything not on this list (the weather, a demand curve, "the market") is not a player — it does not choose, so it cannot be strategic.

2. Strategies — what can each player choose?

For each player $i$, a set $S_i$ of available strategies. It might be a short list (Confess or Stay silent) or an infinite continuum (any quantity $q_i \ge 0$). A strategy profile $s = (s_1, s_2, \dots, s_n)$ is one choice by every player — a complete description of "what happened."

3. Payoffs — how does each player rank the outcomes?

For each player $i$, a function $u_i(s)$ giving that player's payoff at every strategy profile $s$. Crucially $u_i$ depends on everyone's choice, not just $i$'s. That single fact — your payoff has other people's decisions inside it — is what makes the problem strategic.

Trap. Payoffs are utility numbers, not necessarily money. Two prisoners' payoffs are years off a sentence; two firms' payoffs are profits; a kicker's payoff is a scoring probability. All the theory needs is that higher is better for that player. And "player 2's payoff" must be written from player 2's point of view — a very common error is filling a matrix with player 1's payoffs twice.

3

Two questions that organize everything

Every game in this course is classified by the answers to two questions, and the answers tell you which tool to reach for. Learn to ask them before you write anything down.

Question 1: Do players move at the same time?

Simultaneous → nobody sees the other's choice before choosing. Draw a payoff matrix, hunt for Nash equilibrium.

Sequential → one player moves first and the second sees it. Draw a game tree, solve by backward induction.

"Simultaneous" really means uninformed, not literally at the same instant. Two firms mailing sealed bids on different days are still playing a simultaneous game, because neither sees the other's bid before committing.

Question 2: Is this played once, or over and over?

One-shot → no future, so no way to punish. Cheating is free.

Repeated → today's cheating buys tomorrow's punishment. Cooperation can become self-enforcing.

This is why the same cartel that must collapse on paper survives for years in practice. Nothing about the one-shot payoffs changed — the horizon did.

Describing a game

These are the terms you need before any technique makes sense. Each entry gives the formal statement first and the plain-English translation second — read both, and make sure you can say why they are the same sentence.

To keep the notation tractable, almost every definition below is also instantiated in one running example — the cartel game that the rest of this sheet keeps returning to:

The cartel game. Two firms sell into demand $p = 120 - Q$ with $MC = 30$. Each simultaneously picks one of two outputs: Collude ($q = 22.5$, half the monopoly quantity) or Compete ($q = 30$, the Cournot quantity). Profits, computed cell by cell in the Simultaneous tab:

$$u_i(\text{Collude},\text{Collude}) = 1012.5 \qquad u_i(\text{Compete},\text{Compete}) = 900$$ $$u_1(\text{Compete},\text{Collude}) = 1125 \qquad u_1(\text{Collude},\text{Compete}) = 843.75$$

Whenever a formula below looks abstract, drop these numbers into it — every quantifier ("for all $s_{-i}$…") turns into a two-line check you can actually do. Definitions that need sequencing or randomness borrow the entry game (Sequential tab) or Matching Pennies (Mixed tab) instead.

Game (in normal / strategic form)

A triple consisting of a set of players, a strategy set for each player, and a payoff function for each player.

$$G = \Big\langle \; N,\; \{S_i\}_{i \in N},\; \{u_i\}_{i \in N} \;\Big\rangle$$ where $N = \{1,\dots,n\}$ is the player set, $S_i$ is player $i$'s strategy set, and $u_i : S_1 \times \cdots \times S_n \to \mathbb{R}$ is player $i$'s payoff function.

Who plays, what they can do, and how much they like each outcome. The payoff matrix is just a picture of this for two players with short strategy lists.

Cartel game $N = \{1, 2\}$ (the firms); $S_1 = S_2 = \{\text{Collude}, \text{Compete}\}$; and $u_i$ is the profit table above — e.g. $u_1(\text{Compete}, \text{Collude}) = 1125$ reads “firm 1’s profit when firm 1 competes and firm 2 colludes is $1125$.” Three ingredients written down, so the game is fully specified.

Strategy vs. action

An action is a single move. A strategy is a complete contingent plan: it specifies an action at every point where the player might have to move, including points that never get reached.

In a simultaneous game the distinction is invisible — one move, one strategy. In a sequential game it matters enormously: the second mover's strategy must say "if he does A, I do X; if he does B, I do Y," even though only one branch will actually happen. Think of it as the instructions you would leave a stand-in who has to play for you while you are out of the room.

Entry game In the entry game, the Incumbent’s action is Fight or Accommodate, but his strategy is a rule like “Accommodate if entry occurs.” The Entrant enters, so only that branch is played — yet the plan for the unplayed branch is still part of the strategy, and it is exactly the part that non-credible threats hide in.

Strategy profile and the $-i$ notation

A strategy profile $s = (s_1, \dots, s_n)$ is one strategy for each player. We write $s_{-i}$ for the profile of everyone except $i$, so that $s = (s_i, s_{-i})$.

$s_{-i}$ is read "s-minus-i" and means "what everybody else is doing." It is pure notation convenience: it lets us hold the rest of the world fixed and ask what player $i$ should do about it. In a two-player game, $s_{-1}$ is just $s_2$.

Cartel game One profile is $s = (\text{Compete}, \text{Collude})$: firm 1 competes, firm 2 colludes. From firm 1’s seat, $s_{-1} = \text{Collude}$ — “the world I face is a colluding rival.” Writing $u_1(s_1, s_{-1}) = u_1(\text{Compete}, \text{Collude}) = 1125$ is the same number as before, just bracketed as “my move, their move.”

Common knowledge of rationality

Every player is rational (maximizes their own payoff), every player knows this, every player knows that every player knows it, and so on without end.

This is a strong assumption and it is doing real work. When you say "she won't play that strategy because it is dominated," you are relying on her rationality; when you then say "and since she won't, I should play this," you are relying on your knowledge of her rationality.

Cartel game Firm 1 reasons: “Firm 2 is rational, so it will notice Compete pays more in both of its scenarios and play it; knowing that, my real choice is between $843.75$ (collude against a competitor) and $900$ (compete against a competitor).” Step one uses firm 2’s rationality; step two uses firm 1’s knowledge of it. The iterated-elimination problem in Practice (P8d) needs a third layer.

Comparing strategies

Strictly dominant strategy

Strategy $s_i^{\,d}$ is strictly dominant for player $i$ if it gives a strictly higher payoff than every other strategy, no matter what the others do.

$$u_i(s_i^{\,d},\, s_{-i}) \;>\; u_i(s_i',\, s_{-i}) \qquad \text{for all } s_i' \ne s_i^{\,d} \text{ and all } s_{-i}$$

"Best in every column." If you have one, strategy is over — play it and stop thinking about the other player. This is rare and precious.

Cartel game Test $s_1^{\,d} = \text{Compete}$. Against Collude: $1125 > 1012.5$. ✓ Against Compete: $900 > 843.75$. ✓ The “for all $s_{-i}$” quantifier meant exactly two checks, both passed — so Compete is strictly dominant for firm 1 (and by symmetry for firm 2).

Strictly dominated strategy

Strategy $s_i'$ is strictly dominated if some other strategy $s_i''$ beats it against every possible $s_{-i}$.

$$\exists \, s_i'' \in S_i \;\text{ such that }\; u_i(s_i'',\, s_{-i}) \;>\; u_i(s_i',\, s_{-i}) \quad \text{for all } s_{-i}$$

A strategy you would never use, whatever you believed about the opponent. A rational player never plays one — which means you can cross it out and shrink the game. Doing this repeatedly is called iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS).

Cartel game Same two inequalities, read from the other side: Collude earns strictly less than Compete against every rival choice ($1012.5 < 1125$ and $843.75 < 900$), so Collude is strictly dominated. Cross it out for both firms and only $(\text{Compete},\text{Compete})$ survives — the game solves itself.

Weakly dominated strategy

Same, but with $\ge$ everywhere and $>$ in at least one column: never worse, and sometimes better.

$$u_i(s_i'',\, s_{-i}) \ge u_i(s_i',\, s_{-i}) \;\;\forall\, s_{-i}, \qquad \text{with strict inequality for at least one } s_{-i}$$

Weaker claim, weaker conclusion. A rational player may still play a weakly dominated strategy (it is tied in some scenarios), so eliminating these can destroy real equilibria. Eliminate strictly dominated strategies freely; treat weak dominance with care.

Entry game The cartel game has no weak dominance (every comparison is strict), so borrow the entry game’s matrix (Sequential tab): when the Entrant stays out, the Incumbent earns $10$ whether his plan says Fight or Accommodate — a tie in one column, strictly worse for Fight in the other ($2 < 5$). Fight is weakly dominated, yet $(\text{Stay out}, \text{Fight})$ is still a genuine Nash equilibrium — the one subgame perfection later rejects.

Best response

Strategy $s_i^{*}$ is a best response to a specific $s_{-i}$ if nothing else does better against that particular choice.

$$s_i^{*} \in BR_i(s_{-i}) \iff u_i(s_i^{*},\, s_{-i}) \ge u_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}) \quad \text{for all } s_i \in S_i$$

Dominance asks "best against everything?"; best response asks "best against this?" Best responses always exist in a finite game; dominant strategies usually do not. Nearly every technique on this sheet is best-response bookkeeping.

Cartel game Fix $s_{-1} = \text{Collude}$. Firm 1 compares $1012.5$ (collude too) against $1125$ (compete) and takes the bigger: $BR_1(\text{Collude}) = \text{Compete}$. Fix $s_{-1} = \text{Compete}$ instead: $843.75$ vs $900$, so $BR_1(\text{Compete}) = \text{Compete}$ as well. One answer per scenario — that is all a best response is.

Solutions

Nash equilibrium (pure strategy)

A strategy profile in which every player is playing a best response to what the others are actually doing — so no single player can gain by unilaterally switching.

$$s^{*} = (s_1^{*},\dots,s_n^{*}) \text{ is a Nash equilibrium} \iff u_i(s_i^{*},\, s_{-i}^{*}) \;\ge\; u_i(s_i,\, s_{-i}^{*}) \quad \forall\, s_i \in S_i,\; \forall\, i \in N$$

A situation with no regret: look at what everyone else did, and you would not change your own move. Note the word unilaterally — one player deviating, alone, with everyone else held fixed. Two players deviating together is not a test of Nash equilibrium, which is exactly why a Nash equilibrium can be terrible for everyone.

Cartel game Candidate: $s^{*} = (\text{Compete}, \text{Compete})$. Firm 1’s check: $u_1(\text{Compete},\text{Compete}) = 900 \ge u_1(\text{Collude},\text{Compete}) = 843.75$. ✓ Firm 2’s check is symmetric. ✓ Every inequality in the definition holds, so it is a NE. Now try $(\text{Collude},\text{Collude})$: firm 1’s check fails, $1012.5 < 1125$ — a unilateral switch to Compete pays. Not a NE, even though both firms prefer it to the real one.

Mixed strategy

A probability distribution over a player's pure strategies: play $s_i^1$ with probability $p_1$, $s_i^2$ with probability $p_2$, and so on, with $\sum p_k = 1$. Payoffs are then expected payoffs.

Nash's theorem (1950): every finite game — finitely many players, finitely many strategies each — has at least one Nash equilibrium, once mixed strategies are allowed.

Deliberate unpredictability. Some games (penalty kicks, auditing, bluffing) have no sensible predictable play, and the equilibrium is a coin flip with the right bias.

Matching Pennies In Matching Pennies, “play Heads with probability $\tfrac12$” is a mixed strategy. Against it, choosing Heads pays $\tfrac12(+1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0$ in expectation — that weighted average is the expected payoff the definition refers to. The cartel game never needs this: it already has a pure equilibrium.

Pareto efficiency and Pareto dominance

Outcome $A$ Pareto dominates outcome $B$ if every player is at least as well off at $A$ and at least one is strictly better off. An outcome is Pareto efficient if nothing Pareto dominates it.

A completely separate question from Nash equilibrium. Nash asks will it happen?; Pareto asks is it good? The entire drama of the Prisoner's Dilemma is one outcome answering yes to the first and no to the second.

Cartel game $(\text{Collude},\text{Collude})$ pays $(1012.5,\, 1012.5)$; the equilibrium $(\text{Compete},\text{Compete})$ pays $(900,\, 900)$. Both firms do strictly better in the first, so it Pareto dominates the equilibrium — the equilibrium is not Pareto efficient. One game, opposite verdicts from the two tests: that tension is the whole cartel story.

Subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPNE)

A strategy profile that is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame — that is, the plan is still optimal starting from any node of the tree, including nodes that equilibrium play never reaches.

The sequential-game refinement. It rules out equilibria propped up by threats a player would never actually want to carry out. Backward induction is the algorithm that finds it.

Entry game The entry game has two NE: $(\text{Enter};\, \text{Accommodate})$ and $(\text{Stay out};\, \text{Fight})$. Only the first is subgame perfect — inside the subgame where entry has already happened, Fight pays the Incumbent $2$ against Accommodate’s $5$, so a plan that says “Fight” is not optimal in that subgame, and the second equilibrium fails the test.

Credible vs. non-credible threat

A threat is credible if carrying it out is a best response at the moment it would have to be carried out. Otherwise it is non-credible: cheap talk that a rational opponent should ignore.

"Enter my market and I'll burn the place down" is not a strategy, it is a bluff — unless burning the place down is genuinely your best move once they are in.

Entry game “Fight if you enter” is non-credible in the base entry game ($2 < 5$ at the moment of truth). Give the Incumbent pre-built capacity so fighting pays $8 > 5$, and the identical sentence becomes credible — nothing about the words changed, only the payoff at the node where the threat would be executed.

Discount factor $\delta$

The weight a player puts on next period's payoff relative to this period's, with $0 \le \delta < 1$. A payoff $\pi$ received $t$ periods from now is worth $\delta^{t}\pi$ today.

$$\delta = \frac{1}{1+r} \quad \text{for interest rate } r, \qquad \text{or } \delta = (\text{patience}) \times \Pr(\text{the game continues})$$

How much you care about the future. $\delta$ near $0$: live for today, cheat now. $\delta$ near $1$: the future dwarfs today, so a lasting punishment is terrifying and cooperation can hold.

Cartel game At $\delta = 0.6$, next period’s $1012.5$ of cartel profit is worth $0.6 \times 1012.5 = 607.5$ today, the period after $0.6^2 \times 1012.5 = 364.5$, and the whole future stream $\tfrac{1012.5}{1-0.6} = 2531.25$. Since $0.6 > \delta^{*} = \tfrac{9}{17} \approx 0.529$, that future is big enough to keep both firms honest — the Repeated tab does this computation in full.

Dominance: the easy case

Finding a strictly dominant strategy

Concept

A strategy is strictly dominant if it wins in every scenario the opponent could create. The test is mechanical, and the key discipline is compare within a column, never across cells diagonally. Holding the opponent's choice fixed is the entire point — you are asking "given that they do this, what is best for me?"

Procedure (row player). Cover up all columns but one. Within that column, circle the row with the highest green number. Repeat for every column. If the same row wins every time, that row is strictly dominant.

Worked Example

Given: the Prisoner's Dilemma matrix above. Find: whether player 1 has a strictly dominant strategy.

0

The plan. There are two things player 2 might do. We check player 1's two options against each of them separately, and see whether one option wins both times.

1

Suppose player 2 stays silent (left column). Player 1 compares $u_1(\text{Silent}, \text{Silent}) = -1$ against $u_1(\text{Confess}, \text{Silent}) = 0$. Since $0 > -1$, Confess is better.

2

Suppose player 2 confesses (right column). Player 1 compares $u_1(\text{Silent}, \text{Confess}) = -10$ against $u_1(\text{Confess}, \text{Confess}) = -5$. Since $-5 > -10$, Confess is better again.

3

Apply the definition. Confess strictly beat Silent for every $s_{-1}$, which is exactly the condition $u_1(s_i^{\,d}, s_{-i}) > u_1(s_i', s_{-i})\ \forall s_{-i}$.

$$\boxed{\textit{Confess is strictly dominant for player 1}}$$

By symmetry of the matrix, Confess is strictly dominant for player 2 as well.

So the outcome is (Confess, Confess), payoffs $(-5,-5)$. Notice what just happened: both players played their individually unbeatable strategy and landed on an outcome that is worse for both than $(-1,-1)$. Nobody was irrational and nobody was tricked. That is the dilemma.

Practice

In a two-firm advertising game, each firm chooses Ad or No ad. Payoffs (in $\$$m): $(\text{No},\text{No}) = (10,10)$; $(\text{No},\text{Ad}) = (2,14)$; $(\text{Ad},\text{No}) = (14,2)$; $(\text{Ad},\text{Ad}) = (6,6)$. Does firm 1 have a strictly dominant strategy? What is the equilibrium, and is it Pareto efficient?

Against No ad: $14 > 10$, so advertise. Against Ad: $6 > 2$, so advertise. Ad is strictly dominant; by symmetry so is firm 2's. Equilibrium is $(\text{Ad},\text{Ad}) = (6,6)$, and it is not Pareto efficient — $(10,10)$ makes both strictly better off. Another Prisoner's Dilemma wearing a different costume.

Best responses: the method that always works

The underlining method

Concept

Dominant strategies are a luxury; most games do not have them. The best-response method works on every finite matrix, and it is nothing more than the Nash definition made visual. A Nash equilibrium is a cell where both players are simultaneously best-responding — so mark each player's best responses and look for a cell carrying both marks.

Procedure.

1. Go down each column. Underline the largest green number in that column — that is the row player's best response to that column.

2. Go across each row. Underline the largest copper number in that row — the column player's best response to that row.

3. Any cell with both numbers underlined is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. Cells with one mark or none are not.

Why cartels fall apart

Collusion is a Prisoner's Dilemma in disguise

Concept

Two firms in a duopoly can see the arithmetic as clearly as you can: total industry profit is highest when they behave as one monopolist and split the proceeds. So why does that agreement fail? Not because firms are stupid, and not because collusion is illegal — it fails for the same structural reason the prisoners confess. The agreed-upon output is not a best response to itself.

Given: market demand $p = 120 - Q$ where $Q = q_1 + q_2$; both firms have $MC = 30$ and no fixed costs. Each firm chooses one of two quantities: Collude $= 22.5$ (half the monopoly output) or Compete $= 30$ (the Cournot output).

Worked Example

Find: the payoff matrix, the Nash equilibrium, and whether it is Pareto efficient.

0

The plan. Each cell is one pair of quantities. For each pair, add the quantities to get $Q$, read the price off the demand curve, and compute each firm's profit as $\pi_i = (p - MC)\,q_i$. Four cells, four repetitions of the same three-line calculation. Then apply the underlining method.

1

Both collude. $Q = 22.5 + 22.5 = 45$, so $p = 120 - 45 = 75$ and $\pi_i = (75-30)(22.5) = 45 \times 22.5 = 1012.5$ each. (Total $2025$ — exactly the monopoly profit, as it must be, since the pair is reproducing the monopolist's output.)

When one player moves first

Change one thing about a game — let the second player see the first player's move before choosing — and the answer can change completely. Information is not a detail; it is part of the specification of the game.

Sequential games are drawn as game trees (the extensive form) rather than matrices. The vocabulary:

  • A node is a point where some player must move; it is labelled with whose turn it is. The leftmost node is the root.
  • A branch is an action available at that node.
  • A terminal node is an endpoint, labelled with the payoff pair $(u_1, u_2)$ — still in the order (first mover, second mover).
  • A subgame is any node together with everything that follows it — a smaller game hanging off the tree.

Strategy still means "complete plan." The incumbent below has one decision node, so a strategy is a single action. But if a player could be called on to move at two different nodes, their strategy must specify an action at both, even if the second is only reached when the first went differently. Counting strategies in a tree is a classic exam trap: two nodes with two actions each gives $2 \times 2 = 4$ strategies, not $2$.

2

Backward induction

Solve the end first, then work backwards

Concept

The first mover cannot decide anything until she knows how the second mover will react — so we solve the last decision first, where there is nothing left to anticipate and the choice is a simple comparison of numbers. Then we replace that whole subgame by its predicted payoff and step back one node. Repeat until you reach the root.

Algorithm. (1) Find the decision nodes closest to the terminal nodes. (2) At each, the player picks the branch with their own highest payoff — prune the others. (3) Replace that node by the payoff pair of the surviving branch. (4) Move one step toward the root and repeat. The surviving path is the equilibrium path; the full list of chosen actions at every node is the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium.

Worked Example

Given: A potential Entrant decides whether to enter a market. If she enters, the Incumbent chooses to Fight a price war or Accommodate. Payoffs in millions of dollars, listed as (Entrant, Incumbent).

Payoffs are (Entrant, Incumbent). The gold box is the equilibrium outcome, derived below.

Find: the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium and the resulting payoffs.

0

The plan. Two decision nodes. Solve the Incumbent's node first, because at that point the Entrant has already moved and there is nothing left to predict. Then hand the Entrant the Incumbent's known reaction and let her choose.

1

The last mover. Suppose entry has happened. The Incumbent compares his own payoffs: Fight gives $2$, Accommodate gives $5$. Since $5 > 2$, he accommodates. Prune the Fight branch.

Read only the second coordinate here. The Entrant's $-2$ versus $4$ is irrelevant to a decision the Incumbent is making.

2

Fold the subgame. The entire "Incumbent moves" subgame now has a known value: $(4, 5)$. Replace that node with those numbers, and the tree collapses to a single choice by the Entrant.

3

The first mover. The Entrant compares Enter, worth $4$ (because she correctly forecasts accommodation), against Stay out, worth $0$. Since $4 > 0$, she enters.

4

State the equilibrium as strategies, not just an outcome.

$$\boxed{\text{SPNE} = \big(\ \text{Enter}\ ;\ \text{Accommodate if entry occurs}\ \big), \quad \text{payoffs } (4,\,5)}$$

The Incumbent's part of the equilibrium is a conditional plan, which is why it is written with an "if." That is the strategy-versus-action distinction earning its keep.

1

The same game, played again tomorrow

The one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma has a grim verdict: cooperation is impossible between rational players. But real cartels hold for years, real neighbours keep promises, and real firms sometimes maintain high prices without any enforceable contract. The theory is not wrong — the game was mis-specified. Those players are not playing once.

Stage game and repeated game

The stage game is the one-shot game (our matrix). The repeated game is the stage game played in periods $t = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, with all past play observed before each new round. A player's total payoff is the discounted sum of stage payoffs:

$$U_i = \pi_i^{(1)} + \delta\,\pi_i^{(2)} + \delta^{2}\pi_i^{(3)} + \cdots = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \delta^{\,t-1}\,\pi_i^{(t)}$$

Repetition changes nothing about the stage payoffs and everything about the strategy sets. Now a strategy can say "I will do X, unless you did Y last period" — behaviour can be conditioned on history, and that is what makes punishment possible.

Finite repetition: the unraveling problem

Why a known last round destroys cooperation

Concept

Suppose the Prisoner's Dilemma is played exactly $T = 10$ times, and both players know it. Surely ten rounds is enough room to build cooperation? Backward induction says no — and this is the same algorithm from the sequential tab, applied to the calendar instead of a tree.

0

The plan. Start at the final round, where the future is empty, and work backwards one round at a time — showing at each stage that the round we just solved gives no leverage over the round before it.

1

Round 10 (the last). Nothing follows it. Whatever a player does here cannot be punished, so round 10 is a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. Both defect. This is certain, and both players know it is certain.

2

Round 9. Cooperating in round 9 could only pay if it bought better behaviour in round 10 — but round 10's outcome is already pinned down as mutual defection regardless of what happens in round 9. So round 9 also has an empty future in the only sense that matters. Both defect.

3

Induct. The same sentence now applies to round 8, then 7, and so on down to round 1. Every round's future is fixed, so every round is effectively one-shot.

$$\boxed{\text{With a known, finite } T:\ \text{the unique SPNE is defection in every round}}$$

What actually does the damage. Not the finiteness — the certainty about the end. The argument needs a known last round to get started. Remove that single ingredient and the whole chain fails to launch, which is the subject of the next section. This is also why the theory predicts cheating in a firm's final quarter before liquidation, or in the last week before a supplier relationship ends.

3

Infinite (or indefinite) repetition and the grim trigger

Making cooperation a best response

Concept

If the game has no known end — either it truly runs forever, or each period there is a probability it continues — then no round is "the last," and the unraveling argument never starts. Now punishment has teeth, and the simplest punishment plan is the grim trigger strategy:

Grim trigger: cooperate in period 1; in every later period, cooperate if and only if nobody has ever defected. After a single defection by anyone, play the stage-game Nash equilibrium forever.

Two facts about $\delta$ before the algebra. First, patience and survival collapse into the same parameter: if you value next year's dollar at $\tfrac{1}{1+r}$ and the relationship also survives to next year only with probability $\rho$, the effective discount factor is $\delta = \tfrac{\rho}{1+r}$. Second, the sum you will need:

$$1 + \delta + \delta^{2} + \delta^{3} + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-\delta} \qquad \text{for } 0 \le \delta < 1$$

(Geometric series. Sanity-check it: at $\delta = 0.5$ the sum is $2$, meaning a stream of $\$1$ per period forever is worth $\$2$ today — the future is worth as much as the present. At $\delta = 0.9$ it is $10$; at $\delta \to 1$ it explodes, because a patient player weights an endless future almost as heavily as today.)

Worked Example

Given: the cartel game from the Simultaneous tab — $p = 120 - Q$, $MC = 30$ — repeated forever with discount factor $\delta$. Both firms play grim trigger around the collusive quantity $22.5$. The relevant per-period profits are:

  • $\pi^{C} = 1012.5$ — collude, and the other firm colludes.
  • $\pi^{D} = 1139.0625$ — the best one-period cheat against a colluder: $q_1 = \tfrac{90-22.5}{2} = 33.75$, giving $Q = 56.25$, $p = 63.75$, $\pi = (63.75-30)(33.75)$.
  • $\pi^{N} = 900$ — the Cournot (stage-Nash) profit, earned forever after a defection.

Find: the smallest $\delta$ for which collusion is sustainable.

0

The plan. Grim trigger is an equilibrium exactly when neither firm wants to deviate. So write the present value of the two lifetime plans — cooperate forever, or cheat once and take the punishment forever — and impose that cooperating is at least as good. That inequality will contain only $\delta$, and solving it gives the threshold.

1

Value of cooperating forever. The firm collects $1012.5$ this period and every period after, discounted:

$$PV_{C} = 1012.5\,(1 + \delta + \delta^{2} + \cdots) = \frac{1012.5}{1-\delta}$$

2

Value of cheating. One fat period at $1139.0625$, then the trigger fires and both firms play Cournot forever starting next period. The punishment stream starts one period late, so it carries a factor of $\delta$:

$$PV_{D} = 1139.0625 + \delta\big(900 + 900\delta + \cdots\big) = 1139.0625 + \frac{900\,\delta}{1-\delta}$$

Forgetting that leading $\delta$ is the single most common error here. Punishment cannot begin until the other firm has seen the defection.

3

Impose no-deviation. Collusion holds when $PV_{C} \ge PV_{D}$:

$$\frac{1012.5}{1-\delta} \;\ge\; 1139.0625 + \frac{900\,\delta}{1-\delta}$$

4

Clear the denominators. Multiply both sides by $(1-\delta)$, which is strictly positive for $\delta < 1$, so the inequality direction is preserved:

$$1012.5 \;\ge\; 1139.0625\,(1-\delta) + 900\,\delta$$

5

Collect the $\delta$ terms. Expanding the right-hand side gives $1139.0625 - 1139.0625\delta + 900\delta = 1139.0625 - 239.0625\,\delta$. So

$$1012.5 \ge 1139.0625 - 239.0625\,\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 239.0625\,\delta \ge 126.5625$$

6

Solve.

$$\delta \;\ge\; \frac{126.5625}{239.0625} = \frac{9}{17} \approx \boxed{0.529}$$

If both firms weight next period at more than about $53\%$ of this period, the cartel is self-enforcing — no contract, no lawyer, no trust required, just arithmetic. Below that threshold it collapses in period 1.

In interest-rate terms, $\delta = \tfrac{1}{1+r}$ gives $r \le \tfrac{1-\delta}{\delta} = \tfrac{8}{9} \approx 89\%$ per period. A very impatient firm — or one that expects the market to disappear soon — cheats.

Games where being predictable is the mistake

Matching Pennies had no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium: in every cell, exactly one player wanted to move, so the underlining method produced a chase with no resting point. The same structure shows up whenever one player wants to match and the other wants to differ — a penalty kick, a tax audit, a bluff, a surprise inspection.

In those games any predictable rule is exploitable. If the goalie knows you always shoot right, he waits right. The only stable behaviour is deliberate randomization, and a probability distribution over your pure strategies is called a mixed strategy.

Mixed strategy and expected payoff

Let the row player play Up with probability $p$ and Down with $1-p$; let the column player play Left with probability $q$ and Right with $1-q$. Payoffs become expectations, weighting each cell by the probability it occurs:

$$EU_{1}(p,q) = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \Pr(\text{row } i)\Pr(\text{col } j)\, u_{1}(i,j)$$

Because the two players randomize independently, the probability of landing in a given cell is just the product of the two probabilities. A pure strategy is the special case $p = 1$ or $p = 0$ — mixed strategies do not replace pure ones, they contain them.

The indifference principle

The one idea that solves every mixed equilibrium

Concept

Here is the argument, and it is worth reconstructing rather than memorizing.

1

Suppose in equilibrium the row player genuinely mixes — that is, $0 < p < 1$, so both Up and Down are actually used.

2

If Up had a strictly higher expected payoff than Down against the opponent's mix, the row player would do strictly better by shifting all probability onto Up. Then $p=1$, contradicting the assumption that she mixes.

3

So a player only mixes when she is exactly indifferent between the strategies she is mixing over. Her own probabilities are not what makes her indifferent — the opponent's probabilities are.

The rule, stated carefully: in a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium, each player chooses probabilities that make the other player indifferent between their pure strategies. You solve for $p$ (the row player's mix) using the column player's payoffs, and for $q$ using the row player's payoffs.

The most common error on this topic is solving for your own mix using your own payoffs. It feels natural and it is always wrong. Say the sentence out loud while you work: "I am choosing $p$ to make him indifferent."

Worked Example

Given: Matching Pennies. Row wins (payoff $+1$) when the coins match, loses ($-1$) otherwise; the column player's payoffs are the negatives. Find: the mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium.

0

The plan. Two unknowns, $p$ and $q$, so we need two equations. One comes from making the column player indifferent (that equation contains $p$); the other from making the row player indifferent (that one contains $q$).

1

Set up the column player's expected payoffs, treating $p$ as given. Reading down the Heads column, then the Tails column, using only copper numbers:

$$EU_{2}(\text{Heads}) = p(-1) + (1-p)(+1) = 1 - 2p$$ $$EU_{2}(\text{Tails}) = p(+1) + (1-p)(-1) = 2p - 1$$

2

Impose indifference and solve for $p$.

$$1 - 2p = 2p - 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2 = 4p \;\Longrightarrow\; p = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

3

Repeat from the row player's side, now using green numbers and treating $q$ as given:

$$EU_{1}(\text{Heads}) = q(+1) + (1-q)(-1) = 2q-1, \qquad EU_{1}(\text{Tails}) = q(-1)+(1-q)(+1) = 1-2q$$ $$2q - 1 = 1 - 2q \;\Longrightarrow\; q = \tfrac{1}{2}$$

4

$$\boxed{\big(p^{*}, q^{*}\big) = \big(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\big), \qquad EU_1 = EU_2 = 0}$$

Each player flips a fair coin, and each expects zero. Neither can do better: against a fair coin every strategy yields $0$, so there is nothing to exploit. That is exactly what makes it an equilibrium.

Given: Battle of the Sexes. Alex (row) and Bailey (column) each pick Game or Movie; being apart pays $(0,0)$. Together at the game pays $(3,2)$; together at the movie pays $(2,3)$. Find: all Nash equilibria.

2

$$EU_{B}(\text{Game}) = 2p + 0(1-p) = 2p, \qquad EU_{B}(\text{Movie}) = 0p + 3(1-p) = 3 - 3p$$ $$2p = 3 - 3p \;\Longrightarrow\; 5p = 3 \;\Longrightarrow\; p^{*} = \tfrac{3}{5}$$

3

Choose $q$ (Bailey's probability of Game) to make Alex indifferent, using Alex's payoffs $3$ and $2$:

$$3q = 2(1-q) \;\Longrightarrow\; 5q = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; q^{*} = \tfrac{2}{5}$$

4

$$\boxed{\text{Three NE: } (G,G),\;(M,M),\;\text{and } \big(p^{*}=\tfrac{3}{5},\, q^{*}=\tfrac{2}{5}\big)}$$

Alex's expected payoff in the mixed equilibrium is $3q^{*} = \tfrac{6}{5} = 1.2$, and Bailey's is $2p^{*} = \tfrac{6}{5} = 1.2$ — worse for both than either pure equilibrium, because they now show up at different places $52\%$ of the time. Mixed equilibria are often bad for everyone; being an equilibrium is a statement about stability, not about quality.

The reveal: you already solved a game

Everything so far used games with short menus — two or three named choices, a grid you could draw. Cournot duopoly has the same structure with one change: the strategy set is a continuum. Firm $i$ may choose any quantity $q_i \ge 0$.

Cournot duopoly as a game, in the three-ingredient format:

Players: $N = \{1, 2\}$, the two firms.

Strategies: $S_i = [0, \infty)$ — a quantity, chosen simultaneously (neither firm sees the other's before committing).

Payoffs: $\;\pi_i(q_1, q_2) = \big[\,a - b(q_1 + q_2) - c\,\big]\,q_i$ — profit, which depends on both quantities through the market price.

The matrix is now infinitely wide and infinitely tall, so underlining is out. But the definition of Nash equilibrium never mentioned matrices — it said each player's strategy must be a best response to the others'. With a continuum, "find the best response" changes from "scan a column for the biggest number" to "take a derivative and set it to zero." That is the only difference.

Two names, one object. What you called the reaction function $q_1 = R_1(q_2)$ in the market-power sheet is exactly the best-response function $BR_1(q_2)$ of this sheet. What you called the Cournot equilibrium — the point where the reaction curves cross — is exactly a Nash equilibrium: the strategy profile at which both firms are simultaneously best-responding.

Best responses when the strategy set is a continuum

Deriving the best-response function

Concept

Freeze the rival's quantity. With $q_2$ fixed, firm 1 faces the residual demand $p = (a - bq_2) - bq_1$ — the market demand curve shifted left by whatever firm 2 already sells. Firm 1 is now a small monopolist on what remains, so run the monopoly recipe on the residual: set $MR = MC$. The answer, as a function of $q_2$, is the best-response function.

Worked Example

Given: $p = 120 - Q$ with $Q = q_1 + q_2$; both firms have $MC = 30$; quantities chosen simultaneously. Find: the best-response functions and the Nash (Cournot) equilibrium, and verify it satisfies the Nash definition.

0

The plan. Get firm 1's best response to an arbitrary $q_2$, use symmetry to get firm 2's, then solve the two together — the Nash condition is that both hold at once. Finally, check the definition directly.

1

Write firm 1's profit with $q_2$ held fixed.

$$\pi_1(q_1, q_2) = \big[120 - (q_1+q_2) - 30\big]q_1 = (90 - q_2)q_1 - q_1^{2}$$

2

Maximize in $q_1$. Differentiate with respect to $q_1$ (treating $q_2$ as a constant, because firm 1 cannot choose it) and set to zero — this is the $MR = MC$ condition on residual demand:

$$\frac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = (90 - q_2) - 2q_1 = 0$$

3

Solve for the best-response function.

$$q_1 = BR_1(q_2) = \frac{90 - q_2}{2}, \qquad\text{and by symmetry}\qquad q_2 = BR_2(q_1) = \frac{90 - q_1}{2}$$

Endpoint check: $q_2 = 0$ gives $q_1 = 45$, the monopoly output — correct, since firm 1 would then have the market to itself. $q_2 = 90$ gives $q_1 = 0$ — also correct, since the price would already be at marginal cost.

4

Impose the Nash condition: both best responses hold simultaneously. With symmetric firms set $q_1 = q_2 = q$:

$$q = \frac{90 - q}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; 2q = 90 - q \;\Longrightarrow\; 3q = 90 \;\Longrightarrow\; q^{*} = 30$$

5

Price and profits. $Q = 60$, $p = 120-60 = 60$, and $\pi_i = (60-30)(30) = 900$ each.

$$\boxed{(q_1^{*}, q_2^{*}) = (30, 30), \quad p^{*} = 60, \quad \pi_1^{*} = \pi_2^{*} = 900}$$

6

Verify against the definition of Nash equilibrium. The definition requires $\pi_1(q_1^{*}, q_2^{*}) \ge \pi_1(q_1, q_2^{*})$ for every alternative $q_1$. Hold $q_2^{*} = 30$ and ask what firm 1's best reply is: $BR_1(30) = \tfrac{90-30}{2} = 30$ — it is already doing it. Same for firm 2. Neither firm can gain by unilaterally changing quantity, which is precisely the no-regret condition.

Contrast with the collusive point $(22.5, 22.5)$: there $BR_1(22.5) = 33.75 \ne 22.5$, so firm 1 does want to move. Collusion fails the Nash test, and the arithmetic of that failure is the payoff matrix in the Simultaneous tab.

Both firms best-respond, and both end up poorer than if they had colluded. Consumers capture the difference.

Repeated game → a cartel that holds

$$\delta \ge \delta^{*} = \frac{\pi^{D} - \pi^{C}}{\pi^{D} - \pi^{N}}$$

With our numbers, $\delta^{*} = 9/17$. Patient firms in a long-lived market can hold monopoly prices with no contract at all.

Stackelberg: Cournot with a first mover next sheet

Concept

Take the Cournot game and change exactly one thing — firm 1 commits to $q_1$ first, and firm 2 observes it before choosing. Simultaneous becomes sequential, so the tool changes from "solve both best responses at once" to backward induction. A preview of the mechanics:

1

Solve the follower first (backward induction). Firm 2 sees $q_1$ and best-responds: $q_2 = \tfrac{90 - q_1}{2}$ — the same reaction function as before. Nothing new here; what is new is that firm 1 knows this and can use it.

2

Substitute the follower's reaction into the leader's profit, so firm 1 optimizes along firm 2's reaction curve rather than against a guess:

$$\pi_1 = \left[120 - q_1 - \tfrac{90-q_1}{2} - 30\right]q_1 = \left(45 - \tfrac{q_1}{2}\right)q_1$$

3

Maximize. $\tfrac{d\pi_1}{dq_1} = 45 - q_1 = 0 \Rightarrow q_1 = 45$, hence $q_2 = \tfrac{90-45}{2} = 22.5$, $Q = 67.5$, $p = 52.5$.

$$\boxed{\pi_1 = 1012.5 \;>\; 900, \qquad \pi_2 = 506.25 \;<\; 900}$$

Moving first is worth $112.5$ to the leader and costs the follower $393.75$. Note the leader produces the monopoly quantity $45$ — and note that this only works because the commitment is observable and irreversible. An announcement firm 1 could quietly reverse would be a non-credible threat, and firm 2 would ignore it and play Cournot.

Also coming: Bertrand. Change a different thing — let firms compete in prices rather than quantities, with identical products. Undercutting a rival by a penny captures the whole market, so each firm keeps undercutting until $p = MC$ and profits hit zero — with only two firms. Same players, same costs, same demand, and a completely different equilibrium. That contrast, and what it says about which model to reach for, is where the next sheet starts.

1

Dominance and the dilemma. Two neighbouring countries choose Arm or Disarm. Payoffs (Country 1, Country 2): $(\text{D},\text{D}) = (8,8)$; $(\text{D},\text{A}) = (1,10)$; $(\text{A},\text{D}) = (10,1)$; $(\text{A},\text{A}) = (3,3)$.

a Draw the matrix with rows = Country 1. Then, holding Country 2 at Disarm, which strategy is better for Country 1?

Compare $u_1(\text{A},\text{D}) = 10$ against $u_1(\text{D},\text{D}) = 8$. Arm is better. Remember to compare within the Disarm column only — the other column is a different scenario.

b Now hold Country 2 at Arm. Which is better for Country 1? What can you conclude?

$u_1(\text{A},\text{A}) = 3 > u_1(\text{D},\text{A}) = 1$, so Arm again. Arm beat Disarm in every column, so Arm is strictly dominant for Country 1 — and by symmetry for Country 2.

c State the Nash equilibrium (as strategies, then payoffs). Is it Pareto efficient? Name the outcome that Pareto dominates it, if any.

NE $= (\text{Arm},\text{Arm})$ with payoffs $(3,3)$. Not Pareto efficient: $(\text{Disarm},\text{Disarm}) = (8,8)$ makes both strictly better off. This is a Prisoner's Dilemma — an arms race is the same game as two prisoners confessing.

d A treaty is signed promising mutual disarmament, with no enforcement. Using the definition of Nash equilibrium, explain in one sentence why it fails.

$(\text{Disarm},\text{Disarm})$ is not a Nash equilibrium because a unilateral deviation to Arm raises that country's payoff from $8$ to $10$ — and Nash equilibrium tests exactly that one-player-at-a-time deviation. Joint optimality is not stability.

2

Best responses with no dominant strategy. Two firms pick a technical standard, $A$ or $B$. Payoffs (Firm 1, Firm 2): $(A,A) = (4,3)$; $(A,B) = (1,1)$; $(B,A) = (0,0)$; $(B,B) = (3,4)$.

a Show that neither firm has a strictly dominant strategy.

Firm 1 against $A$: $4 > 0$, so $A$. Against $B$: $3 > 1$, so $B$. Different answers in different columns — no dominant strategy. Firm 2 is symmetric ($3>1$ in row $A$; $4>0$ in row $B$).

b Apply the underlining method and list every pure-strategy Nash equilibrium.

Row's best responses: $A$ in column $A$, $B$ in column $B$. Column's: $A$ in row $A$, $B$ in row $B$. Two cells carry both marks: $\boxed{(A,A) \text{ and } (B,B)}$. This is a coordination game — Nash equilibrium says both are stable but cannot say which occurs.

c Find the mixed-strategy equilibrium. Let $p = \Pr(\text{Firm 1 plays } A)$ and $q = \Pr(\text{Firm 2 plays } A)$, and remember whose payoffs set which variable.

$p$ makes Firm 2 indifferent: $3p + 0(1-p) = 1p + 4(1-p) \Rightarrow 3p = 4-3p \Rightarrow p^{*} = \tfrac{2}{3}$. $q$ makes Firm 1 indifferent: $4q + 1(1-q) = 0q + 3(1-q) \Rightarrow 1+3q = 3-3q \Rightarrow q^{*} = \tfrac{1}{3}$. So three Nash equilibria in total.

3

Build the cartel matrix. Market demand is $p = 200 - Q$ with $Q = q_1 + q_2$; both firms have $MC = 20$ and no fixed costs. Each firm chooses Collude $= 45$ or Compete $= 60$.

a Verify the two labels: show that $45$ is half the monopoly output and $60$ is the Cournot quantity. (Use $Q^{M} = \tfrac{a-c}{2b}$ and $q^{*} = \tfrac{a-c}{3b}$, or derive them.)

Here $a = 200$, $b = 1$, $c = 20$, so $a - c = 180$. Monopoly: $Q^{M} = \tfrac{180}{2} = 90$, half of which is $45$. ✓ Cournot: $q^{*} = \tfrac{180}{3} = 60$. ✓

b Compute the $(\text{Collude},\text{Collude})$ cell: find $Q$, then $p$, then each firm's $\pi = (p - MC)q_i$.

$Q = 90$, $p = 200-90 = 110$, $\pi_i = (110-20)(45) = 90 \times 45 = 4050$ each ($8100$ total, the monopoly profit).

c Compute the two off-diagonal cells and the $(\text{Compete},\text{Compete})$ cell.

Mixed: $Q = 105$, $p = 95$. Colluder gets $(95-20)(45) = 3375$; competitor gets $(95-20)(60) = 4500$. Both compete: $Q = 120$, $p = 80$, $\pi_i = (80-20)(60) = 3600$ each.

d Find the Nash equilibrium and explain why the cartel is unstable even though both firms would prefer the collusive cell.

Against Collude: $4500 > 4050$. Against Compete: $3600 > 3375$. Compete is strictly dominant, so NE $= (\text{Compete},\text{Compete}) = \boxed{(3600, 3600)}$ — Pareto dominated by $(4050,4050)$. The collusive cell is jointly better but not individually stable: each firm gains $450$ by cheating while the other holds back.

4

Backward induction and credibility. Firm A chooses Launch or Wait. If A launches, Firm B chooses Match or Concede. Payoffs (A, B): Wait $\to (2,6)$; Launch then Match $\to (1,1)$; Launch then Concede $\to (5,3)$.

a Draw the tree. Which node does backward induction solve first, and why that one?

Firm B's node — the one closest to the terminal payoffs. At that point A has already moved, so B has nothing left to predict and the choice is a plain comparison of B's own numbers. You cannot solve A's node first, because A's payoff depends on a reaction that has not been pinned down yet.

b Solve B's node, then fold the subgame and solve A's node.

B compares its own payoffs: Concede $= 3 >$ Match $= 1$, so B concedes. The subgame is therefore worth $(5,3)$. A then compares Launch $= 5$ against Wait $= 2$ and launches.

c Write the SPNE. Be careful to state B's part as a complete plan, not just an action.

$\boxed{\text{SPNE} = (\text{Launch};\ \text{Concede if A launches}),\ \text{payoffs } (5,3)}$. B's strategy has to specify what B does at its node, which is why it reads as an "if" statement.

d Write the game in normal form and find all pure Nash equilibria. Explain why one of them is rejected by subgame perfection.

Cells: $(\text{Launch},\text{Match}) = (1,1)$; $(\text{Launch},\text{Concede}) = (5,3)$; $(\text{Wait},\text{Match}) = (2,6)$; $(\text{Wait},\text{Concede}) = (2,6)$. Two pure NE: $(\text{Launch},\text{Concede})$ and $(\text{Wait},\text{Match})$. The second survives the Nash test only because B's threat is never tested — if A did launch, B would rather concede ($3 > 1$). Match is a non-credible threat, so that equilibrium is not subgame perfect.

5

Sustaining cooperation. A Prisoner's Dilemma is repeated infinitely with discount factor $\delta$. Per-period payoffs: mutual cooperation $50$; cheating on a cooperator $70$; mutual defection $30$. Both players use grim trigger.

a Write the present value of cooperating forever. (You will need $1 + \delta + \delta^2 + \cdots = \tfrac{1}{1-\delta}$.)

$PV_{C} = 50 + 50\delta + 50\delta^{2} + \cdots = \dfrac{50}{1-\delta}$.

b Write the present value of cheating once and then being punished forever. Watch where the punishment stream starts.

$PV_{D} = 70 + \dfrac{30\,\delta}{1-\delta}$. The punishment cannot begin until next period — the opponent has to observe the defection first — so the whole punishment stream is multiplied by $\delta$. Dropping that $\delta$ is the standard mistake.

c Set up the no-deviation inequality and clear the denominator.

$\dfrac{50}{1-\delta} \ge 70 + \dfrac{30\delta}{1-\delta}$. Multiply through by $(1-\delta) > 0$ (so the inequality sign is unchanged): $50 \ge 70(1-\delta) + 30\delta$.

d Solve for $\delta^{*}$, then check it against the general formula $\delta^{*} = \dfrac{\pi^{D}-\pi^{C}}{\pi^{D}-\pi^{N}}$.

$50 \ge 70 - 40\delta \Rightarrow 40\delta \ge 20 \Rightarrow \boxed{\delta \ge \tfrac{1}{2}}$. General formula: $\tfrac{70-50}{70-30} = \tfrac{20}{40} = \tfrac12$. ✓

e Interpret: using $\delta = \tfrac{1}{1+r}$, what is the highest per-period interest rate at which cooperation survives? And what happens if both players learn the game will definitely end after period $20$?

$\delta \ge \tfrac12 \iff r \le 1$, i.e. up to $100\%$ per period. With a known end at period $20$, backward induction unravels the whole thing: period $20$ has no future, so both defect; that makes period $19$ effectively final, and so on down to period $1$. Cooperation collapses entirely — it is the certainty of an end, not the finite length, that does the damage.

6

Mixed strategies: the auditing game. An employer chooses Audit or Not; a worker chooses Shirk or Work. Payoffs (Employer, Worker): $(\text{Audit},\text{Shirk}) = (2,0)$; $(\text{Audit},\text{Work}) = (-1,4)$; $(\text{Not},\text{Shirk}) = (-4,6)$; $(\text{Not},\text{Work}) = (1,4)$.

a Use the underlining method to show there is no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium.

Employer: against Shirk, $2 > -4$ so Audit; against Work, $1 > -1$ so Not. Worker: against Audit, $4 > 0$ so Work; against Not, $6 > 4$ so Shirk. Every cell has exactly one mark, so no cell has both — no pure NE. The logic chases itself: auditing makes working attractive, working makes auditing pointless.

b Let $p = \Pr(\text{Audit})$. Write the worker's expected payoff from Shirk and from Work as functions of $p$.

$EU_{W}(\text{Shirk}) = 0p + 6(1-p) = 6-6p$ and $EU_{W}(\text{Work}) = 4p + 4(1-p) = 4$. Working pays $4$ regardless — an audit does not hurt an honest worker — so that line is flat.

c Solve for $p^{*}$ and say in words what the employer is doing.

$6 - 6p = 4 \Rightarrow p^{*} = \tfrac{1}{3}$. The employer audits one third of the time — the exact frequency at which the worker no longer cares whether he shirks. Audit less and shirking becomes profitable; audit more and you are burning money on unnecessary audits.

d Let $q = \Pr(\text{Shirk})$. Solve for $q^{*}$ using the employer's payoffs, and compute the employer's expected payoff in equilibrium.

$EU_{E}(\text{Audit}) = 2q - 1(1-q) = 3q-1$; $EU_{E}(\text{Not}) = -4q + 1(1-q) = 1-5q$. Setting them equal: $3q - 1 = 1 - 5q \Rightarrow 8q = 2 \Rightarrow q^{*} = \tfrac14$. Employer's expected payoff $= 3(\tfrac14) - 1 = \boxed{-0.25}$; the worker's is $4$.

e The employer buys cheaper audit software, so $(\text{Audit},\text{Work})$ improves from $-1$ to $0$. Recompute $q^{*}$. Does the worker's shirking rate rise or fall, and why is that direction surprising at first?

Now $EU_{E}(\text{Audit}) = 2q + 0(1-q) = 2q$, so $2q = 1-5q \Rightarrow q^{*} = \tfrac17 \approx 0.143$: shirking falls. The surprise is the mechanism — the change was to the employer's payoffs, yet it moved the worker's behaviour and left $p^{*} = \tfrac13$ untouched (the worker's payoffs never changed). In mixed equilibrium each player's payoffs determine the other player's probabilities.

Cournot as a Nash equilibrium. Two firms face $p = 150 - 3Q$ with $Q = q_1+q_2$, and both have $MC = 30$. Quantities are chosen simultaneously.

a Write firm 1's profit as a function of $q_1$ with $q_2$ held fixed, and expand it.

$\pi_1 = \big[150 - 3(q_1+q_2) - 30\big]q_1 = (120 - 3q_2)q_1 - 3q_1^{2}$.

b Differentiate with respect to $q_1$, set to zero, and solve for the best-response function $BR_1(q_2)$.

$\dfrac{\partial \pi_1}{\partial q_1} = 120 - 3q_2 - 6q_1 = 0 \Rightarrow q_1 = 20 - \dfrac{q_2}{2}$. Endpoint check: $q_2 = 0$ gives $q_1 = 20$, which is the monopoly output $\tfrac{a-c}{2b} = \tfrac{120}{6}$. ✓

c Impose the Nash condition (both best responses hold at once) and find $q^{*}$, $Q$, $p$, and $\pi_i$.

Symmetry: $q = 20 - \tfrac q2 \Rightarrow \tfrac32 q = 20 \Rightarrow q^{*} = \tfrac{40}{3} \approx 13.33$ each. $Q = \tfrac{80}{3}$, $p = 150 - 80 = 70$, $\pi_i = (70-30)\tfrac{40}{3} = \tfrac{1600}{3} \approx \boxed{533.3}$ each.

d If instead the firms split the monopoly output, each produces $10$. Show that $(10,10)$ is not a Nash equilibrium by computing firm 1's best response to $q_2 = 10$.

$BR_1(10) = 20 - 5 = 15 \ne 10$, so firm 1 strictly wants to move — the profile fails the no-regret condition. Confirming with profits: at $(10,10)$, $p = 90$ and $\pi_1 = 600$; deviating to $15$ gives $Q = 25$, $p = 75$, $\pi_1 = 45 \times 15 = 675 > 600$.

e Now repeat the game forever with grim trigger. Using $\pi^{C} = 600$, $\pi^{D} = 675$, $\pi^{N} = \tfrac{1600}{3}$, find $\delta^{*}$.

$\delta^{*} = \dfrac{675 - 600}{675 - \tfrac{1600}{3}} = \dfrac{75}{\tfrac{425}{3}} = \dfrac{225}{425} = \boxed{\dfrac{9}{17}} \approx 0.529$. The same threshold as the $p = 120-Q$ example on the Repeated tab — not a coincidence: for any symmetric linear-demand, constant-$MC$ Cournot duopoly, this collusion-versus-Cournot comparison always gives $\delta^{*} = \tfrac{9}{17}$. The $a$, $b$, and $c$ all cancel.

Which tool do I reach for?

1

Do the players move at the same time (or without seeing each other)?

Yes → payoff matrix, go to step 2. No, one moves first and it is observed → game tree and backward induction, go to step 5.

2

Does anyone have a strictly dominant strategy?

Yes → they play it; the game may collapse immediately. Check for strictly dominated strategies too and eliminate them iteratively (IESDS).

3

Underline best responses.

Down each column for the row player, across each row for the column player. Cells with both marks are the pure Nash equilibria. Report them as strategy pairs.

4

How many did you find?

Zero → solve for the mixed equilibrium using indifference. One → that is the prediction; check Pareto efficiency separately. Two or more → coordination problem; there is also a mixed equilibrium, and theory alone cannot select among them.

5

Sequential: solve the last node first, fold, repeat.

Report the SPNE as a full contingent plan ("Enter; Accommodate if entry occurs"). Any Nash equilibrium the tree rejects is resting on a non-credible threat.

6

Is it repeated?

Known finite end → unravel by backward induction; the stage-Nash outcome repeats every period. No known end → compare $PV$ of cooperating against $PV$ of cheating once and being punished, and solve for $\delta^{*}$.

7

Continuous strategies (quantities, prices)?

Same definitions, calculus instead of scanning. Best response = maximize $\pi_i$ over $q_i$ with $q_{-i}$ fixed; Nash equilibrium = solve the best-response functions simultaneously.

Definitions in one line each

Best response

$$u_i(s_i^{*}, s_{-i}) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i})\ \ \forall s_i$$

Best against this specific choice by the others. Always exists.

Nash equilibrium

$$u_i(s_i^{*}, s_{-i}^{*}) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^{*})\ \ \forall s_i,\ \forall i$$

Everyone best-responding at once. No unilateral deviation pays.

Strictly dominant

$$u_i(s_i^{d}, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i', s_{-i})\ \ \forall s_i',\ \forall s_{-i}$$

Best in every column. Rare; if present, play it and stop.

Mixed-strategy indifference

$$EU_2(\text{L} \mid p) = EU_2(\text{R} \mid p) \;\Rightarrow\; p^{*}$$

Your probabilities are set by the opponent's payoffs. Solve $q^{*}$ from your own.

Geometric series (for $PV$)

$$1+\delta+\delta^{2}+\cdots = \frac{1}{1-\delta}$$

A payoff $\pi$ every period forever is worth $\pi/(1-\delta)$ today.

Grim-trigger threshold

$$\delta^{*} = \frac{\pi^{D}-\pi^{C}}{\pi^{D}-\pi^{N}}$$

Temptation over total damage. Cooperation holds iff $\delta \ge \delta^{*}$.

Cournot best response

$$q_i = \frac{a-c-b\,q_{-i}}{2b}$$

From $\partial\pi_i/\partial q_i = 0$ on residual demand. The reaction function.

Cournot equilibrium ($n$ firms)

$$q_i = \frac{a-c}{(n+1)b}, \quad p = \frac{a+nc}{n+1}$$

$n=1$ monopoly, $n=2$ duopoly, $n \to \infty$ gives $p \to c$.

3

The classic games at a glance

Prisoner's Dilemma — one equilibrium, and it is bad

Both players have a strictly dominant strategy; the unique NE is Pareto dominated. Real examples: arms races, advertising wars, overfishing, and cartels. Fix: repetition with a patient enough $\delta$.

Coordination (Stag Hunt, standards) — two equilibria

Both players want to match; cooperation is an equilibrium, it just is not unique. Nash equilibrium cannot pick between them — that is where communication, custom, and reputation matter. A mixed equilibrium also exists.

Pure conflict (Matching Pennies, penalty kicks) — no pure equilibrium

One player matches, the other differs, so the best responses chase in a cycle. The equilibrium is in mixed strategies, found by making the opponent indifferent. Predictability is the only real mistake.

Entry deterrence — sequential, with a bluff in it

The normal form has an extra Nash equilibrium built on a threat the incumbent would never carry out. Backward induction kills it. Making the threat real requires commitment — changing your own payoffs so that fighting becomes a best response.

4

Traps that cost marks

Reporting a payoff instead of an equilibrium. "The Nash equilibrium is $(900, 900)$" is wrong. The equilibrium is $(\text{Compete}, \text{Compete})$; $(900,900)$ is what it pays.

Comparing diagonally. Best responses are found within a column (row player) or within a row (column player). Comparing a cell to a diagonal neighbour changes two things at once.

Stopping at the first equilibrium. Finding one Nash equilibrium does not mean it is unique. Check every cell, and if there are none, look for a mixed equilibrium.

Solving for your own mix with your own payoffs. $p^{*}$ comes from the opponent's indifference condition. Always.

Forgetting the leading $\delta$ on punishment. $PV_{D} = \pi^{D} + \dfrac{\delta\,\pi^{N}}{1-\delta}$, not $\pi^{D} + \dfrac{\pi^{N}}{1-\delta}$ — punishment starts next period.

Writing a sequential strategy as a single action. A strategy is a complete contingent plan. The second mover's strategy needs an action at every node they could face, including nodes that equilibrium play never reaches.

Confusing "equilibrium" with "good." Nash equilibrium is a prediction about stability, not a welfare judgment. Pareto efficiency is a separate test, and the Prisoner's Dilemma exists to show the two can disagree completely.
