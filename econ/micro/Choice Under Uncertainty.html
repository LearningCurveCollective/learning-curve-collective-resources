The one-page version

The thread. Expected value alone cannot explain how people choose between gambles. If a person's preferences over lotteries satisfy four axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence), then they choose as if they maximize expected utility: the probability-weighted average of a utility function $u$ applied to each outcome. The shape of $u$ is the person's attitude toward risk. Two famous experiments, Allais and Ellsberg, show that real people sometimes break the axioms.

Each row below has an Econ line (how you would write it on an exam) and a Plain English line (what it means). Use the toggle to hide the Plain English lines once you no longer need them.
Lottery
Econ$L=(p_1,x_1;\,p_2,x_2;\,\dots;\,p_n,x_n)$ with $p_i\ge 0$ and $\sum_i p_i=1$.
Plain EnglishA list of what could happen and how likely each thing is. The probabilities have to add to 1.
Degenerate lottery
Econ$M=(1,x)$: a single outcome with probability 1.
Plain EnglishA sure thing, written in lottery notation so it can be compared with real gambles.
Preference symbols
Econ$L\succsim M$ (at least as good), $L\succ M$ (strictly better), $L\sim M$ (indifferent). These are preferences over lotteries, not just over outcomes.
Plain EnglishSame symbols as consumer theory, but now the things being ranked are gambles.
Compound lottery and its reduction
EconA lottery whose prizes are themselves lotteries. Reduce it by multiplying probabilities down each branch and adding up by final outcome. We assume people care only about the reduced lottery.
Plain EnglishA coin flip that decides which gamble you play is still just one gamble. Work out the final odds of each prize and ignore the packaging.
Expected value vs. expected utility
Econ$EV(L)=\sum_i p_i x_i$ averages the dollars. $EU(L)=\sum_i p_i\,u(x_i)$ averages the utility of the dollars. In general $EU(L)\neq EV(L)$.
Plain EnglishEV is what the gamble pays on average. EU is how good the gamble feels on average. Two gambles with the same EV can feel very different.
Axiom 1: Completeness
EconFor any two lotteries $L,M$: either $L\succsim M$, or $M\succsim L$, or both ($L\sim M$).
Plain EnglishYou can always rank any two gambles. "I don't know" is not allowed.
Axiom 2: Transitivity
EconIf $L\succsim M$ and $M\succsim N$, then $L\succsim N$.
Plain EnglishRankings chain together with no loops. Otherwise someone could sell you the same gamble over and over.
Axiom 3: Continuity
EconIf $B\succ X\succ W$, there is some $p\in(0,1)$ with $X\sim(p,B;\,1-p,W)$.
Plain EnglishAny middle outcome is worth exactly some gamble between the best and worst. That gamble's probability becomes the outcome's utility number.
Axiom 4: Independence
EconIf $L\succsim M$, then for any lottery $N$ and any $p\in(0,1]$: $(p,L;\,1-p,N)\succsim(p,M;\,1-p,N)$.
Plain EnglishMixing both options with the same third gamble should not flip your ranking. The shared part cancels. This is the axiom people most often violate.
Expected Utility Theorem
EconIf preferences satisfy Axioms 1 to 4, there is a $u$ such that $L\succsim M \iff U(L)\ge U(M)$ where $U(L)=\sum_i p_i\,u(x_i)$. This $u$ is unique up to a positive affine transformation $v=a+b\,u$, $b>0$.
Plain EnglishFour reasonable rules force you to rank gambles by expected utility. You may shift or stretch the utility scale, but you may not bend it: squaring $u$ or flipping its sign can change the ranking.
Allais paradox
EconMost people choose $A\succ B$ and $D\succ C$. Writing both in expected utility gives $0.11\,u(1\text{M})>0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0)$ and its exact reverse. Independence is violated.
Plain EnglishThe two pairs differ only by a shared 0.89 chance of the same prize. Cross out that shared row and the two choices are the same choice. People still answer differently because certainty feels special.
Ellsberg paradox
EconUrn: 30 red, 60 black or yellow in unknown proportion. Betting on red over black implies $P(\text{black})<\tfrac13$; betting on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow implies $P(\text{black})>\tfrac13$. No single belief fits both.
Plain EnglishPeople avoid bets whose odds they do not know, even when the known and unknown bets are equally good on paper. That is ambiguity aversion, which is different from risk aversion.
Risk aversion
EconRisk averse: prefers $EV(L)$ for sure over $L$, so $u(EV(L))>EU(L)$. Equivalent to $u$ concave ($u''<0$). Linear $u$ is risk neutral; convex $u$ is risk loving.
Plain EnglishWith a curve that flattens out, each extra dollar adds less, so the downside hurts more than the upside helps. On the graph, the curve sits above the chord connecting the two outcomes.
Certainty equivalent and risk premium
Econ$CE$ solves $u(CE)=EU(L)$. Risk premium $RP=EV(L)-CE$. Risk averse $\iff CE<EV \iff RP>0$.
Plain EnglishCE is the sure amount that feels exactly as good as the gamble. The risk premium is how much average payoff you would give up to make the risk go away.
Insurance (preview)
EconWealth $W_0$, loss $L$ with probability $p$. Actuarially fair premium $=pL$. A risk-averse person buys full coverage at the fair price. Maximum willingness to pay $=W_0-CE$ of the uninsured gamble.
Plain EnglishA fair premium just equals the average loss. A risk-averse person will happily pay that, and will pay up to the risk premium on top of it. That gap is how insurance companies make money.

The square-root example

The opening puzzle

$\$50$ for sure vs. $(0.5,\$100;\,0.5,\$0)$. Both have $EV=50$.

With $u=\sqrt{W}$: $u(50)\approx 7.07$ vs. $EU=0.5(10)+0.5(0)=5$.

Same average dollars, different expected utility. That gap is the whole reason EU theory exists.

Allais lotteries

$A=(1,\$1\text{M})$, $B=(0.89,\$1\text{M};\,0.10,\$5\text{M};\,0.01,\$0)$

$C=(0.11,\$1\text{M};\,0.89,\$0)$, $D=(0.10,\$5\text{M};\,0.90,\$0)$

Typical answers: $A\succ B$ and $D\succ C$. Shared row: 0.89 of $\$1$M in the first pair, 0.89 of $\$0$ in the second.

Ellsberg urn

90 balls: 30 red, 60 black or yellow (unknown split).

$P(\text{red})=\tfrac13$, $P(\text{black or yellow})=\tfrac23$. $P(\text{black})$ and $P(\text{yellow})$ are unknown.

Typical answers: red over black, and black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow.

Insurance example

$u=\sqrt{W}$, $W_0=400$, loss $L=300$ with $p=0.2$.

Uninsured: $EU=0.8(20)+0.2(10)=18$, $CE=324$.

Fair premium $=0.2(300)=60$. Insured wealth $340$, $u=\sqrt{340}\approx 18.44>18$. Max willingness to pay $=400-324=76$.

Rescaling check

If $v=a+b\,u$ with $b>0$, then $EV_v(L)=a+b\,EU_u(L)$.

Adding a constant and multiplying by a positive number happen to every lottery equally, so the ranking cannot change. Any nonlinear change (squaring, logs, sign flips) can.

What a lottery is

Notation

Concept

Start with a set of possible outcomes $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ (dollar amounts, usually). A lottery assigns a probability to each outcome:

$$L=(p_1,x_1;\;p_2,x_2;\;\dots;\;p_n,x_n), \qquad p_i\ge 0,\quad \sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i=1$$

Read each pair as "probability, then outcome." The coin flip above is $L=(0.5,\$100;\,0.5,\$0)$. The sure $\$50$ is also a lottery, a degenerate one: $M=(1,\$50)$. Writing sure things as lotteries lets us compare them with gambles using one set of rules.

We assume a person has preferences over lotteries and we use the usual symbols:

  • $L\succsim M$: $L$ is at least as good as $M$
  • $L\succ M$: $L$ is strictly preferred
  • $L\sim M$: the person is indifferent between $L$ and $M$

The expected value of a lottery is the probability-weighted average of its outcomes:

$$EV(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i\,x_i$$

Compound lotteries

A lottery whose prizes are lotteries

Concept

Suppose a coin flip decides which of two gambles you get to play. Heads: you play $L=(0.5,\$100;\,0.5,\$0)$. Tails: you get $M=(1,\$50)$. This is a compound lottery. The prizes at the first stage are themselves lotteries.

To reduce a compound lottery, multiply probabilities down each branch of the tree, then add up the probabilities that land on the same final outcome:

$$P(\$100)=0.5\times 0.5=0.25,\qquad P(\$0)=0.5\times 0.5=0.25,\qquad P(\$50)=0.5\times 1=0.5$$ $$S=(0.25,\$100;\;0.5,\$50;\;0.25,\$0)$$

$S$ is the reduced (simple) lottery associated with the compound one. We will assume throughout that a person cares only about the probabilities of final outcomes. In other words, a person is indifferent between a compound lottery and its reduced form. The packaging does not matter, only the final odds.

Why this assumption matters. The independence axiom in Tab 2 is stated using mixtures like $(p,L;\,1-p,N)$, which are compound lotteries. Reduction is what lets us compute with them.

Worked example and practice

Worked example

A game show host flips a fair coin. Heads: you choose a box that holds $\$300$ with probability $0.2$ and $\$0$ otherwise. Tails: you get $\$60$ with probability $0.5$ and $\$0$ otherwise. Write the reduced lottery and its expected value.

Branch probabilities. $P(\$300)=0.5\times 0.2=0.10$. $P(\$60)=0.5\times 0.5=0.25$.

The $\$0$ outcome appears on both sides. $P(\$0)=0.5\times 0.8+0.5\times 0.5=0.40+0.25=0.65$.

Check. $0.10+0.25+0.65=1$.

$$S=(0.10,\$300;\;0.25,\$60;\;0.65,\$0),\qquad EV(S)=0.10(300)+0.25(60)+0.65(0)=45$$

Practice

With probability $0.4$ you play $L_1=(0.5,\$10;\,0.5,\$0)$ and with probability $0.6$ you play $L_2=(0.25,\$10;\,0.75,\$0)$. What is the probability of ending up with $\$10$?

$P(\$10)=0.4(0.5)+0.6(0.25)=0.20+0.15=0.35$. So the reduced lottery is $(0.35,\$10;\,0.65,\$0)$ with $EV=3.50$.

True or false: a person who cares only about final outcome probabilities must be indifferent between $(0.5,L;\,0.5,L)$ and $L$ itself, for any lottery $L$.

True. Reducing $(0.5,L;\,0.5,L)$ gives each outcome of $L$ probability $0.5p_i+0.5p_i=p_i$, which is exactly $L$. Both branches lead to the same lottery, so the coin flip changes nothing.

1

The question

We know that people rank lotteries. The question is whether that ranking can be represented by a utility function $U$ over lotteries, so that $L\succsim M$ exactly when $U(L)\ge U(M)$, and if so, what form $U$ can take. The answer (the von Neumann-Morgenstern Expected Utility Theorem) is that if preferences satisfy four axioms, then $U$ has to look like this:

$$U(L)=p_1\,u(x_1)+p_2\,u(x_2)+\dots+p_n\,u(x_n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i\,u(x_i)$$

Here $u$ is a utility function over outcomes and $U$ is its expected value under the lottery. $U(L)$ is written $EU(L)$ when we want to emphasize that it is expected utility, and it is not the same thing as $EV(L)$, which averages the dollars rather than the utility of the dollars.

2

The four axioms

Completeness

For any two lotteries $L$ and $M$, the person can compare them: $L\succsim M$, or $M\succsim L$, or both. No gamble is "uncomparable."

Transitivity

For any three lotteries, if $L\succsim M$ and $M\succsim N$, then $L\succsim N$. Rankings chain together without cycles. Someone with a cycle $L\succ M\succ N\succ L$ would pay to trade $N$ for $M$, $M$ for $L$, and $L$ for $N$, ending where they started with less money. That is the "money pump."

Continuity

Take three outcomes with $B\succ X\succ W$ (best, intermediate, worst). Then there is some probability $p$ such that $X\sim(p,B;\,1-p,W)$. Any middle outcome is worth exactly some gamble between the best and the worst. This is what lets us attach a number (the $p$) to the intermediate outcome.

Independence

Suppose $L\succsim M$. Take any third lottery $N$ and any probability $p\in(0,1]$. Then $(p,L;\,1-p,N)\succsim(p,M;\,1-p,N)$. Mixing both options with the same third lottery, in the same proportions, does not change the ranking. The common part is irrelevant. This is the axiom that gets violated most often in real life (Tab 3).

Continuity builds the utility scale

Turning an outcome into a number

Concept

Fix the best outcome $B$ and the worst outcome $W$ and set $u(B)=1$, $u(W)=0$. For any intermediate outcome $X$, continuity promises a probability $p$ with $X\sim(p,B;\,1-p,W)$. Define $u(X)=p$. That is the entire trick: an outcome's utility is the probability of the best prize in the gamble that the person finds exactly as good as the outcome.

Plain English. If you would trade a sure $\$36$ for a ticket with a 60% chance at $\$100$ (and 40% at $\$0$), but no less, then $\$36$ is worth 0.6 on your scale. Better outcomes need a higher $p$ to match, so $u$ increases with the outcome.

Why expected utility falls out: an informal proof

0

The plan. We want to show that if the axioms hold, then the utility of a lottery equals the probability-weighted average of the utilities of its outcomes. The strategy has three moves. First, use continuity to assign a number to each outcome. Second, use independence to swap each outcome in a lottery for the best/worst gamble it is indifferent to. Third, reduce the resulting compound lottery and read off the probability of the best prize. That probability will turn out to be exactly $\sum p_i u(x_i)$.

1

Anchor the scale. Take four outcomes ranked $B\succ X\succ Y\succ W$. Set $u(B)=1$ and $u(W)=0$. These two values are our choice; nothing in the axioms pins them down, which is why $u$ will only be unique up to rescaling (Section 5).

2

Assign utilities by continuity. By the continuity axiom there is a $p$ with $X\sim(p,B;\,1-p,W)$. Suppose it is $p=0.8$, so $u(X)=0.8$. Likewise there is a $q$ with $Y\sim(q,B;\,1-q,W)$. Suppose $q=0.4$, so $u(Y)=0.4$.

$$X\sim(0.8,B;\,0.2,W),\qquad Y\sim(0.4,B;\,0.6,W)$$

3

Pick a lottery to evaluate. Let $L=(0.5,X;\,0.5,Y)$. We want $U(L)$. The axioms say nothing directly about $L$, but they do let us rewrite it.

4

Substitute using independence. Since $X\sim(0.8,B;\,0.2,W)$, the independence axiom lets us replace $X$ inside $L$ with that gamble without changing how the person feels about $L$ (the other branch, $Y$, plays the role of the common lottery $N$). Do the same for $Y$. Now $L$ is indifferent to a compound lottery:

$$L\sim\big(0.5,\,(0.8,B;\,0.2,W);\;\;0.5,\,(0.4,B;\,0.6,W)\big)$$

5

Reduce the compound lottery. Multiply down each branch and add up by final outcome. For $B$: through the $X$ branch, $0.5\times 0.8=0.4$; through the $Y$ branch, $0.5\times 0.4=0.2$. Total $P(B)=0.6$. For $W$: $0.5\times 0.2+0.5\times 0.6=0.1+0.3=0.4$.

$$L\sim(0.6,B;\,0.4,W)$$

6

Read off the utility. A gamble between $B$ and $W$ with $P(B)=0.6$ has utility $0.6$ on our scale (that is how we defined the scale in Step 2). So $U(L)=0.6$. Now compare with the probability-weighted average of the outcome utilities:

$$0.5\,u(X)+0.5\,u(Y)=0.5(0.8)+0.5(0.4)=0.4+0.2=0.6$$

They match, and they match for a structural reason: the reduction in Step 5 computes exactly $0.5\cdot 0.8+0.5\cdot 0.4$, which is $\sum p_i u(x_i)$ by construction.

$$\boxed{\,U(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_i\,u(x_i)\,}$$

What each axiom did. Completeness and transitivity make the ranking well defined. Continuity gives every outcome a number. Independence allows the substitution in Step 4. Remove independence and the proof stops at Step 3, which is exactly why the Allais paradox (Tab 3) is such a problem for the theory.

5

How much freedom is there in u?

Unique up to a positive affine transformation

Concept

In ordinary consumer theory, any increasing transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences. Expected utility is stricter. If $u$ represents the preferences, so does $v=a+b\,u$ for any constants $a$ and $b>0$, and only transformations of that form are guaranteed to work. The reason is one line of algebra:

$$EV_v(L)=\sum_i p_i\,(a+b\,u(x_i))=a\sum_i p_i+b\sum_i p_i\,u(x_i)=a+b\,EU_u(L)$$

Every lottery's expected utility gets shifted by the same $a$ and stretched by the same positive $b$, so the ranking is untouched. A nonlinear transformation (squaring, taking logs, flipping the sign) does not pull out of the sum like that, and it can reverse a ranking. Test it below.

The Allais paradox

Most people choose A and D. Expected utility says that cannot happen.

Concept

Write the four lotteries in standard notation:

$$A=(1,\$1\text{M}) \qquad B=(0.89,\$1\text{M};\;0.10,\$5\text{M};\;0.01,\$0)$$ $$C=(0.11,\$1\text{M};\;0.89,\$0) \qquad D=(0.10,\$5\text{M};\;0.90,\$0)$$

Lay them side by side with a shared set of probability "slots." The table makes the structure visible: in both pairs, the two options agree on what happens in the 0.89 slot. In Choice 1 that shared prize is $\$1$M; in Choice 2 it is $\$0$. Everything else is identical across the two pairs.

The plan. Assume the person maximizes expected utility with some $u$. Write $A\succ B$ and $D\succ C$ as inequalities in $u(\$0)$, $u(\$1\text{M})$, $u(\$5\text{M})$, cancel what is common, and show the two inequalities contradict each other.

1

Write Choice 1 in expected utility. $A\succ B$ means $U(A)>U(B)$:

$$u(1\text{M})>0.89\,u(1\text{M})+0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0)$$

2

Move the $0.89\,u(1\text{M})$ to the left. Subtracting it from both sides leaves $1-0.89=0.11$ on the left:

$$0.11\,u(1\text{M})>0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0) \qquad (\star)$$

3

Write Choice 2 in expected utility. $D\succ C$ means $U(D)>U(C)$:

$$0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.90\,u(0)>0.11\,u(1\text{M})+0.89\,u(0)$$

4

Cancel the shared $0.89\,u(0)$. Subtract $0.89\,u(0)$ from both sides; $0.90-0.89=0.01$ remains on the left:

$$0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0)>0.11\,u(1\text{M}) \qquad (\star\star)$$

5

Compare. $(\star)$ and $(\star\star)$ are the same two quantities with the inequality pointing opposite ways. No utility function $u$ can satisfy both. So the pattern $A\succ B$ together with $D\succ C$ is inconsistent with expected utility maximization.

Which axiom fails? Independence. $A$ and $C$ are both "$(0.11$ slot$)$ mixed with a shared 0.89 slot," and so are $B$ and $D$. Independence says the shared slot cannot flip the ranking of the remaining 0.11 slot. It does.

Why people do it anyway. In Choice 1, option A is certain. Giving up certainty to chase $\$5$M while accepting a 1% chance of nothing feels like a bad trade. In Choice 2, neither option is certain, so the 1% difference in the chance of winning feels small and the bigger prize wins. Psychologists call this the certainty effect. The axiom says a 1% slot should carry the same weight in both pairs; people do not treat it that way.

The Ellsberg paradox

Ambiguity aversion

Concept

An urn holds 90 balls. Exactly 30 are red. The other 60 are black or yellow, in a proportion you are not told. One ball is drawn at random.

  • Gamble A: you win if the ball is red.
  • Gamble B: you win if the ball is black.
  • Gamble C: you win if the ball is red or yellow.
  • Gamble D: you win if the ball is black or yellow.

Most people choose $A\succ B$ and $D\succ C$. In each pair they pick the gamble whose odds they know exactly ($P(\text{red})=\tfrac13$, $P(\text{black or yellow})=\tfrac23$) over the gamble whose odds depend on the unknown split. That is ambiguity aversion: a dislike of not knowing the probabilities, which is separate from a dislike of risk itself.

Why it is a paradox. Let $b$ be the number of black balls. Choosing $A\succ B$ means you act as if $\tfrac{30}{90}>\tfrac{b}{90}$, so $b<30$. Choosing $D\succ C$ means you act as if $\tfrac{60}{90}>\tfrac{90-b}{90}$, so $b>30$. No single belief about $b$ makes both choices right. The person is not using any one probability for "black," which is what expected utility requires.

A friend says: "I'd take A over B, and I'd also take C over D." Is this friend violating expected utility? Show the algebra.

No. $A\succ B$ gives $0.11\,u(1\text{M})>0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0)$. $C\succ D$ gives $0.11\,u(1\text{M})+0.89\,u(0)>0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.90\,u(0)$, and cancelling $0.89\,u(0)$ gives $0.11\,u(1\text{M})>0.10\,u(5\text{M})+0.01\,u(0)$. Same inequality both times. This friend is consistent with expected utility (and fairly risk averse).

In the Ellsberg urn, suppose someone believes there are exactly 20 black balls. Which gamble should they pick in each pair?

With $b=20$: $P(A)=\tfrac{30}{90}=0.333$, $P(B)=\tfrac{20}{90}=0.222$, so $A$. $P(C)=\tfrac{30+40}{90}=0.778$, $P(D)=\tfrac{60}{90}=0.667$, so $C$. A believer in $b=20$ picks $A$ and $C$. The paradoxical pattern $A$ and $D$ is not the choice of anyone with a single belief about $b$.

Attitudes toward risk

Risk averse, risk neutral, risk loving

Concept

Take a lottery $L$ and compare two things: the lottery itself, and receiving its expected value $EV(L)$ for sure. A person is:

Risk averse

Prefers the sure $EV(L)$ to $L$: $u(EV(L))>EU(L)$. The utility function is concave ($u''<0$). Each extra dollar adds less utility than the one before, so the possible loss hurts more than the possible gain helps.

Risk neutral

Indifferent: $u(EV(L))=EU(L)$. The utility function is linear. This person ranks lotteries by expected value alone.

Risk loving

Prefers $L$ to the sure $EV(L)$: $u(EV(L))<EU(L)$. The utility function is convex ($u''>0$).

The square-root example. $u(W)=\sqrt{W}$ and $L=(0.5,\$100;\,0.5,\$400)$.

$$EV(L)=0.5(100)+0.5(400)=250$$ $$EU(L)=0.5\sqrt{100}+0.5\sqrt{400}=0.5(10)+0.5(20)=15$$ $$u(EV(L))=\sqrt{250}\approx 15.81>15=EU(L)$$

The sure $\$250$ gives more utility than the gamble, so this person is risk averse. Any concave $u$ gives this ordering; that is Jensen's inequality.

2

Certainty equivalent and risk premium

Two numbers that measure how much risk bothers you

Concept

The certainty equivalent $CE$ is the sure amount of money that gives the same utility as the lottery. The risk premium $RP$ is how much expected value the person is willing to give up to replace the lottery with a sure thing.

$$u(CE)=EU(L) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad CE=u^{-1}\big(EU(L)\big)$$ $$RP=EV(L)-CE$$

For a risk-averse person, $CE<EV$ and $RP>0$. For a risk-neutral person, $CE=EV$ and $RP=0$. For a risk lover, $CE>EV$ and $RP<0$.

Worked example

Find $CE$ and $RP$ for the square-root example above.

Step 1: expected utility. $EU(L)=15$ (from above).

Step 2: invert $u$. $u(CE)=\sqrt{CE}=15$, so $CE=15^2=225$.

Step 3: risk premium. $RP=EV-CE=250-225=25$.

This person would accept $\$225$ for sure in place of a gamble that pays $\$250$ on average. The $\$25$ gap is the price they put on getting rid of the risk.

Common mistake. $CE$ is not $u(EV)$ and it is not $EU$. It is a dollar amount, found by running $EU$ backwards through $u$. Always check units: $EU$ is in utils, $CE$ is in dollars.

Insurance preview of what comes next

Why a risk-averse person buys insurance

Concept

Start with wealth $W_0$. With probability $p$ a loss of $L$ dollars happens. Uninsured, the person faces the lottery $(1-p,\,W_0;\;p,\,W_0-L)$. An insurance contract charges a premium $\pi$ up front and pays back $L$ if the loss happens, so with full coverage the person has $W_0-\pi$ for sure.

Actuarially fair premium. The premium that just covers the expected payout: $\pi_{\text{fair}}=p\,L$.

Result. A risk-averse person offered full coverage at the fair premium takes it, because $W_0-pL=EV$ of the uninsured lottery, and a risk-averse person prefers the sure $EV$ to the lottery.

Maximum willingness to pay. The highest premium the person will accept makes them indifferent: $u(W_0-\pi_{\max})=EU_{\text{uninsured}}$, so $W_0-\pi_{\max}=CE$ and $\pi_{\max}=W_0-CE=\pi_{\text{fair}}+RP$.

Worked example

$u(W)=\sqrt{W}$, $W_0=400$, a loss of $300$ happens with probability $0.2$. Find the fair premium, check that the person buys at that price, and find the most they would pay.

Uninsured lottery. $(0.8,\,400;\;0.2,\,100)$. $EV=0.8(400)+0.2(100)=340$. $EU=0.8(20)+0.2(10)=18$.

Fair premium. $\pi_{\text{fair}}=0.2\times 300=60$. Insured wealth $=400-60=340$, and $u(340)=\sqrt{340}\approx 18.44>18$. Buy.

Maximum premium. $CE$ solves $\sqrt{CE}=18$, so $CE=324$. $\pi_{\max}=400-324=76$. That is the fair premium $60$ plus the risk premium $340-324=16$.

Practice

Practice

$u(W)=\sqrt{W}$, $L=(0.25,\$16;\,0.75,\$64)$. Find $EV$, $EU$, $CE$ and $RP$, and state the risk attitude.

$EV=0.25(16)+0.75(64)=4+48=52$. $EU=0.25(4)+0.75(8)=1+6=7$. $CE=7^2=49$. $RP=52-49=3$. $CE<EV$, so risk averse (as it must be for a concave $u$).

Repeat with $u(W)=W^2$ and $L=(0.5,\$10;\,0.5,\$30)$. What sign does the risk premium have?

$EV=20$. $EU=0.5(100)+0.5(900)=500$. $CE=\sqrt{500}\approx 22.36$. $RP=20-22.36\approx -2.36<0$. Convex $u$, so risk loving: this person would pay to take on the gamble rather than pay to avoid it.

$u(W)=\sqrt{W}$, $W_0=900$, loss of $500$ with probability $0.1$. Find the fair premium and the maximum premium the person would pay for full coverage.

Fair: $0.1\times 500=50$. Uninsured $EU=0.9(30)+0.1(20)=29$, so $CE=29^2=841$. $\pi_{\max}=900-841=59$. Any premium between $50$ and $59$ is profitable for the insurer and still acceptable to the customer.
