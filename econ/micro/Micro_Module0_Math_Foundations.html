Derivative Rules

The derivative $f'(x)$ (also written $\dfrac{df}{dx}$) tells you how $f$ changes as $x$ changes. Graphically, $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of $f$ at $x = x_0$. In micro, derivatives show up as marginal quantities: marginal cost is $C'(q)$, marginal revenue is $R'(q)$, marginal utility is $U'(x)$. They also build elasticities: $\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\dfrac{P}{Q}$.

Constant Rule

$$f(x) = C \;\Longrightarrow\; f'(x) = 0$$

Fixed costs vanish when you take marginal cost.

Power Rule

$$f(x) = ax^n \;\Longrightarrow\; f'(x) = nax^{\,n-1}$$

Bring the exponent down front, subtract one from the exponent. Works for negative and fractional $n$.

Addition Rule

$$f = g + h \;\Longrightarrow\; f' = g' + h'$$

Differentiate term by term.

Product Rule

$$f = g\,h \;\Longrightarrow\; f' = g'h + g\,h'$$

"Derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times derivative of the second."

Quotient Rule

$$f = \frac{g}{h} \;\Longrightarrow\; f' = \frac{g'h - g\,h'}{h^2}$$

"Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."

Chain Rule

$$f = g(h(x)) \;\Longrightarrow\; f' = g'(h(x))\cdot h'(x)$$

Differentiate the outside, keep the inside, multiply by the derivative of the inside.

Exponential

$$f(x) = e^{x} \;\Longrightarrow\; f'(x) = e^{x}$$

With chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{h(x)} = e^{h(x)}\,h'(x)$.

Natural Log

$$f(x) = \ln x \;\Longrightarrow\; f'(x) = \frac{1}{x}$$

With chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln h(x) = \dfrac{h'(x)}{h(x)}$.

Optimization Conditions

First-Order Condition (FOC)

$$f'(x^*) = 0$$

At a max or min the tangent line is flat. Solve this to find the candidate $x^*$.

Second-Order Condition (SOC)

$$f''(x^*) < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{max} \qquad f''(x^*) > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{min}$$

Slope decreasing through $x^*$ means a peak; slope increasing means a valley.

Econ translation: Profit max sets $\pi'(q) = MR - MC = 0$, i.e. $MR = MC$. Cost min sets $C'(q) = 0$. Always check the SOC so you don't accidentally report a minimum profit.

Partial Derivatives

For $f(x,y)$, the partial $f_x = \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ is the derivative with respect to $x$ treating $y$ as a constant. Likewise $f_y$ treats $x$ as a constant.

$$f(x,y) = x^2y + 3xy^2 + 3x + y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_x = 2xy + 3y^2 + 3, \qquad f_y = x^2 + 6xy + 1$$

Econ translation: For utility $U(x,y)$, $U_x$ is marginal utility of $x$ and $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{U_x}{U_y}$. For production $F(K,L)$, $F_K = MP_K$ and $F_L = MP_L$.

Cobb-Douglas → take logs FIRST. If you need the MRS (the ratio of marginal utilities, $MU_x/MU_y$ — see the Partial Derivatives tab) of $U = x^a y^b$, don't fight the fractional exponents. Transform: $\ln U = a\ln x + b\ln y$, then differentiate: $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \dfrac{a}{x}$, $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \dfrac{b}{y}$, so $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{a/x}{b/y} = \dfrac{a}{b}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}$. See the Exponents & Logs tab.

Exponent Rules

Negative Exponent

$$x^{-a} = \frac{1}{x^a}$$

Same Exponent, Product

$$x^a y^a = (xy)^a$$

Same Exponent, Quotient

$$\frac{x^a}{y^a} = \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{a}$$

Same Base, Product

$$x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$$

Power of a Power

$$(x^a)^b = x^{ab}$$

Useful Extras

$$\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{x^a}{x^b} = x^{a-b}$$

Rewrite roots and fractions as exponents before differentiating. Returns to scale: $AK^aL^b$ scales as $\lambda^{a+b}$ — CRS if $a+b=1$, IRS if $>1$, DRS if $<1$.

5

Log Rules

Product

$$\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y$$

Quotient

$$\ln\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \ln x - \ln y$$

Power

$$\ln(x^a) = a\ln x$$

The workhorse for Cobb-Douglas: exponents become coefficients.

Inverses

$$\ln(e^{x}) = x, \qquad e^{\ln x} = x$$

Special Values

$$\ln 1 = 0, \qquad \ln e = 1$$

Derivative

$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln h(x) = \frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}$$

Systems of Equations

  1. Isolate one variable in one equation.
  2. Substitute that expression into the other equation.
  3. Solve the resulting one-variable equation.
  4. Substitute back to get the other variable.

Or eliminate: scale one equation so a variable's coefficients match, then add or subtract the equations to cancel it.

Econ translation: Consumer choice = tangency condition ($\text{MRS} = p_x/p_y$) + budget line. Market equilibrium = $Q_d = Q_s$. Both are two equations in two unknowns.

Power, Constant & Addition Rules

Polynomials and Rewriting

Concept

Differentiate term by term with the power rule. Constants drop out. Rewrite roots and fractions as powers first: $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ and $\dfrac{1}{x^2} = x^{-2}$.

Worked Example 1

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = 5x^3 - 2x^2 + 7x - 4$.

Apply the power rule to each term (addition rule lets us go one at a time):

$$\frac{d}{dx}(5x^3) = 15x^2, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-2x^2) = -4x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(7x) = 7, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(-4) = 0$$ $$\boxed{f'(x) = 15x^2 - 4x + 7}$$
Worked Example 2

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = 3\sqrt{x} - \dfrac{4}{x} + 2x^3 + 9$.

Rewrite first so every term is $ax^n$:

$$f(x) = 3x^{1/2} - 4x^{-1} + 2x^3 + 9$$

Now power rule term by term:

$$f'(x) = \tfrac{3}{2}x^{-1/2} - 4(-1)x^{-2} + 6x^2 + 0$$ $$\boxed{f'(x) = \frac{3}{2\sqrt{x}} + \frac{4}{x^2} + 6x^2}$$

Watch the double negative on the $-4x^{-1}$ term: $-4 \times (-1) = +4$.

Practice

(a) $f(x) = \dfrac{3}{x^2}$    (b) $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$    (c) $TR(q) = 100q - 2q^2$ — find $MR(q)$

(d) $C(q) = 0.5q^2 + 3q + 40$ — find $MC(q)$    (e) $f(x) = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}$

(a) Rewrite: $f(x) = 3x^{-2}$. Power rule: $f'(x) = -6x^{-3} = \boxed{-\dfrac{6}{x^3}}$

(b) Rewrite: $f(x) = x^{1/2}$. Power rule: $f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}}$

(c) $MR(q) = TR'(q) = \boxed{100 - 4q}$

(d) $MC(q) = C'(q) = \boxed{q + 3}$ (the fixed cost $40$ vanishes)

(e) Rewrite: $f(x) = 2x^{-1/2}$. $f'(x) = -x^{-3/2} = \boxed{-\dfrac{1}{x^{3/2}}}$

Product & Quotient Rules

Two Functions Multiplied or Divided

Concept

Label the pieces $g$ and $h$ before you start. Write $g'$ and $h'$ off to the side, then plug into the formula.

$$(gh)' = g'h + gh' \qquad\qquad \left(\frac{g}{h}\right)' = \frac{g'h - gh'}{h^2}$$
Worked Example 1

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = x^2 e^{x}$.

Key: $g = x^2,\; g' = 2x$  |  $h = e^x,\; h' = e^x$
$$f'(x) = g'h + gh' = 2x\,e^x + x^2 e^x$$

Factor the common $xe^x$:

$$\boxed{f'(x) = xe^x(x + 2)}$$
Worked Example 2

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = \dfrac{e^{x}}{x}$.

Key: $g = e^x,\; g' = e^x$  |  $h = x,\; h' = 1$
$$f'(x) = \frac{g'h - gh'}{h^2} = \frac{e^x\cdot x - e^x\cdot 1}{x^2}$$

Factor $e^x$ out of the numerator:

$$\boxed{f'(x) = \frac{e^x(x - 1)}{x^2}}$$
Practice

(a) $f(x) = x\ln x$    (b) $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x - 1}$    (c) $f(x) = (x^2 + 1)(x^3 - 2x)$

(d) $f(x) = \dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$    (e) $f(x) = \dfrac{3x + 1}{2x - 5}$

(a) $g = x,\, g' = 1$; $h = \ln x,\, h' = 1/x$.

$$f'(x) = (1)(\ln x) + x\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \boxed{\ln x + 1}$$

(b) $g = x^2+1,\, g' = 2x$; $h = x-1,\, h' = 1$.

$$f'(x) = \frac{2x(x-1) - (x^2+1)(1)}{(x-1)^2} = \frac{2x^2 - 2x - x^2 - 1}{(x-1)^2} = \boxed{\frac{x^2 - 2x - 1}{(x-1)^2}}$$

(c) $g = x^2+1,\, g' = 2x$; $h = x^3 - 2x,\, h' = 3x^2 - 2$.

$$f'(x) = 2x(x^3 - 2x) + (x^2+1)(3x^2 - 2)$$ $$= 2x^4 - 4x^2 + 3x^4 - 2x^2 + 3x^2 - 2 = \boxed{5x^4 - 3x^2 - 2}$$

(Check: expanding first gives $f = x^5 - x^3 - 2x$, same derivative.)

(d) $g = \ln x,\, g' = 1/x$; $h = x^2,\, h' = 2x$.

$$f'(x) = \frac{\frac{1}{x}\cdot x^2 - \ln x\cdot 2x}{x^4} = \frac{x - 2x\ln x}{x^4} = \boxed{\frac{1 - 2\ln x}{x^3}}$$

(e) $g = 3x+1,\, g' = 3$; $h = 2x-5,\, h' = 2$.

$$f'(x) = \frac{3(2x-5) - (3x+1)(2)}{(2x-5)^2} = \frac{6x - 15 - 6x - 2}{(2x-5)^2} = \boxed{\frac{-17}{(2x-5)^2}}$$

Chain Rule (with $e^x$ and $\ln x$)

Functions Inside Functions

Concept

Identify the outside function and the inside function. Differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big[h(x)\big]^n = n\big[h(x)\big]^{n-1}h'(x) \qquad \frac{d}{dx}e^{h(x)} = e^{h(x)}h'(x) \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln h(x) = \frac{h'(x)}{h(x)}$$
Worked Example 1

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = (3x^2 + 1)^4$.

Key: outside $g(u) = u^4$, inside $h(x) = 3x^2 + 1$, so $h'(x) = 6x$
$$f'(x) = 4(3x^2+1)^3 \cdot 6x = \boxed{24x(3x^2+1)^3}$$
Worked Example 2

Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = \ln(3x^2 + 2x)$.

Key: outside $g(u) = \ln u$, inside $h(x) = 3x^2 + 2x$, so $h'(x) = 6x + 2$

Use $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln h(x) = \dfrac{h'(x)}{h(x)}$:

$$\boxed{f'(x) = \frac{6x + 2}{3x^2 + 2x}}$$

Alternative: use log rules first. $\ln(3x^2 + 2x) = \ln\big(x(3x+2)\big) = \ln x + \ln(3x+2)$, so $f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{3}{3x+2}$ — the same thing over a common denominator.

Practice

(a) $f(x) = e^{-2x}$    (b) $f(x) = \ln(x^2 + 4)$    (c) $f(x) = e^{x^2}$    (d) $f(x) = \sqrt{2x + 5}$    (e) $f(x) = 5e^{3x^2}$

(a) inside $= -2x$, $h' = -2$: $\;\boxed{f'(x) = -2e^{-2x}}$

(b) inside $= x^2+4$, $h' = 2x$: $\;\boxed{f'(x) = \dfrac{2x}{x^2+4}}$

(c) inside $= x^2$, $h' = 2x$: $\;\boxed{f'(x) = 2xe^{x^2}}$

(d) $f(x) = (2x+5)^{1/2}$, $h' = 2$: $\;f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2x+5)^{-1/2}\cdot 2 = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2x+5}}}$

(e) inside $= 3x^2$, $h' = 6x$: $\;f'(x) = 5e^{3x^2}\cdot 6x = \boxed{30xe^{3x^2}}$

Marginal Quantities & Elasticity

Where Derivatives Show Up in Micro

Concept

"Marginal" means "derivative of the total." Two other things we'll use constantly:

$$\text{Price elasticity of demand: } \varepsilon = \frac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\frac{P}{Q}$$ $$\text{Diminishing marginal utility: } U'(x) > 0 \text{ and } U''(x) < 0$$

Elasticity is the derivative rescaled so that units don't matter: it's the percent change in $Q$ per percent change in $P$. $|\varepsilon| > 1$ is elastic, $|\varepsilon| < 1$ is inelastic. The second derivative's sign is how we say "each extra unit helps, but less than the last one."

Worked Example 1

Demand is $Q = 100 - 2P$. Find the price elasticity of demand at $P = 20$.

Givens: $Q = 100 - 2P$, $P = 20$  |  Key: $\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\cdot\dfrac{P}{Q}$

1. Derivative: $\dfrac{dQ}{dP} = -2$ (constant, since demand is linear).

2. Quantity at that price: $Q = 100 - 2(20) = 60$.

3. Assemble:

$$\varepsilon = (-2)\cdot\frac{20}{60} = \boxed{-\tfrac{2}{3}} \quad\text{(inelastic, since } |\varepsilon| < 1\text{)}$$

Note the slope is the same everywhere on a linear demand curve, but the elasticity is not — it depends on where you are.

Worked Example 2

Show that $U(x) = \sqrt{x}$ exhibits diminishing marginal utility.

Rewrite $U(x) = x^{1/2}$ and differentiate twice with the power rule:

$$U'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} > 0 \qquad U''(x) = -\tfrac{1}{4}x^{-3/2} = -\frac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0$$

$U' > 0$: more $x$ is always better. $U'' < 0$: the marginal utility is falling as $x$ grows. $\boxed{\text{Diminishing MU} \checkmark}$

Practice

(a) $C(q) = q^3 - 6q^2 + 15q + 30$. Find $MC(q)$ and $AC(q)$.

(b) $Q = 50 - P$. Find $\varepsilon$ at $P = 10$ and at $P = 40$. Which is elastic?

(c) $Q = \dfrac{200}{P}$. Show that $\varepsilon = -1$ at every price.

(d) Does $U(x) = \ln x$ exhibit diminishing marginal utility?

(e) $TR(q) = 50q - q^2$. Find $MR(q)$. At what $q$ is $MR = 0$, and what is true of $TR$ there?

(a) $MC = C'(q) = \boxed{3q^2 - 12q + 15}$; $\;AC = \dfrac{C(q)}{q} = \boxed{q^2 - 6q + 15 + \dfrac{30}{q}}$

(b) $\dfrac{dQ}{dP} = -1$. At $P = 10$: $Q = 40$, $\varepsilon = -\dfrac{10}{40} = -0.25$ (inelastic). At $P = 40$: $Q = 10$, $\varepsilon = -\dfrac{40}{10} = -4$ (elastic). $\boxed{P = 40 \text{ is elastic}}$

(c) $Q = 200P^{-1}$, so $\dfrac{dQ}{dP} = -200P^{-2}$. Then

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{200}{P^2}\cdot\frac{P}{200/P} = -\frac{200}{P^2}\cdot\frac{P^2}{200} = \boxed{-1}$$

(d) $U' = \dfrac{1}{x} > 0$ and $U'' = -\dfrac{1}{x^2} < 0$. $\boxed{\text{Yes}}$

(e) $MR = \boxed{50 - 2q}$; $MR = 0$ at $\boxed{q = 25}$, which is exactly the FOC for maximizing $TR$ — revenue peaks where marginal revenue hits zero.

The FOC / SOC Recipe

  1. FOC: compute $f'(x)$, set it equal to zero, solve for $x^*$.
  2. SOC: compute $f''(x)$ and evaluate at $x^*$. Negative $\Rightarrow$ max, positive $\Rightarrow$ min.
  3. Plug back in to get the optimal value $f(x^*)$ if the question asks for it.

Common mistake: stopping after the FOC. The FOC finds candidates; the SOC tells you which kind of candidate you found. Also: when there is more than one solution to the FOC, check the SOC at each one.

Figure. The FOC is the same for both pictures — a flat tangent. The SOC is what tells them apart: whether the slope is on its way down through zero (max) or on its way up through zero (min).

Where MR = MC Comes From

Derivation 1: The Profit-Max Condition

0

The plan. Profit is revenue minus cost. We'll write that as a function of quantity, apply the first-order condition, and see that "$\pi'(q) = 0$" is literally the same sentence as "$MR = MC$." Then we'll check what the second-order condition says.

1

Define profit. Total revenue $TR(q)$ and total cost $C(q)$ are both functions of output $q$:

$$\pi(q) = TR(q) - C(q)$$
2

Apply the FOC. The firm chooses $q$ to maximize $\pi$, so at the optimum $q^*$ the derivative is zero:

$$\pi'(q^*) = 0$$
3

Differentiate term by term. By the addition rule, the derivative of a difference is the difference of the derivatives:

$$\pi'(q) = TR'(q) - C'(q)$$
4

Name the pieces. By definition, marginal revenue is $MR(q) = TR'(q)$ and marginal cost is $MC(q) = C'(q)$. Substituting:

$$\pi'(q) = MR(q) - MC(q)$$
5

Combine with the FOC. Setting step 4 equal to zero (step 2):

$$MR(q^*) - MC(q^*) = 0$$
$$\boxed{MR(q^*) = MC(q^*)}$$
6

The SOC. For a maximum we need $\pi''(q^*) < 0$. Differentiating step 4 once more:

$$\pi''(q) = MR'(q) - MC'(q) < 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad MR'(q^*) < MC'(q^*)$$

In words: at the optimum, the $MC$ curve must be steeper than the $MR$ curve — $MC$ cuts $MR$ from below. If $MC$ crossed $MR$ from above you'd be at a profit minimum.

Special case — a price-taking firm. If the firm can't affect price, $TR(q) = Pq$, so $MR = TR'(q) = P$ (constant rule times $q$). The condition becomes $\boxed{P = MC}$. This is why the competitive firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve.

Derivation 2: Why MC Passes Through the Minimum of AC

0

The plan. Average cost is total cost divided by quantity. That's a quotient, so the quotient rule will tell us its slope — and the slope turns out to depend on whether $MC$ is above or below $AC$.

1

Define average cost.

$$AC(q) = \frac{C(q)}{q}$$
2

Quotient rule with $g = C(q)$, $g' = C'(q)$, $h = q$, $h' = 1$:

$$AC'(q) = \frac{C'(q)\cdot q - C(q)\cdot 1}{q^2}$$
3

Rewrite in terms of MC and AC. Split the fraction: $\dfrac{C'(q)\,q}{q^2} = \dfrac{C'(q)}{q}$ and $\dfrac{C(q)}{q^2} = \dfrac{1}{q}\cdot\dfrac{C(q)}{q} = \dfrac{AC(q)}{q}$. Since $C'(q) = MC(q)$:

$$AC'(q) = \frac{MC(q) - AC(q)}{q}$$
4

Read the sign. Since $q > 0$, the sign of $AC'$ is the sign of $MC - AC$:

  • $MC < AC \Rightarrow AC' < 0$: average cost is falling.
  • $MC > AC \Rightarrow AC' > 0$: average cost is rising.

(Same logic as your GPA: a grade below your average pulls it down, a grade above pulls it up.)

5

FOC. $AC$ is minimized where $AC'(q) = 0$, i.e. where the numerator is zero:

$$\boxed{MC(q^*) = AC(q^*) \text{ at the minimum of } AC}$$

Figure. $AC = q + 16/q$ and $MC = 2q$ from the practice problems. $MC$ crosses $AC$ exactly at $AC$'s lowest point, $q^* = 4$, where both equal $8$.

Profit Maximization

Maximizing a Profit Function

Concept

Either write $\pi(q) = TR(q) - C(q)$ and set $\pi'(q) = 0$, or equivalently set $MR = MC$. They are the same condition: $\pi'(q) = TR'(q) - C'(q) = MR - MC$.

Worked Example 1

A firm's profit is $\pi(q) = 60q - q^2 - 100$. Find the profit-maximizing output and the maximum profit.

Givens: $\pi(q) = 60q - q^2 - 100$  |  Find: $q^*$ and $\pi(q^*)$

FOC:

$$\pi'(q) = 60 - 2q = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q^* = 30$$

SOC:

$$\pi''(q) = -2 < 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{maximum} \checkmark$$

Optimal value:

$$\pi(30) = 60(30) - 30^2 - 100 = 1800 - 900 - 100 = \boxed{800}$$

So $\boxed{q^* = 30}$ and maximum profit is $800$.

Worked Example 2 — the MR = MC route

A firm has revenue $TR(q) = 120q - 2q^2$ and cost $C(q) = q^2 + 20$. Find $q^*$ using $MR = MC$, verify the SOC, and compute profit.

Givens: $TR = 120q - 2q^2$, $C = q^2 + 20$  |  Key: $MR = TR'(q)$, $MC = C'(q)$
$$MR = 120 - 4q, \qquad MC = 2q$$

FOC ($MR = MC$):

$$120 - 4q = 2q \;\Longrightarrow\; 120 = 6q \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{q^* = 20}$$

SOC: $\pi''(q) = MR'(q) - MC'(q) = -4 - 2 = -6 < 0$ $\checkmark$ maximum.

Profit:

$$\pi(20) = TR(20) - C(20) = (2400 - 800) - (400 + 20) = 1600 - 420 = \boxed{1180}$$
Practice

(a) A firm sells at fixed price $P = 50$ with cost $C(q) = q^2 + 10q + 20$. Write $\pi(q)$, then find $q^*$ and max profit.

(b) A monopolist faces inverse demand $P = 120 - 3Q$ and has zero cost. Find the revenue-maximizing $Q$, the price charged, and total revenue.

(c) $\pi(q) = 80q - 2q^2 - 50$. Find $q^*$ and max profit.

(d) Price is fixed at $P = 30$ and $C(q) = 0.5q^2 + 6q + 10$. Find $q^*$ and max profit.

(e) A monopolist faces $P = 100 - Q$ with cost $C(Q) = Q^2$. Use $MR = MC$ to find $Q^*$, then find $P^*$ and profit.

(a) $\pi(q) = 50q - q^2 - 10q - 20 = 40q - q^2 - 20$

$$\text{FOC: } 40 - 2q = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = 20 \qquad \text{SOC: } \pi'' = -2 < 0 \;\checkmark$$ $$\pi(20) = 800 - 400 - 20 = \boxed{380}$$

(b) $TR(Q) = (120 - 3Q)Q = 120Q - 3Q^2$

$$\text{FOC: } 120 - 6Q = 0 \Rightarrow Q^* = 20 \qquad \text{SOC: } -6 < 0 \;\checkmark \qquad P = 60,\; TR = \boxed{1200}$$

(c) FOC: $80 - 4q = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = 20$. SOC: $-4 < 0$ $\checkmark$. $\pi(20) = 1600 - 800 - 50 = \boxed{750}$

(d) $\pi(q) = 30q - 0.5q^2 - 6q - 10 = 24q - 0.5q^2 - 10$

$$\text{FOC: } 24 - q = 0 \Rightarrow q^* = 24 \qquad \text{SOC: } -1 < 0 \;\checkmark$$ $$\pi(24) = 576 - 288 - 10 = \boxed{278}$$

(e) $TR = 100Q - Q^2$, so $MR = 100 - 2Q$; $MC = 2Q$.

$$100 - 2Q = 2Q \Rightarrow Q^* = 25, \qquad P^* = 100 - 25 = 75$$ $$\pi = 75(25) - 25^2 = 1875 - 625 = \boxed{1250}$$

SOC: $\pi'' = -2 - 2 = -4 < 0$ $\checkmark$

Minimization & Multiple Candidates

When the SOC Really Matters

Worked Example 1

Find and classify all critical points of $f(x) = -x^3 + 3x$.

FOC:

$$f'(x) = -3x^2 + 3 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 1 \text{ or } x = -1$$

SOC: $f''(x) = -6x$. Check each candidate:

Treat the Other Variable as a Number

Concept

When computing $f_x$, pretend $y$ is a constant like $5$. Any term with no $x$ in it has derivative $0$. Any $y$ attached to an $x$-term just rides along as a coefficient.

Worked Example 1

Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y) = x^3y^2 + 4x - 2y^3$.

$f_x$ (treat $y$ as constant): $x^3y^2 \to 3x^2y^2$; $\;4x \to 4$; $\;-2y^3 \to 0$.

$$\boxed{f_x = 3x^2y^2 + 4}$$

$f_y$ (treat $x$ as constant): $x^3y^2 \to 2x^3y$; $\;4x \to 0$; $\;-2y^3 \to -6y^2$.

$$\boxed{f_y = 2x^3y - 6y^2}$$
Worked Example 2

Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ for $f(x,y) = x^2e^{y} + y\ln x + 3y^2$.

$f_x$ (treat $y$, and therefore $e^y$, as constants): $x^2e^y \to 2xe^y$; $\;y\ln x \to y\cdot\frac{1}{x}$; $\;3y^2 \to 0$.

$$\boxed{f_x = 2xe^{y} + \frac{y}{x}}$$

$f_y$ (treat $x$, and therefore $\ln x$, as constants): $x^2e^y \to x^2e^y$; $\;y\ln x \to \ln x$; $\;3y^2 \to 6y$.

$$\boxed{f_y = x^2e^{y} + \ln x + 6y}$$

Marginal Utility, the MRS, and the Log Shortcut

Step 1: Marginal Utilities Are Just Partial Derivatives

Concept

A utility function $U(x,y)$ assigns a "happiness number" to each bundle of goods. The marginal utility of $x$ is how much $U$ rises when you get a little more $x$, holding $y$ fixed — which is exactly the partial derivative with respect to $x$. Same for $y$.

$$MU_x = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = U_x \qquad\qquad MU_y = \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = U_y$$

Most utility functions in this course are Cobb-Douglas: $U(x,y) = x^a y^b$. Their partials come from the power rule with fractional exponents, which is where things get fiddly — so we'll do it carefully.

Worked Example 1

$U(x,y) = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$. Find $MU_x$ and $MU_y$.

$MU_x$: treat $y^{1/2}$ as a constant coefficient sitting in front of $x^{1/2}$. Power rule on $x^{1/2}$: bring down $\tfrac12$, subtract $1$ from the exponent.

$$MU_x = y^{1/2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}x^{1/2 - 1} = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}$$

If you want it without negative exponents: $x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$, so $MU_x = \dfrac{\sqrt{y}}{2\sqrt{x}}$.

$MU_y$: now $x^{1/2}$ is the constant. Power rule on $y^{1/2}$:

$$MU_y = x^{1/2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}y^{1/2 - 1} = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{1/2}y^{-1/2} = \frac{\sqrt{x}}{2\sqrt{y}}$$ $$\boxed{MU_x = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}} \qquad \boxed{MU_y = \tfrac{1}{2}x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}}$$

Step 2: The MRS Is the Ratio of Marginal Utilities

Concept

The marginal rate of substitution answers: "how many units of $y$ would this person give up to get one more unit of $x$ and feel exactly as happy?" If $x$ gives twice the marginal utility of $y$, they'd trade away two $y$'s for one $x$. So the exchange rate is the ratio:

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{U_x}{U_y}$$

Computing it is two partials and one division — but the division is where exponent bookkeeping bites, so watch each move.

Worked Example 2 — dividing directly

Using $MU_x$ and $MU_y$ from Worked Example 1, find the MRS for $U = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$.

1. Set up the ratio.

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}}$$

2. Cancel the constants. The $\tfrac12$'s divide out:

$$= \frac{x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}}{x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}}$$

3. Handle the $x$'s. Same base, so subtract exponents: $x^{-1/2 - 1/2} = x^{-1}$.

4. Handle the $y$'s. $y^{1/2 - (-1/2)} = y^{1/2 + 1/2} = y^{1}$.

$$= x^{-1}y^{1} = \boxed{\frac{y}{x}}$$

Notice steps 3 and 4 required two separate exponent subtractions, one of them with a double negative. That's the part the next step lets you skip.

Step 3: The Log Shortcut — Transform First, Then Differentiate

Concept

Rule of thumb: whenever you need the MRS of $x^a y^b$, take the natural log of $U$ before you take any derivatives. The exponents become plain coefficients, the derivative of $\ln x$ is just $\dfrac{1}{x}$, and no fractional or negative exponents ever appear.

The recipe, one line per step:

1. Transform with $\ln(x^a y^b) = a\ln x + b\ln y$:

$$\ln U = a\ln x + b\ln y$$

2. Differentiate each side with respect to $x$, then with respect to $y$ (the other term is a constant and drops out):

$$\frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \frac{a}{x} \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \frac{b}{y}$$

3. Divide the $x$-derivative by the $y$-derivative:

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{a/x}{b/y} = \frac{a}{x}\cdot\frac{y}{b} = \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{y}{x}$$

Why you're allowed to do this. By the chain rule, $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \dfrac{1}{U}\cdot U_x$ and $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \dfrac{1}{U}\cdot U_y$. Both derivatives pick up the same factor $\dfrac{1}{U}$, so when you divide one by the other that factor cancels and you're left with $\dfrac{U_x}{U_y}$ — the MRS, unchanged. Caution: the shortcut is only valid for the ratio. $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x}$ is not $MU_x$; if a question asks for a marginal utility by itself, differentiate $U$ directly as in Step 1.

Worked Example 3 — same MRS, log route

$U(x,y) = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$ again. Find the MRS using logs, and confirm it matches Worked Example 2.

1. Transform. Take $\ln$ of both sides. Product rule for logs splits the two factors; power rule for logs brings each exponent down front:

$$\ln U = \ln\!\big(x^{1/2}y^{1/2}\big) = \ln\!\big(x^{1/2}\big) + \ln\!\big(y^{1/2}\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln x + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln y$$

2. Differentiate with respect to $x$. The term $\tfrac12\ln y$ has no $x$ in it, so it's a constant and drops. The derivative of $\ln x$ is $\tfrac{1}{x}$:

$$\frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{2x}$$

Differentiate with respect to $y$. Now $\tfrac12 \ln x$ is the constant that drops:

$$\frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \frac{1}{2y}$$

3. Divide. Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its flip:

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{1/(2x)}{1/(2y)} = \frac{1}{2x}\cdot\frac{2y}{1} = \frac{2y}{2x} = \boxed{\frac{y}{x}}$$

Same answer as Worked Example 2 — with no exponent subtraction anywhere.

Worked Example 4 — with a constant out front

$U(x,y) = 4x^{0.3}y^{0.7}$. Find the MRS.

1. Transform. There are three factors now: $4$, $x^{0.3}$, $y^{0.7}$. The log of a product is the sum of the logs:

$$\ln U = \ln 4 + \ln\!\big(x^{0.3}\big) + \ln\!\big(y^{0.7}\big) = \ln 4 + 0.3\ln x + 0.7\ln y$$

2. Differentiate. $\ln 4$ is just a number, so it vanishes both times:

$$\frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \frac{0.3}{x} \qquad\qquad \frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \frac{0.7}{y}$$

3. Divide.

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{0.3/x}{0.7/y} = \frac{0.3}{x}\cdot\frac{y}{0.7} = \frac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot\frac{y}{x} = \boxed{\frac{3y}{7x}}$$

The $4$ never affected the answer: multiplying a utility function by a positive constant never changes the MRS.

Level Curves & the Slope of an Indifference Curve

Where Indifference Curves and Isoquants Come From

Concept

A level curve of $f(x,y)$ is the set of $(x,y)$ points where $f$ takes one fixed value $c$. In micro these have names: level curves of a utility function are indifference curves; level curves of a production function are isoquants. Moving along one, $f$ doesn't change, and that single fact gives us the slope:

$$\text{Along } f(x,y) = c:\qquad f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{f_x}{f_y}$$

Econ translation: the slope of an indifference curve is $-\dfrac{U_x}{U_y} = -\text{MRS}$. That's why the MRS shows up as a slope in the tangency figure above, and why "MRS $= p_x/p_y$" means "indifference curve is tangent to the budget line."

Figure. Level curves of $U = xy$ for $U = 2, 4, 8, 12$. Every point on one curve gives the same utility; curves farther from the origin are higher utility. Each is $y = c/x$, a hyperbola.

Worked Example 1

$U(x,y) = xy$. Write the indifference curve for $U = 12$ as $y$ in terms of $x$, find its slope at $(3, 4)$, and confirm it equals $-\text{MRS}$.

1. Solve for $y$. $xy = 12 \Rightarrow y = \dfrac{12}{x} = 12x^{-1}$.

2. Differentiate. $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -12x^{-2} = -\dfrac{12}{x^2}$. At $x = 3$: $-\dfrac{12}{9} = \boxed{-\tfrac{4}{3}}$.

3. Compare with $-U_x/U_y$. $U_x = y$, $U_y = x$, so $-\dfrac{U_x}{U_y} = -\dfrac{y}{x} = -\dfrac{4}{3}$ at $(3,4)$. $\checkmark$ Same slope, no need to solve for $y$.

Worked Example 2 — why $dy/dx = -f_x/f_y$

Derive the level-curve slope formula.

0. The plan. A small step along the curve changes $x$ by $dx$ and $y$ by $dy$. The total change in $f$ is the sum of the two partial effects. On a level curve that total change must be zero — that one equation gives the slope.

1. Total change in $f$. Moving $x$ by $dx$ changes $f$ by about $f_x\,dx$; moving $y$ by $dy$ changes it by about $f_y\,dy$. Together:

$$df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy$$

2. Stay on the level curve. $f$ is constant there, so $df = 0$:

$$f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy = 0$$

3. Solve for the slope. Move $f_x\,dx$ across and divide by $f_y\,dx$:

$$f_y\,dy = -f_x\,dx \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{f_x}{f_y}}$$

The minus sign is why indifference curves slope down: if both marginal utilities are positive, giving up some $y$ must be compensated with more $x$.

Exponent Rules in Action

Simplifying Before (or After) You Differentiate

Concept

Most exponent errors come from mixing up "same base" rules (add exponents) with "same exponent" rules (combine bases). Say out loud which one you're using.

Worked Example 1

Show that $F(K,L) = K^{0.5}L^{0.5}$ satisfies $F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda F(K,L)$. (Section 2 explains what this means economically.)

Plug in $\lambda K$ and $\lambda L$, then split each factor using $(xy)^a = x^a y^a$:

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = (\lambda K)^{0.5}(\lambda L)^{0.5} = \lambda^{0.5}K^{0.5}\,\lambda^{0.5}L^{0.5}$$

Combine the $\lambda$'s using $x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$:

$$= \lambda^{0.5 + 0.5}K^{0.5}L^{0.5} = \lambda^{1}\,K^{0.5}L^{0.5} = \boxed{\lambda F(K,L)}$$
Worked Example 2

Simplify $\dfrac{\left(x^{1/2}y\right)^4}{xy^2}$.

Numerator: apply the power of a power rule to each factor:

$$\left(x^{1/2}y\right)^4 = x^{(1/2)\cdot 4}\,y^{4} = x^2y^4$$

Divide using $x^a/x^b = x^{a-b}$ on each base separately:

$$\frac{x^2y^4}{xy^2} = x^{2-1}y^{4-2} = \boxed{xy^2}$$

Returns to Scale

Scaling Every Input by the Same Factor

Concept

Take a production function $F(K,L)$ and multiply both inputs by the same number $\lambda > 1$ (say $\lambda = 2$: double everything). Does output double, more than double, or less than double? That's the returns-to-scale question, and it's answered by comparing $F(\lambda K, \lambda L)$ to $\lambda F(K,L)$:

Constant returns to scale (CRS): $F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = \lambda\,F(K,L)$  — output scales exactly.

Increasing returns (IRS): $F(\lambda K, \lambda L) > \lambda\,F(K,L)$  — output more than scales.

Decreasing returns (DRS): $F(\lambda K, \lambda L) < \lambda\,F(K,L)$  — output less than scales.

For Cobb-Douglas the exponent rules do all the work. Using $(xy)^a = x^a y^a$ and $x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$:

$$F(K,L) = AK^aL^b \;\Longrightarrow\; F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = A(\lambda K)^a(\lambda L)^b = \lambda^{a+b}\,AK^aL^b = \lambda^{a+b}F(K,L)$$

So compare $\lambda^{a+b}$ with $\lambda^1$:   $a + b = 1 \Rightarrow$ CRS,   $a + b > 1 \Rightarrow$ IRS,   $a + b < 1 \Rightarrow$ DRS. The constant $A$ never matters.

Don't just quote the rule on an exam — show the computation of $F(\lambda K, \lambda L)$, then conclude. The rule tells you what the answer will be; the computation is the "show your work."

Worked Example 1 — increasing returns

$F(K,L) = K^{0.6}L^{0.6}$. Classify the returns to scale, and say what happens to output when both inputs double.

1. Substitute $\lambda K$ and $\lambda L$.

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = (\lambda K)^{0.6}(\lambda L)^{0.6}$$

2. Split each factor with $(xy)^a = x^a y^a$:

$$= \lambda^{0.6}K^{0.6}\cdot\lambda^{0.6}L^{0.6}$$

3. Collect the $\lambda$'s with $x^a x^b = x^{a+b}$:

$$= \lambda^{0.6 + 0.6}K^{0.6}L^{0.6} = \lambda^{1.2}F(K,L)$$

4. Compare to $\lambda^1$. Since $1.2 > 1$, $\lambda^{1.2} > \lambda$ for any $\lambda > 1$: $\boxed{\text{increasing returns to scale}}$.

Doubling inputs: $\lambda = 2$ gives $2^{1.2} \approx 2.30$, so output goes up by about $130\%$, not $100\%$.

Worked Example 2 — decreasing returns, with a constant

$F(K,L) = 2K^{0.3}L^{0.5}$. Classify the returns to scale.

1. Substitute. The $2$ is untouched — only the inputs get scaled:

$$F(\lambda K, \lambda L) = 2(\lambda K)^{0.3}(\lambda L)^{0.5}$$

2. Split and collect.

$$= 2\,\lambda^{0.3}K^{0.3}\,\lambda^{0.5}L^{0.5} = \lambda^{0.8}\cdot 2K^{0.3}L^{0.5} = \lambda^{0.8}F(K,L)$$

3. Compare. $0.8 < 1$, so $\lambda^{0.8} < \lambda$: $\boxed{\text{decreasing returns to scale}}$. Doubling inputs multiplies output by $2^{0.8} \approx 1.74$.

Log Rules

Expanding, Condensing, and Undoing

Concept

Logs turn multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, and powers into multiplication. $\ln$ and $e$ undo each other.

$$\ln(xy) = \ln x + \ln y \qquad \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \ln x - \ln y \qquad \ln(x^a) = a\ln x$$ $$\ln(e^{x}) = x \qquad e^{\ln x} = x \qquad \ln 1 = 0 \qquad \ln e = 1$$

Not rules: $\ln(x + y) \neq \ln x + \ln y$, and $(\ln x)^2 \neq 2\ln x$. Logs only split apart products, quotients, and powers.

Figure. $\ln x$ and $e^x$ are inverses, which is exactly why $\ln(e^x) = x$ and $e^{\ln x} = x$. Also visible: $\ln x$ is increasing but flattening (that's $\frac{1}{x}$ shrinking), which is why log utility gives diminishing marginal utility.

Worked Example 1

Expand $\ln\!\left(\dfrac{x^3y^2}{z}\right)$ into a sum of simple logs.

Quotient rule first:

$$\ln(x^3y^2) - \ln z$$

Product rule on the first piece:

$$\ln(x^3) + \ln(y^2) - \ln z$$

Power rule to bring exponents down:

$$\boxed{3\ln x + 2\ln y - \ln z}$$
Worked Example 2

Solve $e^{2x} = 10$ for $x$.

Take $\ln$ of both sides; $\ln(e^{2x}) = 2x$ because $\ln$ undoes $e$:

$$\ln(e^{2x}) = \ln 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = \ln 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{x = \frac{\ln 10}{2} \approx 1.151}$$

The Log Transformation for Cobb-Douglas

Transform First, Differentiate Second

Concept

Recall from the Partial Derivatives tab: $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \dfrac{U_x}{U_y}$. This section is extra practice with the shortcut, now that the log rules above are fresh.

Whenever you need the MRS (or MRTS) of a Cobb-Douglas function, take logs before you take derivatives. $U = Ax^a y^b$ becomes $\ln U = \ln A + a\ln x + b\ln y$. Differentiating $\ln x$ gives $\dfrac{1}{x}$ — no fractional exponents, no negative exponents, nothing to simplify at the end.

Start: $U = Ax^ay^b$

1. Transform: $\ln U = \ln A + a\ln x + b\ln y$

2. Differentiate: $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \dfrac{a}{x}$  and  $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \dfrac{b}{y}$  ($\ln A$ drops out)

3. Divide: $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{a/x}{b/y} = \dfrac{a}{b}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}$

Why it works: $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \dfrac{U_x}{U}$ and $\dfrac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \dfrac{U_y}{U}$ (chain rule with $\ln$ on the outside). Dividing cancels the $U$: $\dfrac{U_x/U}{U_y/U} = \dfrac{U_x}{U_y}$. The transformation preserves the ratio, not the individual marginal utilities — if you're asked for $U_x$ alone, differentiate $U$ directly.

Worked Example 1

$U(x,y) = x^{0.4}y^{0.6}$. Find the MRS.

1. Transform. Product rule then power rule for logs:

$$\ln U = \ln(x^{0.4}) + \ln(y^{0.6}) = 0.4\ln x + 0.6\ln y$$

2. Differentiate with respect to each variable (the other term is a constant and drops):

$$\frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial x} = \frac{0.4}{x}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln U}{\partial y} = \frac{0.6}{y}$$

3. Divide.

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{0.4/x}{0.6/y} = \frac{0.4}{0.6}\cdot\frac{y}{x} = \boxed{\frac{2y}{3x}}$$
Worked Example 2 — general case

$F(K,L) = AK^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$. Find $\text{MRTS} = \dfrac{MP_L}{MP_K}$.

1. Transform.

$$\ln F = \ln A + \alpha\ln K + (1-\alpha)\ln L$$

2. Differentiate. $\ln A$ is a constant and disappears:

$$\frac{\partial \ln F}{\partial K} = \frac{\alpha}{K}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln F}{\partial L} = \frac{1-\alpha}{L}$$

3. Divide ($L$-derivative on top since MRTS $= MP_L/MP_K$):

$$\text{MRTS} = \frac{(1-\alpha)/L}{\alpha/K} = \boxed{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\frac{K}{L}}$$

Compare to doing this directly: $MP_K = \alpha AK^{\alpha-1}L^{1-\alpha}$ and $MP_L = (1-\alpha)AK^{\alpha}L^{-\alpha}$ — correct, but far more places to slip.

2. Substitute into the second equation:

3. Solve:

4. Substitute back:

Elimination Method

When Substitution Gets Messy

Concept

If neither equation has an easy variable to isolate, add or subtract the equations (after scaling one of them) so that one variable cancels. Substitution and elimination always give the same answer — pick whichever is less work.

Worked Example 1

Solve: $\;3x + 2y = 12\;$ and $\;5x - 2y = 4$.

The $y$-coefficients are already $+2$ and $-2$, so add the equations and $y$ vanishes:

$$(3x + 2y) + (5x - 2y) = 12 + 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; 8x = 16 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{x = 2}$$

Back-substitute into either equation: $3(2) + 2y = 12 \Rightarrow 2y = 6 \Rightarrow \boxed{y = 3}$.

Worked Example 2

Solve: $\;2x + 3y = 13\;$ and $\;4x - y = 5$.

Nothing cancels yet. Scale the first equation by $2$ so the $x$-coefficients match:

$$4x + 6y = 26 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 4x - y = 5$$

Subtract the second from the first:

$$(4x + 6y) - (4x - y) = 26 - 5 \;\Longrightarrow\; 7y = 21 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{y = 3}$$

Back-substitute: $4x - 3 = 5 \Rightarrow \boxed{x = 2}$.

$$\varepsilon = (-4)\cdot\frac{15}{60} = \boxed{-1} \quad\text{(unit elastic)}$$

4. Where is $|\varepsilon| = 1$ in general? Set $4\cdot\dfrac{P}{120 - 4P} = 1$:

$$4P = 120 - 4P \;\Longrightarrow\; 8P = 120 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{P = 15}$$

So $P = 15$ is the unit-elastic point — it's the midpoint of the demand curve (the vertical intercept is $P = 30$). Above it demand is elastic, below it inelastic. Compare with the Bonus problem: this is also where $MR = 0$.

14

Find and classify every critical point of $f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x + 1$.

Optimization — multiple candidates

1. FOC.

$$f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 3(x - 1)(x - 3) = 0$$

Two candidates: $x = 1$ and $x = 3$.

2. SOC — check each one separately. $f''(x) = 6x - 12$.

$$f''(1) = -6 < 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{x = 1 \text{ is a local max}}$$ $$f''(3) = 6 > 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{x = 3 \text{ is a local min}}$$

3. Values (if asked): $f(1) = 1 - 6 + 9 + 1 = 5$ and $f(3) = 27 - 54 + 27 + 1 = 1$.

Common trap: reporting "$x = 1$ and $x = 3$ are the maximum and minimum" without saying which is which. The SOC is what tells you.

15

$U(x,y) = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$. Write the indifference curve for $U = 6$ as $y$ in terms of $x$, find its slope at the bundle $(4, 9)$, and confirm it equals $-\text{MRS}$ there.

Level curves

Key: a level curve is $U(x,y) = c$; its slope is $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{U_x}{U_y} = -\text{MRS}$

1. Solve the level curve for $y$. Square both sides to clear the roots:

$$x^{1/2}y^{1/2} = 6 \;\Longrightarrow\; xy = 36 \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{36}{x} = 36x^{-1}$$

(Check that $(4, 9)$ is on it: $4 \cdot 9 = 36$ $\checkmark$.)

2. Differentiate for the slope:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -36x^{-2} = -\frac{36}{x^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{at } x = 4:\; -\frac{36}{16} = \boxed{-\frac{9}{4}}$$

3. Compare with $-\text{MRS}$. By logs, $\ln U = \tfrac12\ln x + \tfrac12\ln y$ gives $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{y}{x}$. At $(4, 9)$: $-\dfrac{9}{4}$. $\checkmark$ Same number — and this route never needed us to solve for $y$.

16

Two goods are related: $Q_A = 40 - 2P_A + P_B$ and $Q_B = 30 + P_A - 2P_B$. If the observed quantities are $Q_A = 15$ and $Q_B = 20$, find both prices.

System of equations — elimination

Givens: $15 = 40 - 2P_A + P_B$ and $20 = 30 + P_A - 2P_B$  |  Find: $P_A$, $P_B$

1. Tidy each equation so the unknowns are on the left and constants on the right:

$$2P_A - P_B = 25 \qquad\qquad -P_A + 2P_B = 10$$

2. Elimination. Scale the second equation by $2$ so the $P_A$ coefficients are $+2$ and $-2$, then add:

$$\begin{aligned} 2P_A - P_B &= 25 \\ -2P_A + 4P_B &= 20 \\ \hline 3P_B &= 45 \end{aligned} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{P_B = 15}$$

3. Back-substitute into the first equation:

$$2P_A - 15 = 25 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2P_A = 40 \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{P_A = 20}$$

Check both originals: $Q_A = 40 - 40 + 15 = 15$ $\checkmark$; $Q_B = 30 + 20 - 30 = 20$ $\checkmark$.

Why elimination here: substitution works too ($P_B = 2P_A - 25$ from the first), but with both coefficients on both variables, scale-and-add is fewer steps.

17

A consumer has quasilinear utility $U(x,y) = \ln x + y$ and faces $p_x = 2$, $p_y = 1$. Find $MU_x$, $MU_y$, and the MRS, then use the tangency condition to find $x^*$. What's unusual about the answer?

Partials with ln + tangency

Givens: $U = \ln x + y$, $p_x = 2$, $p_y = 1$  |  Key: $\text{MRS} = MU_x/MU_y = p_x/p_y$ at the optimum

1. Marginal utilities. Not Cobb-Douglas, so no log trick needed — differentiate directly:

$$MU_x = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad MU_y = \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = 1$$

2. MRS.

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \frac{1/x}{1} = \frac{1}{x}$$

3. Tangency.

$$\frac{1}{x} = \frac{p_x}{p_y} = \frac{2}{1} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{x^* = \tfrac{1}{2}}$$

4. What's unusual: the MRS depends only on $x$, so $x^*$ is pinned down by prices alone — income didn't enter. Any extra income goes entirely to $y$. (You'd need the budget line to find $y^*$: with income $I$, $y^* = I - 2(1/2) = I - 1$.)

18

An economy's output grows as $Y(t) = Y_0\,e^{0.03t}$, with $t$ in years. How many years until output doubles? Give an exact expression and a decimal.

Solving with logs

Givens: $Y(t) = Y_0e^{0.03t}$  |  Find: $t$ such that $Y(t) = 2Y_0$  |  Key: $\ln(e^{u}) = u$

1. Set up the doubling condition and cancel $Y_0$:

$$Y_0e^{0.03t} = 2Y_0 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{0.03t} = 2$$

2. Take $\ln$ of both sides. The $\ln$ undoes the $e$:

$$\ln\!\big(e^{0.03t}\big) = \ln 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.03t = \ln 2$$

3. Solve.

$$\boxed{t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.03} \approx \frac{0.693}{0.03} \approx 23.1 \text{ years}}$$

Rule of 70: since $\ln 2 \approx 0.69$, doubling time $\approx 70/(\text{growth rate in \%}) = 70/3 \approx 23.3$. Same idea, same log rule.

★

Bonus — prove it. A monopolist faces linear inverse demand $P = a - bQ$ with $a, b > 0$. Show that marginal revenue is also linear, has the same vertical intercept as demand, and is exactly twice as steep.

Power rule + definition of MR

0. The plan. Build total revenue from the demand curve, differentiate, and read off the slope and intercept.

1. Total revenue. Revenue is price times quantity, with price given by the demand curve:

$$TR(Q) = P\cdot Q = (a - bQ)Q = aQ - bQ^2$$

2. Marginal revenue is $TR'(Q)$. Power rule term by term:

$$MR(Q) = \frac{d}{dQ}\big(aQ - bQ^2\big) = a - 2bQ$$

3. Read it off. $MR = a - 2bQ$ is a line in $Q$, so $MR$ is linear. Its intercept (at $Q = 0$) is $a$, the same as demand's. Its slope is $-2b$, versus $-b$ for demand:

$$\boxed{\text{slope of } MR = -2b = 2\times(\text{slope of demand})}$$

Why it makes sense. To sell one more unit the monopolist must lower the price on every unit, not just the last one, so revenue rises by less than the price. $MR$ sits below demand and falls faster.

Corollary worth remembering. $MR = 0$ at $Q = \dfrac{a}{2b}$, exactly halfway to the horizontal intercept of demand $\left(Q = \dfrac{a}{b}\right)$. Revenue is maximized there — and that's also where demand is unit elastic.
