2

What “really inferior” means

For a price increase:

  • The substitution effect is always negative, for every good. A higher price always pushes the consumer away from the good.
  • For any inferior good, the income effect is positive. The price increase makes the consumer poorer, and a poorer consumer buys more of an inferior good.
  • A good is Giffen when that positive income effect is so large it outweighs the always-negative substitution effect.
$$\underbrace{SE}_{\text{always } < 0} \;+\; \underbrace{IE}_{> 0 \text{ and large}} \;=\; \text{Total effect} > 0$$ $$\boxed{\text{Giffen} \iff IE > |SE|}$$
3

How to solve any problem: the 3-step chain

  1. Identify the substitution effect. It always moves opposite to the price.
  2. Normal or inferior? Normal: the income effect moves the same way as SE, so you are done (ordinary). Inferior: the income effect moves opposite to SE, so go to step 3.
  3. Inferior only: is the income effect bigger than the substitution effect? No: inferior but ordinary. Yes: Giffen.
4

Key takeaways

Normal ⇒ ordinary

Every normal good is ordinary, because both effects push the same direction.

Giffen ⇒ inferior

Every Giffen good is inferior, because you need a positive income effect to fight the substitution effect.

Inferior ⇏ Giffen

Most inferior goods are NOT Giffen. Their income effect is positive but too small to beat the substitution effect.

Price falls? Flip every sign.

For a price decrease, SE is always positive, and a Giffen good is bought less when it gets cheaper.

1

Two separate questions

Students often treat “normal / inferior / Giffen” as one list of three. It is really two yes-or-no questions about two different variables.

The income question

If I get richer (prices fixed), do I buy more?

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial m} > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{normal}$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial m} < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{inferior}$$

Equivalent: income elasticity $\varepsilon_{x,m} > 0$ (normal) or $< 0$ (inferior).

The own-price question

If the good itself gets pricier, do I buy less?

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{ordinary}$$

$$\frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{Giffen}$$

Equivalent: own-price elasticity $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} < 0$ (ordinary) or $> 0$ (Giffen).

3

Is own-price elasticity always negative?

No. It is negative for almost every good, but Giffen goods are the exception.

$$\varepsilon_{x,p_x} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x}\cdot\frac{p_x}{x}$$

Price $p_x$ and quantity $x$ are both positive, so $\frac{p_x}{x} > 0$. That means the elasticity always has the same sign as $\frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x}$:

  • Ordinary good: $\frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} < 0$, so $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} < 0$.
  • Giffen good: $\frac{\partial x}{\partial p_x} > 0$, so $\varepsilon_{x,p_x} > 0$.

Watch the convention. Some textbooks report own-price elasticity as an absolute value, so an “elasticity of 2” means $-2$. Check which convention your class uses before you call an answer wrong. Cross-price and income elasticities have no “usually negative” rule: either sign is normal.

1

Every price change splits into two effects

When $p_x$ rises, the change in $x$ is the sum of two pieces:

$$\underbrace{\Delta x}_{\text{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{SE}_{\substack{\text{X got}\\\text{pricier}}} \;+\; \underbrace{IE}_{\substack{\text{you got}\\\text{poorer}}}$$
  • Substitution effect (SE). Hold utility fixed and change only the price ratio. You always slide away from the good that got pricier, so for a price rise $SE \le 0$. (It is strictly negative unless the goods are perfect complements, where the kink makes it zero.)
  • Income effect (IE). The higher price shrinks your purchasing power. Its sign depends on the income question: negative for a normal good, positive for an inferior good.

3

The Giffen condition, step by step

0

The plan. Write the total effect as SE plus IE, plug in what we know about each sign, and ask when the total can be positive.

1

Decompose. $\Delta x = SE + IE$.

2

Use the sign of SE. For a price rise $SE < 0$, so we can write $SE = -|SE|$:

$$\Delta x = IE - |SE|$$

3

Ask when the total is positive.

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta x > 0 &\iff IE - |SE| > 0 \\ &\iff IE > |SE| \end{aligned}$$

4

Read off the consequence. Since $|SE| > 0$, the condition needs $IE > 0$, which means the good must be inferior. So every Giffen good is inferior, but an inferior good is Giffen only if its IE is bigger than $|SE|$.

$$\boxed{\text{Giffen} \iff IE > |SE|}$$

A positive income effect that outweighs the always-negative substitution effect.

Does this happen in real life? Rarely, and only under specific conditions: a staple that takes up a big share of a poor household’s budget, with no cheap substitute. Jensen and Miller (2008, American Economic Review) found Giffen behavior for rice among very poor households in Hunan, China: when rice got pricier, they could afford even less meat, so they filled the gap with more rice.

1

The 3-step chain

Use the same three questions, in the same order, on every income and substitution effect problem. Each step either settles the answer or sends you to the next step.

1

Identify the substitution effect.

The substitution effect always moves opposite to the price. Write its direction down first.

Price rises ↑SE says buy less X.
Price falls ↓SE says buy more X.
2

Is the good normal or inferior?

This tells you which way the income effect points relative to the substitution effect.

NormalThe income effect moves in the same direction as the substitution effect. Both arrows agree, so you are done.Ordinary · stop here
InferiorThe income effect moves opposite to the substitution effect. Now there is a tug-of-war.Go to step 3
3

Inferior only: is the income effect bigger than the substitution effect?

Compare sizes (absolute values). The bigger effect decides which way $x$ moves.

No: $|IE| < |SE|$The substitution effect wins. $x$ still moves opposite to price.Inferior but ordinary
Yes: $|IE| > |SE|$The income effect wins. $x$ moves in the same direction as price.Giffen

Why step 3 is “inferior only.” For a normal good the two effects point the same way, so their sizes do not matter: they add up in one direction. Size only matters when they fight, and they only fight for an inferior good. That is why a normal good can never be Giffen.

2

Worked problems

Example 1: A normal good gets pricier

Worked Example

Coffee is a normal good for Maya. The price of coffee rises. What happens to how much coffee she buys?

Givens: price of coffee ↑; coffee is normal.
Step 1 · SEPrice rises, so the substitution effect says buy less coffee.
Step 2 · Normal?Yes, normal. The income effect moves the same way: Maya is effectively poorer, so she also buys less.
Step 3 · Size?Skip. Both arrows point down, so there is nothing to compare.
Coffee purchases fall for sure. Coffee is ordinary.

Example 2: An inferior good gets cheaper

Worked Example

Bus rides are an inferior good for Jordan (as his income grows, he switches to rideshares). The bus fare falls. The substitution effect is $+6$ rides per month and the income effect is $-2$ rides per month. Classify bus rides for Jordan.

Givens: fare ↓; bus rides are inferior; $SE = +6$, $IE = -2$.
Step 1 · SEPrice falls, so the substitution effect says ride more: $SE = +6$.
Step 2 · Normal?No, inferior. The income effect moves opposite to SE: the cheaper fare makes Jordan effectively richer, so he rides less: $IE = -2$. Tug-of-war, so go on.
Step 3 · Size?$|IE| = 2 < |SE| = 6$. The substitution effect wins. Total $= +6 + (-2) = +4$ rides.
Rides rise as the fare falls. Bus rides are inferior but ordinary.

Example 3: A Giffen good

Worked Example

A very poor household spends most of its budget on rice. The price of rice rises. The substitution effect is $-3$ kg and the income effect is $+5$ kg. Classify rice for this household.

Givens: price of rice ↑; $SE = -3$, $IE = +5$.
Step 1 · SEPrice rises, so the substitution effect says buy less: $SE = -3$.
Step 2 · Normal?The income effect ($+5$) points opposite to SE, so rice must be inferior. Tug-of-war, so go on.
Step 3 · Size?$|IE| = 5 > |SE| = 3$. The income effect wins. Total $= -3 + 5 = +2$ kg.
The household buys more rice when it gets pricier. Rice is Giffen.

Example 4: Computing the effects first (Cobb-Douglas)

Worked Example

$U(x,y) = xy$, income $M = 16$, $p_y = 1$. The price of X rises from $p_x = 1$ to $p_x' = 4$. Find the substitution and income effects on X, then run the chain.

Givens: $U = xy$; $M = 16$; $p_y = 1$; $p_x$: $1 \to 4$. Cobb-Douglas with equal weights, so $x^* = \dfrac{M}{2p_x}$ and $y^* = \dfrac{M}{2p_y}$.

A (original): $x_A = \dfrac{16}{2(1)} = 8$, $\;y_A = 8$, $\;U_0 = 8 \cdot 8 = 64$.

C (new price): $x_C = \dfrac{16}{2(4)} = 2$.

B (old utility, new slope): tangency $\dfrac{y}{x} = 4 \Rightarrow y = 4x$. On the old curve: $x(4x) = 64 \Rightarrow x^2 = 16 \Rightarrow x_B = 4$.

$SE = x_B - x_A = 4 - 8 = -4$, $\quad IE = x_C - x_B = 2 - 4 = -2$.

Step 1 · SEPrice rises, and $SE = -4$: buy less, as expected.
Step 2 · Normal?$IE = -2$ points the same way as SE, so X is normal. Done.
Step 3 · Size?Skip. Total $= -4 + (-2) = -6$, which matches $x_C - x_A = 2 - 8$.
X is normal and ordinary. (Cobb-Douglas goods are always normal.)
