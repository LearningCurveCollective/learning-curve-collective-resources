The Plan

Consumer theory is built from exactly two ingredients, and this sheet is about getting each one right before we combine them next lesson.

Ingredient 1 — what you want

Preferences, summarized by a utility function $u(x,y)$ and drawn as indifference curves. The slope of an indifference curve is the MRS: your personal exchange rate between the two goods.

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y}$$

Ingredient 2 — what you can have

The budget constraint: every bundle you can exactly afford with income $m$ at prices $p_x, p_y$. Its slope is the market's exchange rate.

$$p_x x + p_y y = m \qquad \text{slope} = -\frac{p_x}{p_y}$$

Next lesson's whole job is to find where those two exchange rates agree. This lesson makes sure you can (1) write down and differentiate every common utility function, (2) compute and interpret its MRS, (3) draw its indifference curves, and (4) draw a budget line in ten seconds from its intercepts.

In one line: preferences say what you want; the budget says what you can have; the MRS and the price ratio are the two slopes that will meet next lesson.

1

Notation used on this sheet

Symbols
  • $x, y$ — quantities of the two goods (a bundle is a point $(x,y)$). Your textbook may write $x_1, x_2$; it is the same thing.
  • $u(x,y)$ — the utility function; $MU_x = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ and $MU_y = \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ are the marginal utilities (partial derivatives).
  • $\alpha, \beta$ — exponents in Cobb–Douglas $u = x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}$; $a, b$ — weights in $ax + by$ and $\min(ax, by)$.
  • $p_x, p_y$ — prices, taken as given (exogenously set); $m$ — income.
  • $\text{MRS}_{xy}$ — marginal rate of substitution of $y$ for $x$: how much $y$ you would give up for one more unit of $x$. We report it as a positive number; the indifference curve's slope is $-\text{MRS}$.

Calculus needed: only the power rule, $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^{n} = n x^{n-1}$, applied one variable at a time while the other is held fixed. Every derivative on this sheet is that rule.

1

Preferences: ranking bundles

A bundle is a list of quantities, e.g. $(x,y) = (4\text{ tacos},\ 9\text{ hours of streaming})$. Preferences are the consumer's ranking of bundles. We write $A \succ B$ (“$A$ is strictly preferred to $B$”), $A \sim B$ (“indifferent”) and $A \succsim B$ (“at least as good as”). Everything in consumer theory follows from four assumptions about that ranking.

AssumptionFormal statementPlain English & what it buys us
CompletenessFor any two bundles $A, B$: either $A \succsim B$, or $B \succsim A$, or both.You can always compare. No “I can't decide” — so every bundle sits on some indifference curve.
TransitivityIf $A \succsim B$ and $B \succsim C$, then $A \succsim C$.Rankings are consistent. This is what forbids indifference curves from crossing (see below).
Monotonicity (more is better)If bundle $A$ has at least as much of every good as $B$ and strictly more of one, then $A \succ B$.Goods are goods, not bads. Consequence: indifference curves slope down, and curves further from the origin are better.
Convexity (mixes beat extremes)If $A \sim B$, then any average $tA + (1-t)B$ with $0 < t < 1$ is at least as good as $A$.Variety is (weakly) preferred. Consequence: indifference curves bow toward the origin, and the MRS falls as you move right along one.

Why these four: completeness + transitivity make preferences representable by a utility function (a number for every bundle, higher = better). Monotonicity and convexity are what give the indifference curves their familiar shape — and convexity is exactly the assumption the tangency rule will lean on next lesson.

Indifference curves

An indifference curve is the set of all bundles the consumer ranks as equally good — a contour line of the utility function, $\{(x,y): u(x,y) = k\}$. An indifference map is a family of them at different levels $k$. Utility here is $u = \sqrt{xy}$, so the curve through $A$ is $y = u_A^2/x$. Green quadrant (north-east): strictly better by monotonicity. Tan quadrant (south-west): strictly worse. The curve itself separates the two.

Property 1 — downward sloping

By monotonicity. If you take away some $y$, you must be compensated with more $x$ to stay indifferent — so the curve falls as $x$ rises.

Property 2 — higher curve = better

Also monotonicity. Any curve to the north-east contains bundles with more of both goods than some bundle on the lower curve.

Property 3 — never cross

By transitivity. Proof below: a crossing forces a contradiction with “more is better”.

Property 4 — bowed toward the origin

By convexity. The curve flattens as you move right: the more $x$ you already have, the less $y$ you will give up for one more — that is diminishing MRS (tab 3).

Why indifference curves cannot cross

A proof by contradiction, one move at a time. The two curves below are Cobb–Douglas-style ($xy = 36$ and a steeper one), drawn as if they crossed at $A = (4, 9)$. Press Next to add each step to the picture.

Step 0 of 4
0

The plan. Suppose two indifference curves $I_1$ and $I_2$ did cross at some bundle $A$. We will find two bundles that transitivity says are equally good but monotonicity says are not — a contradiction, so the crossing is impossible.

1

Pick $B$ on $I_1$ and $C$ on $I_2$, directly above one another (same $x$; $B$ has more $y$). Any two such points will do.

2

Indifference along each curve: $A \sim B$ (both on $I_1$) and $A \sim C$ (both on $I_2$). This is just the definition of an indifference curve.

3

Transitivity: $B \sim A$ and $A \sim C$ imply $B \sim C$. So the consumer must be indifferent between $B$ and $C$.

4

Monotonicity: $B$ has the same $x$ as $C$ and strictly more $y$, so $B \succ C$. That contradicts step 3. The only assumption we made was that the curves cross — so indifference curves of one consumer never cross. $\blacksquare$

1

Utility: a number for every bundle

A utility function $u(x,y)$ assigns a number to each bundle so that the numbers agree with the ranking: $u(A) > u(B)$ exactly when $A \succ B$, and $u(A) = u(B)$ exactly when $A \sim B$. That is all it does.

Ordinal, not cardinal. Utility numbers are a ranking, not a measurement. $u(A) = 6$ and $u(B) = 3$ means $A$ is better, not “twice as good.” Any relabeling that keeps the order — doubling every number, squaring them, taking logs — describes the same consumer (Bonus: Transformations tab).

Running example — tacos and streaming
  • $u(x,y) = x^{1/2}y^{1/2} = \sqrt{xy}$, with $x$ = tacos, $y$ = hours of streaming.
  • $u(4,9) = \sqrt{36} = 6$; $\;u(9,4) = 6$; $\;u(1,36) = 6$. All three bundles sit on the same indifference curve $xy = 36$.
  • $u(6,8) = \sqrt{48} \approx 6.93 > 6$, so $(6,8)$ is on a higher curve.
2

Marginal utility: the partial derivative

Marginal utility of $x$ is the extra utility from a little more $x$, holding $y$ fixed. Mathematically that is the partial derivative: differentiate with respect to $x$ treating $y$ as a constant.

$$MU_x = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \qquad MU_y = \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$$

Worked: power rule with the other variable frozen. For $u = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$, treat $y^{1/2}$ as a constant coefficient when differentiating in $x$:

General move
Running example

1. Freeze $y$; the factor $y^{\beta}$ is just a number riding along.

$u = \underbrace{y^{1/2}}_{\text{constant}}\cdot x^{1/2}$

2. Power rule on $x^{\alpha}$: bring the exponent down, subtract one.

$MU_x = y^{1/2}\cdot \tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{y}{x}}$

3. Repeat with the roles swapped for $MU_y$.

$MU_y = x^{1/2}\cdot \tfrac12 y^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{x}{y}}$

4. Evaluate at a bundle and read the units: utils per unit of the good.

At $(4,9)$: $MU_x = \tfrac12\sqrt{9/4} = 0.75$, $\;MU_y = \tfrac12\sqrt{4/9} \approx 0.333$.

Interpret the numbers

At $(4\text{ tacos}, 9\text{ hours})$, one more taco adds about $0.75$ utils; one more hour of streaming adds about $0.33$. A taco is currently worth roughly $0.75/0.33 = 2.25$ hours of streaming to this consumer — hold that ratio, it is the MRS in tab 3.

Diminishing marginal utility

Diminishing marginal utility in $x$ means $MU_x$ falls as $x$ rises (with $y$ held fixed): the 10th taco adds less than the 2nd. In symbols, $\dfrac{\partial MU_x}{\partial x} = \dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} < 0$. Slide $x$ below and watch both panels: the utility curve gets flatter (left) and the $MU_x$ curve slides downhill (right).

$$\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} < 0$$

Differentiate $MU_x$ once more in $x$. Running example: $MU_x = \tfrac12 y^{1/2}x^{-1/2}$, so $\dfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} = -\tfrac14 y^{1/2}x^{-3/2} < 0$. ✓ Diminishing.

Careful: MU is not ordinal-proof

The size of $MU_x$ changes if you relabel utility (e.g. $v = u^2 = xy$ gives $MV_x = y$, not diminishing at all in $x$). What survives relabeling is the ratio $MU_x/MU_y$. That ratio, not MU itself, is the object that carries economic meaning — next tab.

1

The MRS: slope of an indifference curve

The marginal rate of substitution of $y$ for $x$ is the amount of $y$ the consumer is willing to give up for one more unit of $x$ while staying on the same indifference curve. Geometrically it is the steepness of the curve at that bundle.

$$\text{MRS}_{xy} = \left|\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{u = \text{const}} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y}$$

Where the formula comes from (one-line version). Along an indifference curve utility does not change, so the utility gained from a small extra $dx$ must be exactly offset by the utility lost from giving up $dy$:

1

Total change in utility is zero along the curve: $\;MU_x\,dx + MU_y\,dy = 0$.

2

Solve for the slope: $\;\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}$. The minus sign says the curve slopes down; its magnitude is the MRS.

Intuition — why a ratio of marginal utilities is an exchange rate

$MU_x$ is the extra happiness one more unit of $x$ gives you. $MU_y$ is the extra happiness one unit of $y$ gives you. So the ratio $\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}$ answers: “how many units of $y$ would it take to give me the same happiness as one more $x$?” — that is exactly the amount of $y$ you would hand over for one more $x$ and feel no better or worse off.

Check the units: $\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \dfrac{\text{utils per taco}}{\text{utils per hour}} = \text{hours per taco}$. The utils cancel, which is why the MRS is a real, meaningful exchange rate even though utility numbers themselves are not.

With the running numbers at $(4, 9)$: one more taco adds $MU_x = 0.75$ utils; each hour of streaming adds $MU_y \approx 0.333$ utils. To replace the happiness of one taco you would need $0.75 / 0.333 = 2.25$ hours — so you would give up 2.25 hours for one more taco. The minus sign in $dy/dx = -MU_x/MU_y$ just records that the $y$ is given up (moves down) as $x$ goes up.

In one line: MRS = how many $y$ one $x$ is worth to you = the ratio of marginal utilities. (The full implicit-differentiation derivation is in the Bonus: Slopes tab.)

2

Compute it, then read it out loud

General move
Running example $u = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$

1. Compute $MU_x$ and $MU_y$ (power rule, other variable frozen).

$MU_x = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}$, $\;MU_y = \tfrac12 x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}$

2. Divide; cancel common factors. Use $\dfrac{x^{-1/2}}{x^{1/2}} = x^{-1}$ and $\dfrac{y^{1/2}}{y^{-1/2}} = y$.

$\text{MRS} = \dfrac{\tfrac12 x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}}{\tfrac12 x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}} = \dfrac{y}{x}$

3. Plug in the bundle.

At $(4,9)$: $\text{MRS} = 9/4 = 2.25$.  At $(9,4)$: $\text{MRS} = 4/9 \approx 0.44$.

4. Say it in words, with units: “willing to give up ___ $y$ for one more $x$.”

At $(4,9)$: “with only 4 tacos but 9 hours of streaming, I'd give up 2.25 hours for one more taco.” At $(9,4)$: “tacos are plentiful now; I'd only give up 0.44 hours.”

Interpretation checklist

Big MRS (steep curve): $x$ is scarce relative to $y$, so $x$ is precious — you would pay a lot of $y$ for it.
Small MRS (flat curve): you already have plenty of $x$; one more is barely worth any $y$.
MRS = 1: at this bundle you value the two goods one-for-one at the margin.

Units matter: MRS$_{xy}$ is measured in units of $y$ per unit of $x$ (hours per taco here). Swapping the goods gives $\text{MRS}_{yx} = 1/\text{MRS}_{xy}$.

A. Cobb–Douglas — the balanced shopper

Concept
$$u(x,y) = x^{\alpha}y^{\beta} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{MRS} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{y}{x}$$

Picture: tacos and streaming. You always want some of each. The MRS is a ratio of quantities scaled by the ratio of exponents: it blows up as $x \to 0$ (the first taco is priceless) and collapses as $y \to 0$, so the curves hug both axes without ever touching them.

1

Marginal utilities by the power rule with the other variable frozen:

$$MU_x = \alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{\beta}, \qquad MU_y = \beta x^{\alpha}y^{\beta-1}$$

2

Divide. Group the constants, the $x$'s and the $y$'s separately:

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{x^{\alpha}}\cdot\frac{y^{\beta}}{y^{\beta-1}} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot x^{-1}\cdot y^{1} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{y}{x}$$

3

Read it. Only the ratio $\alpha/\beta$ survives. $u = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$, $u = xy$ and $u = x^{3}y^{3}$ all have $\text{MRS} = y/x$; $u = x^{2}y$ has $\text{MRS} = 2y/x$. Whether the exponents add to 1 is irrelevant.

Interpret the values

$\alpha/\beta$ is the relative weight on $x$: with $\alpha/\beta = 2$ you value $x$ twice as much as $y$ when you hold equal amounts ($y/x = 1 \Rightarrow \text{MRS} = 2$).

$y/x$ is the scarcity adjustment: holding lots of $y$ and little $x$ makes $x$ precious. That is why the MRS keeps falling as you slide right — diminishing MRS is automatic for Cobb–Douglas.

B. Quasilinear — the settled appetite

Concept
$$u(x,y) = v(x) + y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{MRS} = \frac{v'(x)}{1} = v'(x)$$

Picture: coffee ($x$) and “money left for everything else” ($y$). Utility is linear in $y$ and curved in $x$. The MRS depends on $x$ alone: how much of everything-else you would trade for one more coffee depends on how many coffees you already have, not on how rich you are. Graphically every indifference curve is the same curve shifted straight up.

1

Rewrite the root as a power: $u = 20x^{1/2} + y$.

2

Marginal utilities. In $x$: $MU_x = 20\cdot\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{x}}$. In $y$: the $20\sqrt{x}$ term is a constant, so $MU_y = 1$.

3

Divide: $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{10/\sqrt{x}}{1} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{x}}$. No $y$ anywhere.

4

Evaluate: at $x = 1$, MRS $= 10$; at $x = 4$, MRS $= 5$; at $x = 25$, MRS $= 2$. Diminishing in $x$, and identical whether $y = 5$ or $y = 50$.

Interpret the values

$\text{MRS} = a/(2\sqrt{x})$ is in dollars of everything-else per coffee when $y$ is money. So at $x = 4$ with $a = 20$, “the fifth coffee is worth about \$5 to me.” That is a willingness-to-pay statement, which is exactly why quasilinear utility is the workhorse for consumer surplus later.

Larger $a$ = stronger taste for coffee = steeper curves everywhere. The MRS never depends on $y$: rich or poor, the fifth coffee is worth the same to you.

C. Perfect substitutes — a constant exchange rate

Concept
$$u(x,y) = ax + by \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad MU_x = a,\quad MU_y = b,\quad \text{MRS} = \frac{a}{b}\ \ (\text{constant})$$

Picture: two brands of bottled water, where a Brand A bottle ($x$) holds $a$ cups and a Brand B bottle ($y$) holds $b$ cups. You only care about total cups, so you will always swap at the fixed rate of $a/b$ B-bottles per A-bottle, no matter how many you hold. Indifference curves are parallel straight lines of slope $-a/b$. There is no diminishing MRS.

D. Perfect complements — the kink

Concept
$$u(x,y) = \min(ax,\ by) \qquad\text{kink line: } ax = by \ \Longleftrightarrow\ y = \frac{a}{b}x$$

Picture: smoothies. Each fruit pack ($x$) is enough for $a$ smoothies, each yogurt tub ($y$) for $b$ smoothies, and a smoothie needs both. Utility is the number of complete smoothies. Extra fruit beyond what your yogurt can match adds nothing, so the curves are L-shaped with corners on the kink line $ax = by$.

1

Split into regions. If $2x < 3y$ (too little fruit, the vertical arm), $u = 2x$: $MU_x = 2$, $MU_y = 0$, so $\text{MRS} = 2/0$ — infinite: you would give up any amount of surplus yogurt for one more fruit pack.

2

If $2x > 3y$ (too little yogurt, the flat arm), $u = 3y$: $MU_x = 0$, $MU_y = 3$, so $\text{MRS} = 0/3 = 0$: extra fruit is worthless to you.

3

At the kink $2x = 3y$ the two arms meet at a corner; there is no single tangent, so the MRS is undefined. Check with numbers: $u(6,4) = \min(12,12) = 12$; $u(9,4) = \min(18,12) = 12$ (three extra packs, zero gain); $u(6,7) = \min(12,21) = 12$.

4

Why it matters: no calculus will find the optimum next lesson — the rule will be “insist on no waste, $ax = by$, then let the budget set the scale.”

The weights $a, b$ only set the recipe ratio: the kink line $y = (a/b)x$ says “for every fruit pack you want $a/b$ tubs.” With $a = 2, b = 3$ that is $2/3$ of a tub per pack. Bonus: monotonic transformations — same consumer, different labels

Optional. Nothing in tabs 5–6 or the next sheet needs this. It answers one exam-style question — “do these two utility functions describe the same preferences?” — and explains why the MRS is the meaningful object while utility numbers and marginal utilities are not.

Because utility is ordinal (tab 2), any relabeling that keeps the order describes the same preferences. Formally: if $f$ is a strictly increasing function, then $v = f(u)$ represents exactly the same preferences as $u$. The indifference curves are the same curves; only the numbers written on them change.

$$v(x,y) = f\big(u(x,y)\big),\quad f' > 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \text{same indifference curves, same MRS}$$

Allowed (strictly increasing $f$)

$2u$, $\;u + 5$, $\;u^2$ (for $u > 0$), $\;\ln u$, $\;\sqrt{u}$, $\;e^{u}$. Anything with $f'(u) > 0$.

Not allowed

$-u$ (flips the ranking), $\;u^2$ when $u$ can be negative, $\;\cos u$ (not monotone). These change which bundle is preferred.

2

Why the MRS is invariant (and MU is not)

0

The plan. Compute the marginal utilities of $v = f(u)$ with the chain rule, then divide. The common factor $f'(u)$ cancels, leaving the original MRS.

1

Chain rule on each partial. $v = f(u(x,y))$, so

$$MV_x = f'(u)\cdot MU_x, \qquad MV_y = f'(u)\cdot MU_y$$

Each marginal utility is rescaled by the same positive number $f'(u)$ — so the size of MU is not meaningful on its own.

2

Divide.

$$\text{MRS}_v = \frac{MV_x}{MV_y} = \frac{f'(u)\,MU_x}{f'(u)\,MU_y} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \text{MRS}_u$$

3

Conclusion. The MRS — the slope of the indifference curve — is a property of the preferences, not of the particular utility function chosen to describe them. That is why the MRS (and next lesson the tangency condition) is the meaningful object. $\blacksquare$

  • $u = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$: $MU_x = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}$, $\;MU_y = \tfrac12 x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}$, $\;\text{MRS} = y/x$.
  • $v = u^2 = xy$: $MV_x = y$, $\;MV_y = x$, $\;\text{MRS} = y/x$.  (Here $f' = 2u$.)
  • $w = \ln u = \tfrac12\ln x + \tfrac12\ln y$: $MW_x = \dfrac{1}{2x}$, $\;MW_y = \dfrac{1}{2y}$, $\;\text{MRS} = \dfrac{1/(2x)}{1/(2y)} = \dfrac{y}{x}$.  (Here $f' = 1/u$.)
  • At $(4,9)$: $MU_x = 0.75$, $MV_x = 9$, $MW_x = 0.125$ — wildly different sizes. MRS $= 2.25$ in all three.

Practical payoff: before differentiating a messy Cobb–Douglas, take logs. $\ln u = \alpha\ln x + \beta\ln y$ gives $MU_x = \alpha/x$, $MU_y = \beta/y$ and $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{\alpha/x}{\beta/y} = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}$ in one line. Same answer, no fractional exponents.

The budget constraint: what you can have

With income $m$ and prices $p_x, p_y$ (taken as given — the consumer is a price taker), the budget set is every bundle you can afford, $p_x x + p_y y \le m$. The budget line is its outer edge, where you spend everything:

$$p_x x + p_y y = m$$
Running example
  • Tacos $p_x = \$2$, streaming $p_y = \$4$ per hour, income $m = \$40$:   $2x + 4y = 40$.
2

Drawing it: intercepts first, always

The fastest and most reliable way to draw a budget line is to find where it hits each axis. The intercepts are “spend it all on one good”:

General move
Running example $2x + 4y = 40$

1. $x$-intercept. Set $y = 0$: $\;x = \dfrac{m}{p_x}$. “All income on $x$.”

$x = 40/2 = 20$ tacos. Mark $(20, 0)$.

2. $y$-intercept. Set $x = 0$: $\;y = \dfrac{m}{p_y}$. “All income on $y$.”

$y = 40/4 = 10$ hours. Mark $(0, 10)$.

3. Connect the two dots with a straight line. Done — it is linear, so two points fix it.

Line from $(0,10)$ to $(20,0)$.

4. Read the slope as rise over run between the intercepts: $\dfrac{-\,m/p_y}{m/p_x} = -\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}$. The $m$ cancels — the slope is set by prices only.

$\dfrac{-10}{20} = -\dfrac12 = -\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}$. Giving up one taco frees \$2, which buys $\tfrac12$ hour.

Slope-intercept form (solve for $y$):

$$y = \frac{m}{p_y} - \frac{p_x}{p_y}\,x$$

Intercept $m/p_y$, slope $-p_x/p_y$. The slope is the market's exchange rate: the number of $y$ the market hands you for one $x$. (The Bonus tab gets this same slope from implicit differentiation.)

Interpret the values

Intercepts are purchasing power in each good: $m/p_x = 20$ says your income is worth 20 tacos. Slope $p_x/p_y = 1/2$ is the opportunity cost of a taco measured in streaming hours: half an hour. Compare with the MRS, which is how many hours a taco is worth to you — tab 7.

Kinked budgets (preview)

Sometimes the price you face changes partway along the line. Two classics:

Quantity discount

Two slopes on one graph

You now own both ingredients. Put them on the same axes and each bundle on the budget line carries two exchange rates:

What you want — MRS

$$\text{MRS} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y}$$

How many $y$ you would willingly give up for one more $x$. Changes from bundle to bundle (for bowed curves).

What you can have — price ratio

$$\frac{p_x}{p_y}$$

How many $y$ the market actually takes from you for one more $x$. The same everywhere on a straight budget line.

The whole intuition of consumer choice is the comparison between these two numbers at the bundle you are currently holding.

2

The comparison, in words

Running example
  • $u = \sqrt{xy}$ so $\text{MRS} = y/x$;   budget $2x + 4y = 40$ so $p_x/p_y = 1/2$.
1

Bundle $(4, 8)$ — on the line? $2(4) + 4(8) = 40$ ✓. Here $\text{MRS} = 8/4 = 2$: you would give up 2 hours for one more taco. The market only asks 0.5 hour. A taco is a bargain by your own valuation — buy more tacos (move right along the line).

2

Bundle $(16, 2)$ — $2(16) + 4(2) = 40$ ✓. Now $\text{MRS} = 2/16 = 0.125$: one more taco is worth only 0.125 hour to you, but it costs 0.5 hour. Tacos are overpriced by your valuation — buy fewer tacos, more streaming (move left).

3

Bundle $(10, 5)$ — $2(10) + 4(5) = 40$ ✓. $\text{MRS} = 5/10 = 0.5 = p_x/p_y$. Your valuation and the market's price agree. No trade in either direction can help you — stop here.

The rule you will use next lesson (interior, bowed curves):

$$\text{MRS} > \frac{p_x}{p_y}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{buy more } x \qquad \text{MRS} < \frac{p_x}{p_y}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{buy less } x \qquad \text{MRS} = \frac{p_x}{p_y}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{optimal}$$

In one line: trade while your heart and the price tag disagree; stop when they match. Because the MRS falls as you buy more $x$ (diminishing MRS), the gap closes on its own and there is exactly one stopping point.

Bonus: the slope of any level curve

Optional but worth ten minutes. One formula gives the slope of an indifference curve and the slope of the budget line, because both are level curves of some function. Only the power rule and one idea — “the total change is zero along a level curve” — are needed.

The implicit-differentiation formula

0

The plan. A level curve is a set where a function $F(x,y)$ is held at a constant $k$. Moving along it, the total change in $F$ is zero. Write that total change in terms of the two partials, then solve for $dy/dx$.

1

Total differential. For a small move $(dx, dy)$, the change in $F$ is the sum of the change from each variable:

$$dF = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,dy$$

2

Stay on the level curve: $F = k$ is constant, so $dF = 0$:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\,dx + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,dy = 0$$

3

Solve for the slope. Move the first term across and divide by $\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,dx$:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dy}{dx}\bigg|_{F = k} = -\,\frac{\partial F/\partial x}{\partial F/\partial y}\;}$$

This is the implicit function theorem in its two-variable form. It says: you never need to solve $F(x,y) = k$ for $y$ to get the slope — two partial derivatives and a minus sign do it.

Application 1 — the indifference curve ($F = u$)

General
Example $u = x^{1/2}y^{1/2}$, level $u = 6$

Level curve: $u(x,y) = k$.

$x^{1/2}y^{1/2} = 6$, i.e. $xy = 36$.

Partials: $\partial u/\partial x = MU_x$, $\;\partial u/\partial y = MU_y$.

$MU_x = \tfrac12 x^{-1/2}y^{1/2}$, $\;MU_y = \tfrac12 x^{1/2}y^{-1/2}$

Formula: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y} = -\text{MRS}$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{y}{x}$. At $(4,9)$: slope $= -2.25$.

Cross-check by solving explicitly when you can and differentiating normally.

$y = 36/x = 36x^{-1}$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -36x^{-2} = -\dfrac{36}{x^2}$. At $x = 4$: $-36/16 = -2.25$. ✓ Same, because $y/x = (36/x)/x = 36/x^2$.

So the MRS formula is not a separate fact to memorize — it is the implicit-slope formula applied to $u$. The explicit route ($y = 36/x$) only works when you can solve for $y$; the implicit route always works.

Application 2 — the budget line ($F = $ spending)

The budget line is also a level curve: spending $g(x,y) = p_x x + p_y y$ held at the level $m$. Run the identical machine.

General
Example $2x + 4y = 40$

Level curve: $g(x,y) = p_x x + p_y y = m$.

$g = 2x + 4y$, level $40$.

Partials: $\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x} = p_x$, $\;\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y} = p_y$ (each is linear, so the derivative is just the coefficient).

$\partial g/\partial x = 2$, $\;\partial g/\partial y = 4$.

Formula: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{\partial g/\partial x}{\partial g/\partial y} = -\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}$.

$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{2}{4} = -\dfrac12$.

Cross-check with slope-intercept form.

$y = 10 - \tfrac12 x \Rightarrow dy/dx = -\tfrac12$. ✓ And rise-over-run between the intercepts $(0,10),(20,0)$: $-10/20 = -\tfrac12$. ✓

Same formula, both slopes:

$$\underbrace{\frac{dy}{dx}\bigg|_{u=k} = -\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}}_{\text{indifference curve}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\frac{dy}{dx}\bigg|_{g=m} = -\frac{p_x}{p_y}}_{\text{budget line}}$$

Why the slope of the budget line is constant and the MRS is not: the partials of the linear function $g$ are constants ($p_x$, $p_y$), while the partials of a bowed $u$ depend on where you stand. Next lesson's tangency condition is literally “set the two implicit slopes equal.”

Your turn (bonus practice)

Given
  • Level curve $x^2 y = 48$ (an indifference curve of $u = x^2 y$). Find $dy/dx$ at $(4, 3)$ two ways.

Implicit: $\partial F/\partial x = 2xy$, $\;\partial F/\partial y = x^2$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{2xy}{x^2} = -\dfrac{2y}{x}$. At $(4,3)$: $-\dfrac{6}{4} = -1.5$.

Explicit: $y = 48x^{-2}$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -96x^{-3}$. At $x = 4$: $-96/64 = -1.5$. ✓

So $\text{MRS} = 2y/x = 1.5$: at $(4,3)$ this consumer would give up 1.5 units of $y$ for one more $x$.

Practice

Nine problems, each broken into lettered parts — one move per part, with its own check. Write the givens first, then work a part, then check it before moving on. The last part of each problem is always the interpretation: say the number out loud in units.

1

Cobb–Douglas from scratch. $u(x,y) = x^{1/3}y^{2/3}$.

aFind $MU_x$. (Freeze $y$; power rule on $x^{1/3}$.)

$MU_x = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3}y^{2/3}$.

bFind $MU_y$.

$MU_y = \tfrac23 x^{1/3}y^{-1/3}$.

cForm the MRS and simplify it all the way. (Expect the exponent ratio times $y/x$.)

$\text{MRS} = \dfrac{\tfrac13 x^{-2/3}y^{2/3}}{\tfrac23 x^{1/3}y^{-1/3}} = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot x^{-1}\cdot y = \dfrac{y}{2x}$.  Shortcut: $\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\dfrac{y}{x} = \dfrac{1/3}{2/3}\dfrac{y}{x}$.

dEvaluate the MRS at $(2, 8)$ and at $(1, 16)$.

At $(2,8)$: $\text{MRS} = 8/4 = 2$.  At $(1,16)$: $16/2 = 8$.

eInterpret both numbers in a sentence each, with units, and say which bundle sits on the steeper part of its curve.

At $(2,8)$ the consumer would give up 2 units of $y$ for one more unit of $x$. At $(1,16)$, with $x$ even scarcer, they would give up 8 units of $y$ — the curve is much steeper there. (Both have $u = 4$, so they are on the same curve; this is diminishing MRS.)

2

Diminishing MRS along one curve. $u(x,y) = x^{2}y$ (exponents do not sum to 1 — that is fine).

aFind the MRS as a function of $x$ and $y$.

$MU_x = 2xy$, $MU_y = x^2$, so $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{2xy}{x^2} = \dfrac{2y}{x}$. (Exponent ratio $\alpha/\beta = 2/1$.)

bThe bundle $(4,3)$ gives $u = 48$. Find the $y$ on the same indifference curve when $x = 2$ and when $x = 8$.

Curve: $x^2 y = 48 \Rightarrow y = 48/x^2$. At $x = 2$: $y = 12$. At $x = 8$: $y = 48/64 = 0.75$.

cCompute the MRS at $(2,12)$, $(4,3)$ and $(8, 0.75)$.

$(2,12)$: $2(12)/2 = 12$.  $(4,3)$: $2(3)/4 = 1.5$.  $(8,0.75)$: $2(0.75)/8 = 0.1875$.

dWhat pattern do the three numbers show, and which preference assumption is it the signature of?

The MRS falls ($12 \to 1.5 \to 0.19$) as we move right along the curve: diminishing MRS, the signature of convexity (the curve bows toward the origin). With lots of $x$ already, one more $x$ is worth very little $y$.

3

Quasilinear. $u(x,y) = 8\sqrt{x} + y$, with $y$ measured in dollars of other spending.

aFind $MU_x$ and $MU_y$. (Write $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ first.)

$MU_x = 8\cdot\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt{x}}$;   $MU_y = 1$.

bFind the MRS. Which variable is missing from it?

$\text{MRS} = \dfrac{4/\sqrt{x}}{1} = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt{x}}$. No $y$ appears.

cEvaluate at $x = 4$ and $x = 16$.

$x = 4$: $4/2 = 2$.  $x = 16$: $4/4 = 1$.

dIs the MRS at $(16, 100)$ different from the MRS at $(16, 3)$? What does that say about the shape of the indifference map?

No — both are $1$. Moving straight up (more $y$, same $x$) never changes the slope, so every indifference curve is the same shape shifted vertically. Rich or poor, the 17th unit of $x$ is worth \$1 of other spending to this consumer.

4

Perfect substitutes. $u(x,y) = 5x + 2y$.

aFind $MU_x$, $MU_y$ and the MRS.

$MU_x = 5$, $MU_y = 2$, $\text{MRS} = 5/2 = 2.5$ — the same at every bundle.

bDraw the indifference curve $u = 20$: give both intercepts and the slope.

$5x + 2y = 20$: $x$-intercept $20/5 = 4$, $y$-intercept $20/2 = 10$. A straight line from $(0,10)$ to $(4,0)$ with slope $-10/4 = -2.5 = -\text{MRS}$.

cInterpret $\text{MRS} = 2.5$ in a sentence. Is there diminishing MRS here?

“One unit of $x$ is always worth exactly 2.5 units of $y$ to me.” No diminishing MRS — the curves are straight, so the exchange rate never changes no matter how much $x$ you hold.

5

Perfect complements. $u(x,y) = \min(x, 2y)$ — e.g. $x$ = hot dogs, $y$ = packs of two buns.

aWrite the kink line as $y = (\ldots)x$.

No waste when $x = 2y$, i.e. $y = \tfrac12 x$. (One pack of two buns per two hot dogs.)

bCompute $u(6, 2)$. How many units of which good are wasted?

$u = \min(6, 4) = 4$. Two packs make 4 buns, so 4 hot dogs are usable and 2 hot dogs are wasted.

cWhat is the MRS at $(6,2)$? (Which arm of the L are you on?)

Here $x > 2y$, so $u = 2y$: $MU_x = 0$, $MU_y = 2$, $\text{MRS} = 0$. You are on the flat arm: an extra hot dog is worth nothing to you.

dGive the no-waste bundle on the same indifference curve as $(6,2)$.

The kink of the $u = 4$ curve: $x = 4$, $y = 2$ (check $\min(4, 4) = 4$). Same utility with two fewer hot dogs.

6

Budget line drill. $p_x = 3$, $p_y = 5$, $m = 60$.

aWrite the budget line and find both intercepts.

$3x + 5y = 60$. $x$-intercept $m/p_x = 20$; $y$-intercept $m/p_y = 12$. Draw the line from $(0,12)$ to $(20,0)$.

bFind the slope two ways (rise over run between intercepts; $-p_x/p_y$) and interpret it.

$-12/20 = -0.6 = -3/5$. Buying one more $x$ costs \$3, which is $3/5$ of a unit of $y$: the opportunity cost of $x$ is 0.6 units of $y$.

cIncome doubles to $m = 120$. New intercepts and slope? Describe the shift.

Intercepts $40$ and $24$; slope still $-3/5$. A parallel shift outward.

dBack to $m = 60$, but $p_x$ doubles to 6. New intercepts and slope? Describe the change.

$x$-intercept $60/6 = 10$; $y$-intercept unchanged at $12$; slope $-6/5 = -1.2$ (steeper). The line pivots inward around $(0,12)$.

eWith the original budget, is $(10, 6)$ affordable? Is $(8, 8)$?

$(10,6)$: $30 + 30 = 60$ — exactly on the line, affordable. $(8,8)$: $24 + 40 = 64 > 60$ — not affordable.

7

Same preferences? $u = xy$, $\;v = x^2y^2 + 7$, $\;w = \ln x + \ln y$, $\;z = -xy$.

aFind $MU_x$ for $u$, $v$ and $w$. Are they equal?

$u$: $y$.  $v$: $2xy^2$.  $w$: $1/x$. Not equal — marginal utility is not invariant.

bFind the MRS for each of $u$, $v$, $w$.

$u$: $y/x$.  $v$: $\dfrac{2xy^2}{2x^2y} = y/x$.  $w$: $\dfrac{1/x}{1/y} = y/x$. All identical.

cWhich of the four functions represent the same preferences as $u$? Name the transformation $f$ in each case.

$v = u^2 + 7$ ($f(u) = u^2 + 7$, increasing for $u > 0$) ✓.  $w = \ln u$ ($f = \ln$, increasing) ✓.  $z = -u$ is decreasing, so it reverses every ranking: not the same preferences.

Want vs. can (no solving!). $u = xy$; prices $p_x = 1$, $p_y = 2$; income $m = 24$.

aWrite the budget line and the price ratio $p_x/p_y$.

$x + 2y = 24$; $\;p_x/p_y = 1/2 = 0.5$. (Intercepts $24$ and $12$.)

bCheck that $(4, 10)$ is on the line, find the MRS there, and say which way the consumer should move.

$4 + 20 = 24$ ✓. $\text{MRS} = y/x = 10/4 = 2.5 > 0.5$. A unit of $x$ is worth 2.5 $y$ to them but costs only 0.5 $y$: buy more $x$ (slide right).

cSame for $(20, 2)$.

$20 + 4 = 24$ ✓. $\text{MRS} = 2/20 = 0.1 < 0.5$. $x$ is now worth less to them than the market charges: buy less $x$, more $y$ (slide left).

dSame for $(12, 6)$. What is special about it?

$12 + 12 = 24$ ✓. $\text{MRS} = 6/12 = 0.5 = p_x/p_y$. The consumer's exchange rate equals the market's: no profitable move in either direction. (Next lesson you will solve for this point directly.)

9

Log trick and the two slopes. $u = x^{2/5}y^{3/5}$; budget $3x + 3y = 60$.

aTake logs of $u$ and use the result to write the MRS in one line.

$\ln u = \tfrac25\ln x + \tfrac35\ln y$, so $MU_x = \dfrac{2}{5x}$, $MU_y = \dfrac{3}{5y}$ and $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{2/(5x)}{3/(5y)} = \dfrac{2}{3}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}$. (Log is a monotonic transformation, so the MRS is unchanged.)

bEvaluate the MRS at $(8, 12)$.

$\dfrac23\cdot\dfrac{12}{8} = \dfrac23\cdot\dfrac32 = 1$.

cUse the implicit-slope formula on the budget line to get its slope; check $(8,12)$ is on the line; compare the two slopes.

$g = 3x + 3y$: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{\partial g/\partial x}{\partial g/\partial y} = -\dfrac33 = -1$, so $p_x/p_y = 1$. On the line: $24 + 36 = 60$ ✓. At $(8,12)$, $\text{MRS} = 1 = p_x/p_y$: the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line there — what you want and what you can have agree.

Cheat Sheet

The two ingredients

$$\underbrace{\text{MRS}_{xy} = \frac{MU_x}{MU_y} = \left|\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{u=k}}_{\text{what you want: your exchange rate}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{p_x x + p_y y = m,\quad \text{slope} = -\frac{p_x}{p_y}}_{\text{what you can have: the market's exchange rate}}$$

$\text{MRS} > p_x/p_y$: $x$ is a bargain to you → buy more $x$. $\;\text{MRS} < p_x/p_y$: buy less $x$. $\;\text{MRS} = p_x/p_y$: stop (next lesson's tangency condition).

◆

The four utility functions

Cobb–Douglas — smooth, bowed

$$u = x^{\alpha}y^{\beta},\qquad \text{MRS} = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{y}{x}$$

$MU_x = \alpha x^{\alpha-1}y^{\beta}$, $MU_y = \beta x^{\alpha}y^{\beta-1}$. Only the ratio $\alpha/\beta$ matters; exponents need not sum to 1. Curves never touch the axes. Log trick: $\ln u = \alpha\ln x + \beta\ln y \Rightarrow MU_x = \alpha/x,\ MU_y = \beta/y$.

Quasilinear — bowed, vertical copies

$$u = v(x) + y,\qquad \text{MRS} = v'(x)$$

$MU_y = 1$; MRS depends on $x$ only, so the slope is the same all the way up any vertical line. Common: $v = a\sqrt{x} \Rightarrow \text{MRS} = \dfrac{a}{2\sqrt{x}}$; $v = a\ln x \Rightarrow \text{MRS} = \dfrac{a}{x}$. When $y$ is dollars, MRS = willingness to pay for one more $x$.

Perfect substitutes — straight lines

$$u = ax + by,\qquad \text{MRS} = \frac{a}{b}\ (\text{constant})$$

$MU_x = a$, $MU_y = b$. Parallel lines of slope $-a/b$; the $u = k$ line has intercepts $k/a$ and $k/b$. No diminishing MRS, no diminishing MU.

Perfect complements — L-shapes

$$u = \min(ax, by),\qquad \text{kink: } y = \frac{a}{b}x$$

MRS $= \infty$ on the vertical arm ($ax < by$), $0$ on the flat arm ($ax > by$), undefined at the kink. Extra units past the kink are wasted. The bigger weight belongs to the good you need less of.

Procedures

MRS in four moves

  1. $MU_x$: freeze $y$, power rule on $x$.
  2. $MU_y$: freeze $x$, power rule on $y$.
  3. Divide and cancel: $\dfrac{x^{\alpha-1}}{x^{\alpha}} = \dfrac{1}{x}$, $\;\dfrac{y^{\beta}}{y^{\beta-1}} = y$.
  4. Plug in the bundle and say it: “willing to give up ___ $y$ for one more $x$.”

Cobb–Douglas shortcut: $\text{MRS} = \dfrac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x}$. Quasilinear: $\text{MRS} = v'(x)$.

Budget line in three moves

  1. $x$-intercept: $\dfrac{m}{p_x}$  (all income on $x$).
  2. $y$-intercept: $\dfrac{m}{p_y}$  (all income on $y$).
  3. Connect the dots. Slope $= -\dfrac{m/p_y}{m/p_x} = -\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}$.

$$y = \frac{m}{p_y} - \frac{p_x}{p_y}\,x$$

Shifters (exogenous) vs. movements (endogenous)

  • $m \uparrow$ — parallel shift out; slope unchanged.
  • $p_x \uparrow$ — pivot in around the $y$-intercept; steeper.
  • $p_y \uparrow$ — pivot in around the $x$-intercept; flatter.
  • $m, p_x, p_y$ all scale together — nothing moves.
  • Choosing a bundle — a movement along the line, not a shift.

Slope of any level curve (bonus)

$$\frac{dy}{dx}\bigg|_{F=k} = -\frac{\partial F/\partial x}{\partial F/\partial y}$$

  • $F = u$  ⇒  slope $= -\dfrac{MU_x}{MU_y}$  (indifference curve).
  • $F = p_x x + p_y y$  ⇒  slope $= -\dfrac{p_x}{p_y}$  (budget line).
!

Facts that survive relabeling — and ones that don't (bonus)

ObjectInvariant under $v = f(u)$, $f' > 0$?Why
Ranking of bundles / indifference curvesYesThat is the definition of a monotonic transformation.
MRSYes$MV_x/MV_y = f'(u)MU_x / f'(u)MU_y$ — the $f'$ cancels.
Utility numbersNoOrdinal: $u = 6$ vs $3$ means “better”, never “twice as good”.
Marginal utility (size)NoRescaled by $f'(u)$. Even “diminishing MU” can flip ($\sqrt{xy}$ vs $xy$).
Diminishing MRS (convexity)YesIt is a statement about the curve's shape, which does not move.

Common traps. (1) Reporting $\text{MRS}_{yx}$ when asked for $\text{MRS}_{xy}$ — it is $MU_x$ on top. (2) Forgetting the exponent ratio in Cobb–Douglas: $x^2y$ has MRS $2y/x$, not $y/x$. (3) Treating the constant in quasilinear as a power: $20\sqrt{x}$ differentiates to $10/\sqrt{x}$, not $20/\sqrt{x}$. (4) Reading the budget slope as $p_y/p_x$ — it is $p_x/p_y$ (price of the horizontal good on top). (5) Comparing $MU_x$ to $MU_y$ directly; the meaningful comparison next lesson is $MU_x/p_x$ vs $MU_y/p_y$.
