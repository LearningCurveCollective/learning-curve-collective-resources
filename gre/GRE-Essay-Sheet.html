1

What separates each score

The jump to a 6 is not mainly grammar. It is engagement with complexity. Each rung adds one thing the rung below it lacks; find your current level, then climb one habit at a time.

3
Limited
One idea, restated

A position is visible but thinly supported. A single example does all the work, and sentence-level mistakes occasionally reverse the argument.

4
Adequate
Clear position, adequate support

States a stance and backs it with relevant reasons. Competent, but the reasons stay general, or two body paragraphs make the same point.

5
Strong
Distinct reasons, named examples, clean prose

Two or three different reasons, each with a concrete named example. Sentences are controlled and the logic is easy to follow.

6
Outstanding
Finds the flaw in the wording, grants the best objection, still holds

Everything a 5 does, plus a paragraph that concedes the strongest opposing point and rebuts it. It holds two sides in tension and defends a position anyway.

The lever for every prompt: attack the overclaim. Words like surely, always, never, only, every, cannot promise too much. Concede the partial truth, then show why the absolute version fails. Everything else on this sheet is a way of executing that one move well.

2

How the 30 minutes are spent

Most people lose this task by writing at minute one. The planning minutes are what produce two distinct body reasons instead of the same idea twice.

Running example · prompt #12Leaders who admit uncertainty lose the confidence of those they lead.
0:00 – 3:00
DiagnoseCircle the single word doing too much work. Decide your position, and make it a reframe, not a flat 'no.'
Deliverable: one sentence you could say out loud.
e.g. There is no hedge at all — 'lose' is stated as a law. Position: followers lose confidence in leaders who are uncertain about their values, not those who are honest about the facts.
3:00 – 7:00
BrainstormTwo genuinely different reasons. One real, named example for each (from the Example Bank). Then the strongest objection someone could raise.
Deliverable: 2 reasons + 2 examples + 1 objection.
e.g. R1: admitted uncertainty invites the information a leader needs → Lincoln's cabinet. R2: false certainty destroys confidence when it fails → Challenger. Objection: in a crisis, visible doubt spreads → Churchill, May 1940.
7:00 – 9:00
OutlineFive paragraphs, one line each. If two body lines say the same thing, fix it here; it is unfixable at minute 22.
Deliverable: five one-line stubs.
e.g. 1 concede + 'lose' + reframe • 2 Lincoln: doubt that gathers information • 3 Challenger: certainty that ignored it • 4 grant Churchill, then: public posture is not private process • 5 convict 'lose', lift to what confidence is actually built on.
9:00 – 26:00
WriteRoughly 500–600 words. Do not stop to polish sentences; the outline is already doing the thinking.
Deliverable: a complete essay with a real conclusion.
e.g. If you are still choosing an example at minute 15, you skipped Brainstorm. Go back to the stub, not to the bank.
26:00 – 30:00
ProofTwo passes: one for errors that break meaning (a missing not), one asking of every example: is this actually true?
Deliverable: nothing a grader can catch you on.
e.g. Pass 1 catches 'leaders who admit uncertainty keep confidence' where you meant lose. Pass 2 catches 'Challenger, 1987' — it was January 1986.

The one rule that outranks all the others: every example you name must be one you could defend as factually true. A fabricated or half-remembered example does more damage than a vague one: a human rater who catches one wrong fact stops trusting everything else on the page, and “persuasive examples” is the criterion that separates a 5 from a 6. If you cannot recall the detail with confidence, choose a different example. The Example Bank exists so that you never have to.

3

The example bar

Rank your options and take the highest one available. Specific, named examples are the single biggest driver of a 5–6 score, and the single biggest source of avoidable losses.

  • 1
    Named historical case with a date or actor you can defend. Strongest. Everything in the Example Bank is this.
  • 2
    Well-documented general phenomenon (a professional practice, a technology's known effects). Workable, but name something inside it.
  • 3
    Personal experience. Acceptable once, never as the whole essay.
  • 4
    Invented hypothetical. Last resort; it proves nothing.
  • 5
    A fact you half-remember. Do not use it. This category has cost more points than any other.
4

Three questions your outline must survive

1. Can I state the overclaiming word out loud?

If not, you have no target and the essay will drift into general commentary about the topic.

2. Are my two body reasons separable?

Write both topic sentences side by side. If one is a restatement of the other, you have one reason and need a second.

3. Would my concession worry me?

If the objection you plan to grant is one you can dismiss in a clause, it is not the strongest case. Find the one that has teeth.

The one-line test before you submit

"Did I concede the other side and beat it?" If yes, you are in 6 territory. If no, you have written a solid 5; go add the concession.

1

The eight moves

This is the shape every Issue essay can take. Eight moves, in this order, and they mold to any prompt ETS can hand you: the claim changes, the examples change, the eight moves do not. Three of the moves carry a short note for the prompt types where they read slightly differently. Execute all eight well and the essay is a 6. The sentence skeleton under each move is a starting shape, not a script. Once you have read the eight moves, scroll down to see them applied to a real prompt, one sentence per move; then open the Model Essays tab to see three full essays built the same way.

1Concede the intuition before you turn

Open by granting why the claim feels right. A reader who sees you understand the appeal of the position trusts your rejection of it. Starting with 'I disagree' signals you never considered it.

Skeleton: Anyone who has [seen the everyday case] will feel the force of this. [One sentence granting the appeal.] But…
2Name the single overclaiming word

Nearly every Issue prompt overreaches through one word: surely, not, any, every, all, only, must, never, cannot, always. Find it and quote it. Your essay is not against the idea; it is against the word that turned a tendency into a law.

Skeleton: The claim is not that some X do Y; it is that [word] X does. That word turns [an observation] into [a rule], and the rule does not survive [the cases].
Type note. On a two-view prompt (Type 4) there is usually no single word; the overclaim is the assumed opposition between the views. Name that instead.
3Thesis = a reframe, not a flat 'no'

Do not swap one absolute for another. Shift the axis: X does not do A, it does B — under condition C. The conditional clause is where the complexity points live.

Skeleton: X does not [what the claim says]; it [what it actually does] — for [who / when / under what condition].
4Body 1 — first reason, one real example, developed

One example, named and specific, carried for the whole paragraph. Three shallow examples are worth less than one you actually explain. State the insight explicitly rather than leaving the reader to infer it.

Skeleton: The clearest counterexample is [name]. [Three or four sentences of what actually happened, with a date.] The result was not [what the claim predicts] but [what happened]. Judged by [criterion], [insight in one sentence].
5Body 2 — a genuinely different reason

The most common 4.0 failure is two body paragraphs making the same argument with different examples. Test it: if Body 2's topic sentence could be swapped into Body 1 without anyone noticing, it is not a second reason.

Skeleton: A second reason to distrust the claim is [a different kind of reason: causation runs the other way / the fear has a bad record / the mechanism it assumes does not exist]. [Named example.] [Insight.]
6Concession — grant the strongest opposing case

Its own paragraph. Steelman it: give the version of the objection that would worry you if you were arguing the other side. A weak strawman concession reads as a dodge and grades like one.

Skeleton: The strongest version of the claim is not [the weak version] but [the strong version]. [Named example where the claim really holds.] This is a real cost, not a trivial one.
7Rebuttal — relocate the blame

The harm is in the manner, not the thing. The same tool, contest, or rule produces opposite results in different hands, so the claim has blamed the object for a failure that belongs to its use.

Skeleton: But notice what that concession shows. It was the same [X], [in the same period], that produced [the good result] and [the bad one]. The [X] did not choose; [the users / the evidence / the conditions] did.
Type note. On recommendation and policy prompts (Types 2 and 6) this reads as: the rule works under circumstance A and fails under circumstance B, and the prompt has ignored the difference.
8Conclusion — convict the word, then lift

Return to the word from Principle 2 and pin the flaw on it by name: 'This is why every gives the claim away.' Then close one level up, on what the reader should now do or judge differently, not on a summary of your paragraphs.

Skeleton: This is why [word] gives the claim away. What the history supports is a narrower and more useful claim: [reframe restated]. The question that shapes my position, then, is not [the prompt's question] but [the better question].
Type note. On Type 4, convict the false opposition rather than a word.
2

One prompt through the architecture

Running example · prompt #12Leaders who admit uncertainty lose the confidence of those they lead.

Each line is what that move produces for this prompt — roughly the topic sentence of the paragraph, before it is developed.

1Concede the intuition

A leader who says 'I am not sure' in a crisis hands the room a reason to look elsewhere; anyone who has served under a wavering manager knows the feeling.

2Name the word

But the claim does not say leaders can lose confidence this way; it says they lose it — no hedge, a law of human nature.

3Reframe thesis

Followers lose confidence in leaders who are uncertain about their values, not in leaders who are honest about the facts; the claim has confused the two.

4Body 1

Lincoln filled his 1861 cabinet with the rivals who had run against him and let them argue in front of him; his openness about what he did not know is what gave him the information to decide, and his authority grew with every decision it produced.

5Body 2 (different reason)

Certainty, meanwhile, is what actually destroys confidence when it fails: Challenger's managers overrode engineers' stated doubts about the O-rings in January 1986, and the certainty they projected became, after the fact, the reason nobody trusted the program's word.

6Concession

Churchill in May 1940 projected a certainty he arguably did not have, and it held a country together; in a crisis, visible doubt can spread faster than the facts.

7Rebuttal — relocate

But Churchill's certainty was public posture over a private process that had just spent three days arguing the question in cabinet; the confidence came from the process, and the posture only reported it.

8Convict the word, lift

This is why lose gives the claim away: what followers track is not how sure a leader sounds but whether the leader's decisions keep turning out to be grounded — and admitted uncertainty is how they get grounded.

Notice what the eight lines already are: an outline that could be written up in seventeen minutes. Bodies 1 and 2 are not the same reason (one is about doubt gathering information, the other about certainty ignoring it); the concession is a real case, not a strawman; and the rebuttal relocates rather than denies. If your own eight lines pass those checks, the essay is a 6 before a full sentence is written.

1

Read the instruction line, not just the claim

Every Issue prompt has two parts: a claim, and an instruction paragraph telling you what to do with it. ETS uses six instruction variants, and essays that ignore the instruction are capped near a 4 no matter how polished. The blueprint is the same for all six; what changes is where the overclaim hides and how the five paragraphs are aimed. Each Prompt Bank entry is tagged with its type.

1

Agree / disagree — ways it might or might not hold

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the statement and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true and explain how these considerations shape your position.
What it is really asking

The most common variant and the closest to the plain blueprint. The instruction is a gift: it explicitly asks for the conditions under which the claim holds and fails, which is exactly what a reframe thesis provides.

Where the overclaim hides

Usually a plain absolute: surely, always, every, never, the more…the less.

How the blueprint bends

No bending needed. Body 1 and Body 2 supply the conditions under which the claim fails; the concession supplies the condition under which it holds. Say 'shape my position' back to the grader in the conclusion.

The common failure

Treating 'might or might not' as permission to fence-sit. You still commit; the conditions are the argument, not a substitute for one.

2

Recommendation — specific circumstances

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the recommendation and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, describe specific circumstances in which adopting the recommendation would or would not be advantageous and explain how these examples shape your position.
What it is really asking

The prompt proposes an action ('should'), and the grader is looking for cases: where does this work, where does it backfire. Examples are not decoration here; the instruction literally asks for them.

Where the overclaim hides

In the scope of the rule: every, all, only, wherever, always require.

How the blueprint bends

Body 1 = a circumstance where the recommendation works, with a named case. Body 2 = a circumstance where it fails, with a named case. The concession then argues about which circumstance is more common or more costly. Thesis becomes: 'adopt it when C, not otherwise.'

The common failure

Arguing about the goal instead of the rule. 'Is breadth good?' is not the question; 'does requiring it produce it?' is.

3

Claim — address the most compelling challenges

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the claim. In developing and supporting your position, be sure to address the most compelling reasons and/or examples that could be used to challenge your position.
What it is really asking

This variant makes the concession paragraph mandatory and grades its quality. A weak objection here is not a missed opportunity; it is a failure to follow the instruction.

Where the overclaim hides

A plain absolute, often only, greatest, any, true.

How the blueprint bends

The concession grows. Give it the best-documented counterexample to your view, developed as fully as a body paragraph, and then rebut with a distinction, not a dismissal. Some 6.0 essays on this type run two concessions.

The common failure

Conceding something trivial. If you can dismiss the objection in a clause, the grader will notice you chose it for that reason.

4

Two views — which aligns more closely with yours

Write a response in which you discuss which view more closely aligns with your own position and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should address both of the views presented.
What it is really asking

There is no single claim to attack. The flaw to find is the assumed opposition between the views: usually they describe two phases of one process, or two halves of one truth. You still pick one.

Where the overclaim hides

There often is none. Look instead for the shared word (primarily, most effective) that both sides overclaim.

How the blueprint bends

Intro: name both views, then a thesis that dissolves the opposition and leans one way. Body 1: your side's best case. Body 2: show the other side's best case is actually your side's case in disguise (Rosa Parks: an individual act that only worked collectively). Concession: the other view's strongest case on its own terms.

The common failure

Never saying which view you hold. 'Both have merit' is a 3.5. The instruction says 'which view.'

5

Claim and reason

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the claim and the reason on which that claim is based.
What it is really asking

Two assertions, not one: a claim and a reason offered in its support. You must address both, and they can come apart. The best essays grant part of the claim and demolish the reason, or the reverse.

Where the overclaim hides

Nearly always in the reason: never, always, only, cannot. The claim is usually hedged; the reason is usually a law.

How the blueprint bends

Body 1 addresses the claim; Body 2 addresses the reason. This is the one type where the two bodies are structurally guaranteed to be different reasons. Conclusion: 'the claim survives in a weaker form; the reason does not survive at all.'

The common failure

Answering the claim only. Graders are instructed to look for both; an essay that never mentions the reason has answered half the question and is capped near a 4.

6

Policy — consider the consequences

Write a response in which you discuss your views on the policy and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider the possible consequences of implementing the policy and explain how these consequences shape your position.
What it is really asking

The prompt is a rule someone might actually enact. The instruction wants you to run it forward: who does it affect, what does it produce, what does it cost. Consequences, not principles, are the currency.

Where the overclaim hides

In the scope: no restrictions, wherever, stop, all.

How the blueprint bends

Each body paragraph traces one consequence to a named historical case where something like this policy was tried or refused. The concession is the policy's best intended consequence, granted honestly. The rebuttal shows the unintended one that the scope word lets in.

The common failure

Arguing that the policy is right or wrong in the abstract. 'Consider the consequences' means show them happening.

Kodak and the digital camera

CoreTechnology & ProgressRisk & CautionBusiness & Economics
The facts to get right
  • Steve Sasson, a Kodak engineer, built the first working digital camera prototype in 1975 — inside Kodak.
  • Kodak's film business was so profitable that the company shelved the technology for years rather than cannibalize it.
  • Kodak filed for bankruptcy protection in January 2012.
What it proves

Caution in defense of an existing success can be the riskiest choice available.

What it complicates

Kodak did not lack the invention; it lacked the will to use it. The failure is institutional, not technical — so it complicates any claim that innovation alone is what matters.

Fits prompts about: risk vs. caution; technology and adaptation; why institutions fail; whether success breeds complacency

Gutenberg's press and the Reformation

Technology & ProgressArt & CultureIndividual & Society
The facts to get right
  • Johannes Gutenberg printed with movable type in Mainz around 1450.
  • Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses (1517) circulated across German-speaking Europe within weeks because they could be printed cheaply.
  • The same presses printed pamphlets on every side of the dispute, including vicious polemic.
What it proves

A communication technology can widen who participates in public argument.

What it complicates

The press also flooded Europe with propaganda and misinformation. It changed the volume of discourse, not its quality — the quality depended on the users.

Fits prompts about: technology and public discourse; whether new media help or harm; individual ideas vs. institutions

The Luddites

Technology & ProgressIndividual & SocietyBusiness & Economics
The facts to get right
  • Between 1811 and 1816, English textile workers broke mechanized knitting frames and looms, mainly in Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire and Lancashire.
  • Parliament made frame-breaking a capital offense in 1812.
  • Their objection was not to machines as such but to the collapse of wages and craft standards that mechanization brought without any transition for the workers.
What it proves

Technological change has real losers, and societies rarely plan for them.

What it complicates

The Luddites are remembered as anti-progress, but the machines did win and living standards did eventually rise. Use them to argue about the distribution of a technology's costs, not its existence.

Fits prompts about: automation and work; whether progress should be slowed; who bears the cost of change

Wikipedia versus Britannica

Technology & ProgressEducation & LearningScience & KnowledgeBusiness & Economics
The facts to get right
  • Wikipedia launched in January 2001, written and edited by unpaid volunteers.
  • Encyclopædia Britannica ended its print edition in 2012 after 244 years.
  • A 2005 study in Nature comparing science entries found the two had broadly similar error rates.
What it proves

Distributed, open collaboration can rival expert institutions.

What it complicates

Wikipedia works because of rules, citations and constant correction — it is not the absence of authority but a different kind. It complicates claims that either experts or crowds are always better.

Fits prompts about: expertise vs. collective knowledge; technology and learning; whether open systems can be trusted

Frances Kelsey and thalidomide

CoreRisk & CautionScience & KnowledgeLeadership & GovernmentEthics & Justice
The facts to get right
  • In 1960, as a new reviewer at the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Kelsey refused to approve thalidomide, a sedative already sold in Europe, because the safety evidence was inadequate.
  • She held out through months of pressure from the manufacturer; meanwhile the drug caused severe birth defects in thousands of children abroad.
  • Congress passed the Kefauver–Harris Amendments in 1962, requiring proof of safety and efficacy before approval.
What it proves

Caution can be the contribution — it can save lives and build institutions.

What it complicates

Kelsey was not timid; she was demanding evidence. The lesson is about calibration to evidence, not about caution as a temperament.

Fits prompts about: risk vs. caution; expertise and authority; individual conscience inside an institution; whether laws lead or follow

Apollo 8

CoreRisk & CautionLeadership & Government
The facts to get right
  • In August 1968 NASA decided to send Apollo 8 into lunar orbit that December, years ahead of the original schedule, partly to stay ahead of the Soviet program.
  • It was the first crewed flight of the Saturn V rocket and the first time humans left Earth orbit.
  • The crew of Borman, Lovell and Anders orbited the Moon ten times on 24 December 1968 and returned safely.
What it proves

Some breakthroughs require accepting a danger that a cautious actor would refuse.

What it complicates

The risk was taken after the Apollo 1 fire (January 1967) had forced a redesign of the spacecraft. It was bold and evidence-based — which is why it belongs in a concession, followed by Challenger.

Fits prompts about: risk and reward; decisive leadership; competition as a driver; learning from failure

The Challenger launch

CoreRisk & CautionLeadership & GovernmentScience & Knowledge
The facts to get right
  • On 28 January 1986 the Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew.
  • The night before, engineers at Morton Thiokol, including Roger Boisjoly, argued against launching because the O-ring seals had not been tested at the forecast cold temperatures.
  • Management overrode the objection. The Rogers Commission identified the O-ring failure and the flawed decision process.
What it proves

Risk accepted over the evidence is not courage; it is a decision failure.

What it complicates

Paired with Apollo 8: same appetite for risk, opposite relationship to the evidence, opposite outcome. This is the cleanest 'relocate the blame' pair in the bank.

Fits prompts about: risk vs. caution; leadership and dissent; institutional pressure; learning from failure

Ignaz Semmelweis and handwashing

Science & KnowledgeEducation & LearningRisk & Caution
The facts to get right
  • In 1847 at Vienna General Hospital, Semmelweis showed that requiring doctors to wash their hands in chlorinated lime cut deaths from childbed fever dramatically on his ward.
  • The medical establishment largely rejected the finding; he could not explain the mechanism, and the implication that doctors carried disease was insulting to them.
  • He died in 1865, before germ theory vindicated him.
What it proves

Established expertise can block a correct new idea for decades.

What it complicates

He was also a poor advocate — combative, and without a theory for his data. Complicates claims that a right idea wins on its own.

Fits prompts about: questioning authority; how new knowledge spreads; whether evidence is enough; expertise as an obstacle

Socrates on writing (Plato's Phaedrus)

CoreTechnology & ProgressEducation & Learning
The facts to get right
  • In Plato's dialogue Phaedrus (fourth century BCE), Socrates retells a myth in which the Egyptian king Thamus warns that writing will weaken memory, because people will rely on external marks instead of remembering.
  • He also complains that a written text cannot answer questions or defend itself.
  • The complaint survives, of course, because someone wrote it down.
What it proves

Every new tool for thinking has been accused of making people stop thinking.

What it complicates

The fear was not baseless: memory practices did change. But the change relocated skill rather than destroying it. This is the anchor example for any technology-and-mind prompt.

Fits prompts about: technology and thinking; whether tools weaken skills; recurring fears about new media; what education should preserve

The Flynn effect

Education & LearningScience & Knowledge
The facts to get right
  • James Flynn documented that raw scores on IQ tests rose across the industrialized world through the twentieth century, roughly three points per decade.
  • Tests had to be renormed repeatedly to keep the average at 100.
  • The gains are largest on abstract, pattern-based reasoning and are usually attributed to schooling, nutrition and a more abstract, symbol-rich environment.
What it proves

Populations can become measurably better at certain kinds of thinking as their environment changes.

What it complicates

Gains are uneven across skills, and some studies find the effect stalling or reversing in a few countries since the 1990s. Use it carefully: it shows plasticity, not unlimited progress.

Fits prompts about: whether people are getting smarter or dumber; the effect of education and technology on the mind

Malala Yousafzai

The facts to get right
  • In October 2012 Malala, then fifteen, was shot by the Taliban in Pakistan's Swat Valley for publicly advocating girls' education.
  • She survived and continued the campaign; in 2014 she became the youngest Nobel Peace Prize laureate.

Lincoln's cabinet of rivals

CoreLeadership & GovernmentIndividual & Society
The facts to get right
  • In 1861 Lincoln appointed his three main rivals for the Republican nomination — William Seward, Salmon Chase and Edward Bates — to his cabinet.
  • Seward initially assumed he would run the administration; Chase openly maneuvered for the 1864 nomination while serving as Treasury Secretary.
  • Lincoln kept them, used their disagreements, and made the final decisions himself. Doris Kearns Goodwin's Team of Rivals (2005) is the standard account.
What it proves

Strong leaders seek out disagreement rather than loyalty.

What it complicates

The arrangement worked because Lincoln decided; consultation without a decider is just a committee. Good for both sides of a consult-vs.-decide prompt.

Fits prompts about: leadership and dissent; whether leaders should consult; the value of disagreement

Churchill and the War Cabinet, May 1940

Leadership & GovernmentRisk & Caution
The facts to get right
  • Between 26 and 28 May 1940, with the British army trapped at Dunkirk, the War Cabinet debated whether to explore peace terms with Germany through Italy.
  • Foreign Secretary Lord Halifax argued for at least finding out what terms were available; Churchill argued that opening talks would itself be fatal.
  • Churchill won the argument, partly by appealing to the wider cabinet, and Britain fought on.
What it proves

Some decisions have to be made against advice, on judgment, before the facts are complete.

What it complicates

Churchill did not act alone: he argued it through a cabinet over three days. Decisiveness and consultation were not opposites here.

Fits prompts about: decisive leadership; acting under uncertainty; risk in politics

Cincinnatus

Leadership & GovernmentIndividual & Society
The facts to get right
  • According to Roman tradition, in 458 BCE the farmer Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus was made dictator to rescue a besieged Roman army.
  • He defeated the enemy, resigned the dictatorship within about two weeks, and returned to his farm.
  • Washington was compared to him for resigning his commission in 1783; the city of Cincinnati is named for the society of officers that honored the parallel.
What it proves

The best measure of a leader is what they do with power they could keep.

What it complicates

The story is semi-legendary; say 'according to Roman tradition.' It is strongest as a statement of an ideal, not as a documented event.

Fits prompts about: the ethics of power; leadership and restraint; what makes a leader admirable

Nelson Mandela and reconciliation

CoreLeadership & GovernmentIndividual & SocietyEthics & Justice
The facts to get right
  • Mandela spent twenty-seven years in prison (1962–1990) for his resistance to apartheid.
  • Released in 1990, he negotiated with the government that had jailed him and became South Africa's first democratically elected president in 1994.

Restraint toward former enemies can be a greater achievement than victory over them.

The TRC was criticized by victims' families for trading justice for stability — a real concession point for any prompt about forgiveness or compromise.

Rosa Parks and the Montgomery Bus Boycott

CoreIndividual & SocietyLeadership & GovernmentEthics & Justice
The facts to get right
  • On 1 December 1955 Parks, a seamstress and NAACP secretary, refused to give up her bus seat to a white passenger in Montgomery, Alabama, and was arrested.
  • The boycott that followed lasted 381 days and was organized by a network of churches and civic groups, with the 26-year-old Martin Luther King Jr. as spokesman.
  • In Browder v. Gayle (1956) the courts held Montgomery's bus segregation unconstitutional.
What it proves

One person's refusal can start a movement.

What it complicates

Parks was a trained activist, not a tired stranger, and the boycott succeeded through institutions and the courts. This is the best two-sided example for individual-vs.-collective prompts.

Fits prompts about: individual vs. society; whether laws should follow custom; how change actually happens

The Milgram obedience experiments

CoreIndividual & SocietyScience & KnowledgeEducation & LearningEthics & Justice
The facts to get right
  • Between 1961 and 1963 at Yale, Stanley Milgram had volunteers deliver what they believed were escalating electric shocks to a 'learner' whenever an experimenter told them to continue.
  • In the baseline version, about 65 percent of participants went to the maximum 450-volt setting.
  • Public reaction to the deception and distress involved helped drive the ethics review rules (institutional review boards) now standard in research on human subjects.
What it proves

Obedience to authority is the human default, not the exception.

What it complicates

The study itself is the counterexample to 'science should have no limits': its harms produced the ethics rules. It serves both sides of research-ethics prompts.

Fits prompts about: questioning authority; conformity; ethics of research; what education must teach

Rachel Carson's Silent Spring

Science & KnowledgeIndividual & SocietyArt & CultureEthics & Justice
The facts to get right
  • Carson, a marine biologist, published Silent Spring in 1962, documenting the effects of DDT and other pesticides on birds and ecosystems.
  • The chemical industry attacked the book and her credentials.

the overclaiming word the reframe thesis
Type 1 · Agree / disagree · Prompt #18

Every new communication technology weakens the quality of public discourse.

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the statement and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true and explain how these considerations shape your position.

IntroPrinciples 1–3: concede the intuition, quote the word, reframe with a condition.

Anyone who has watched a serious argument dissolve into a shouting match in an online comment thread will feel the force of this claim. Each new medium seems to arrive with a promise of enlightenment and deliver a flood of noise, and it is tempting to conclude that the pattern is a law. But the claim is not that some technologies degrade discourse, or that they degrade it at first; it is that every one does, permanently. That word turns an observation about the early life of new media into a rule about their whole life, and the rule does not survive the history of the technologies we already know. New communication technologies change the volume of public discourse and the membership of the public that conducts it; whether the quality falls depends on what the newly admitted participants do with their reach, not on the technology itself.

Body 1Principle 4: one named example, developed for the whole paragraph, insight stated explicitly.

The clearest counterexample is the printing press. Before Gutenberg's movable type spread through Europe after 1450, public argument about the deepest questions of the age was conducted in Latin among a clerical elite. When Martin Luther posted his Ninety-five Theses in 1517, the press carried them across German-speaking Europe within weeks, and it carried the replies, the replies to the replies, and the vernacular Bibles that let ordinary readers check the arguments for themselves. The result was not a degraded discourse but an enormously enlarged one. A debate that had been closed to almost everyone became the central public question of a continent for a century. Judged by how many people could participate and how much of the disagreement was aired in the open, the press did not weaken the quality of public discourse; it created public discourse in something like the modern sense.

Body 2Principle 5: a genuinely different reason — not another counterexample, but the pattern of the fear itself.

A second reason to distrust the claim is that it has been made about every medium and has a poor record. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing itself will weaken memory, because people will trust external marks instead of remembering, and that a written text cannot answer questions or defend itself. The complaint is not foolish; memory practices really did change once texts could be consulted. But the change relocated a skill rather than destroying it, and the whole enterprise of philosophy, science and law that followed was built on the technology Socrates feared. We know his objection only because Plato wrote it down. When an argument has been raised against writing, print, and the telegraph and has been wrong about their long-run effect each time, the burden is on the person raising it again to explain why this medium is different, and the claim as stated does not try.

ConcessionPrinciple 6: the strongest opposing case, given honestly and in its own paragraph.

The strongest version of the claim is not about the long run but about what a new medium rewards while it is new. The same presses that printed Luther printed pamphlets of remarkable viciousness, forged documents, and lurid accounts of miracles and monsters, and they printed them faster than anyone could refute them. A technology that lowers the cost of speaking lowers it for liars as well as for reformers, and in the interval before norms and institutions of correction develop, the liars may have the advantage. Anyone who has lived through the first decade of a new platform has seen this, and it is a real cost, not a trivial one.

RebuttalPrinciple 7: relocate the blame to the manner of use.

But notice what that concession actually shows. It was the same press, in the same decade, that carried the Reformation's arguments and the forgeries. The technology did not choose between them; its users did, and the readers who learned to demand sources and compare accounts did the rest. The quality of the discourse was determined by the participants' habits, and those habits improved as the medium aged. The claim has blamed the instrument for a failure that belongs to how the instrument was used, which is why it cannot explain how the same instrument produced the opposite result.

ConclusionPrinciple 8: convict the word by name, then lift one level.

This is why every gives the claim away. A statement about all communication technologies, past and future, has to be true of the press and of writing, and it is not. What the history supports is a narrower and more useful claim: each new medium enlarges the public and, for a while, rewards whoever is quickest rather than whoever is right. The question that shapes my position, then, is not whether to fear new media but how quickly a society can build the habits of verification that every previous medium eventually required. That is a task for the participants, and it is one that has been completed before.

Why this is a 6, not a 5. A 5 would have made the same case with the press alone and then added a token objection. This essay earns the extra point by (a) naming the word and making the whole essay about its scope, (b) using a second reason that is structurally different from the first — the history of the complaint rather than another counterexample, (c) giving the concession real teeth (the press printed forgeries, and it printed them first), and (d) rebutting with a relocation rather than a denial: same press, opposite uses. The conclusion answers the instruction's phrase 'shape your position' directly.

Type 5 · Claim & reason · Prompt #09

Laws should reflect the customs of the majority, because a law that most citizens do not accept cannot be enforced.

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the claim and the reason on which that claim is based.

IntroPrinciples 1–3, with the claim-and-reason move: separate the two assertions and announce which will survive.

There is an obvious wisdom in this. A law that a population regards as absurd is a law that police will not enforce, juries will not convict under, and citizens will not obey, and a state that passes many such laws teaches its people that law in general is optional. The claim, then, deserves a hearing. But the prompt rests the claim on a reason, and the reason is stated as a fact about the world: a law that most citizens do not accept cannot be enforced. That is not a preference but an empirical assertion, and it is false; laws have been enforced against majority custom many times, and laws have created the very customs they were later said to reflect. Law should follow the consent of the governed rather than the customs of the majority, and where a custom is the practice of some citizens at the expense of others, the law's task is to change the custom, not to ratify it.

Body 1Principle 4: one example developed — and aimed at the reason, since the reason is the weaker assertion.

Begin with the reason, because it is the load-bearing part. In Montgomery, Alabama, in 1955, the custom of the majority of the enfranchised population was bus segregation. It was written into city ordinances and defended as the settled preference of the community. When Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on 1 December 1955 and was arrested, the black population of the city organized a boycott that lasted 381 days, and in Browder v. Gayle (1956) the federal courts held the segregation ordinances unconstitutional. The new rule was enforced, over the objection of the local majority, by federal authority and by the daily insistence of the people who had been excluded. Enforcement was difficult and it was resisted, but 'difficult' is not 'impossible,' and within a generation the once-settled custom was regarded even in Montgomery as an embarrassment. The reason's cannot is simply contradicted by the record.

Body 2Principle 5: a different reason — laws create custom — aimed now at the claim.

A second problem is that the claim gets the direction of causation backward: it assumes custom exists first and law arrives to reflect it, when law often manufactures the custom. In 1960 there was no American custom of demanding proof of safety before a drug was sold; the manufacturer of thalidomide expected routine approval and pressed the FDA reviewer, Frances Kelsey, for it. She refused because the evidence was inadequate, the drug went on to cause thousands of birth defects abroad, and in 1962 Congress passed the Kefauver–Harris Amendments requiring evidence of safety and efficacy before approval. Today the expectation that a medicine has been tested before it reaches a pharmacy is so deeply customary that most people cannot imagine its absence. The custom did not produce the law. The law, and one official's insistence on evidence, produced the custom.

ConcessionPrinciple 6: the strongest opposing case, on its own terms.

The claim's defenders have a real case, and it should be stated at full strength. A law with no consent behind it can be enforced only by force, and force at scale is expensive, corrupting and ultimately unsustainable. South Africa's apartheid pass laws were enforced for decades by an enormous apparatus of police, courts and prisons; Nelson Mandela spent twenty-seven years in that system. The laws did not fall because they were unenforceable in the short run but because a state built on enforcing them against the majority of its people could not be sustained, morally or practically, and by 1990 its own government was negotiating its end. If that is what the prompt means by 'cannot be enforced,' it has a point.

RebuttalPrinciple 7: relocate — the reason confused two different things.

But that case defeats the prompt's own claim, because apartheid law did reflect the customs of the majority of those permitted to vote. What it lacked was the consent of the people it governed. The concession shows that the prompt has confused majority custom with legitimacy: Montgomery's ordinances had the first and not the second, and the apartheid statutes had the first and not the second, and in both cases the law that eventually stood was the one that changed the custom. The enforcement problem the reason describes is real, but it belongs to laws without consent, not to laws that offend a majority's habits.

ConclusionPrinciple 8: convict the word, state what survives of the claim, then lift.

This is why cannot gives the reason away, and with it most of the claim. A law that most citizens dislike can be enforced, has been enforced, and has sometimes become the custom within a generation. What survives of the prompt is a weaker and truer version: law should not run so far ahead of consent that it can be kept only by force. That version is worth defending. It also implies the opposite of what the prompt concludes, because the clearest cases of law without consent are the cases where the majority's custom was the injury the law existed to protect.

Why this is a 6, not a 5. The essay answers both assertions, which is the whole test for this prompt type, and it does so with a structure that keeps the two bodies from collapsing into one: Body 1 attacks the reason with an enforcement case, Body 2 attacks the claim by reversing its causation. The concession is genuinely dangerous to the thesis (apartheid really was unsustainable), and the rebuttal turns it: the concession's own example had majority custom and failed anyway, so custom was never the variable. The conclusion states precisely what survives of the claim, which graders read as complexity rather than fence-sitting.

Type 2 · Recommendation · Prompt #08

Educators should teach students to question the ideas of authorities rather than to accept them.

Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the recommendation and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, describe specific circumstances in which adopting the recommendation would or would not be advantageous and explain how these examples shape your position.

IntroPrinciples 1–3. For a recommendation, the reframe names the circumstances: adopt it when C.

It is hard to argue against a recommendation that seems to be asking only for independent minds. Every generation can name an authority whose confident error cost lives, and a student taught to accept whatever is stated by someone with credentials is a student who will repeat those errors on request. Yet the recommendation is phrased as a choice: question rather than accept. The phrasing treats the two as alternatives, and in the circumstances where the choice actually matters, they are not. Educators should teach students to ask authorities for their reasons; that single habit produces questioning when the reasons are absent and acceptance when they are present, and it is the only version of the recommendation that works in both kinds of case.

Body 1Principle 4, and the instruction's 'specific circumstances': a case where questioning authority would have saved lives.

Consider first the circumstance in which the recommendation is plainly right. In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, working in the maternity clinic of Vienna General Hospital, showed that requiring doctors to wash their hands in chlorinated lime before examining patients cut deaths from childbed fever on his ward dramatically. The authorities of European medicine rejected the finding. He could not explain the mechanism, germ theory being decades away, and the implication that physicians themselves carried death from the dissecting room to the delivery bed was insulting to them. Women continued to die of a preventable infection for a generation because the profession accepted its own authority over a result it could see. A student who had been taught to ask 'what is the evidence?' rather than 'who is speaking?' would have sided with the data, and the recommendation's defenders can fairly say that such students were exactly what was missing.

Body 2Principle 5: a different reason — questioning must be taught because obedience is the default.

The recommendation is also right about something less obvious: questioning has to be taught, because accepting is what people do by default. Between 1961 and 1963 Stanley Milgram had ordinary volunteers at Yale deliver what they believed were escalating electric shocks to a stranger whenever a man in a lab coat told them to continue. About two-thirds went to the maximum setting. Nothing compelled them; the authority in the room had no power beyond calm insistence, and that was enough. If deference to authority is that strong in adults who have been given no reason to defer, then an education that does not deliberately build the habit of asking for reasons is an education that leaves the default in place. Here the recommendation is not merely acceptable but urgent.

ConcessionPrinciple 6: the circumstance where the recommendation fails — the instruction asks for it.

But now consider the other circumstance the instruction asks for. In 1960 Frances Kelsey, a reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration, refused to approve thalidomide, a sedative already sold across Europe, because the manufacturer's safety data were inadequate. The company spent months pressing her, questioning her judgment and her authority to delay a drug that other countries had accepted. Kelsey was the authority in that story, and she was right; the questioning came from the party with the commercial interest, and had it prevailed, the birth defects that followed the drug in Europe would have followed it in America. A student taught simply to question authorities rather than accept them has no way to tell this case from the Semmelweis case, and would have been on the manufacturer's side.

RebuttalPrinciple 7: relocate — the variable was never authority, it was evidence.

The two cases, taken together, show what the recommendation gets wrong. Semmelweis's opponents and thalidomide's manufacturer were doing the same thing: preferring their standing to the evidence in front of them. Semmelweis and Kelsey were also doing the same thing: insisting on the evidence regardless of who outranked whom. Authority was not the variable that separated the right side from the wrong side in either story. The reasons were. An education aimed at questioning authority as such teaches students to attack the wrong target; an education aimed at asking for reasons teaches them to find the right one in both circumstances.

ConclusionPrinciple 8: convict the phrase, then lift to what should be taught instead.

This is why rather than gives the recommendation away. Questioning and accepting are not the alternatives the educator must choose between; they are the two outcomes of a single habit, applied to authorities who can or cannot say why. Adopt the recommendation wherever a student is being asked to defer to a position instead of an argument, and reject it wherever it would teach a student to treat a well-supported expert as an adversary. What both circumstances shape is a narrower and more demanding goal than the prompt proposes: not students who question, but students who can tell when questioning is finished.

Why this is a 6, not a 5. Recommendation prompts are graded on whether the 'specific circumstances' actually appear, and here they are the skeleton: Body 1 is the circumstance where the recommendation works, the concession is the circumstance where it fails, and the rebuttal explains what distinguishes them. That last move is what a 5 lacks: a 5 would list a case for and a case against and call it balance. The 6 finds the single variable (reasons, not status) that predicts both cases, which is what 'insight' means in the rubric. Note also that Kelsey is used against the essay's own opening instinct — the concession has teeth because the essay's own bank example cuts the other way.
