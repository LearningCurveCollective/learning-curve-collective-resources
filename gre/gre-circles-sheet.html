Circle rules

A circle is the set of points a fixed distance $r$ from a center. That single number drives everything below: $r$ is the radius, $d = 2r$ is the diameter, and $n$ denotes a central angle in degrees. Answers are normally left in terms of $\pi$.

Rule 1Radius, diameter, chord:   $d = 2r$, and a diameter is the longest chord
Example Aa chord joins two points on the circle; a diameter is a chord through the center; a tangent touches the circle at one point
Example Ba circle with diameter $18$ has $r = 9$ — halve before using any formula
Every formula below is written in $r$. The GRE hands you a diameter constantly, so make "$r = $" its own written line.
Rule 2Circumference:   $C = 2\pi r = \pi d$
Example A$r = 7$ gives $C = 14\pi \approx 44$
Example Bbackwards: $C = 20\pi$ means $2\pi r = 20\pi$, so $r = 10$
Rule 3Area:   $A = \pi r^{2}$
Example A$r = 6$ gives $A = 36\pi$
Example Bbackwards: $A = 49\pi$ means $r^2 = 49$, so $r = 7$
Area grows with the square of the radius: double $r$ and the area quadruples. All circles are similar, so the similar-figures rule applies — lengths scale by $k$, areas by $k^2$.
Rule 4Arcs and central angles:   an arc's measure equals its central angle; a full circle is $360^\circ$
Example Aarcs take three letters — arc $ABC$ — because two points leave two possible paths, and the middle letter picks the route
Example Bin a regular hexagon inscribed in a circle, each side subtends $\dfrac{360^\circ}{6} = 60^\circ$, so an arc across two sides measures $120^\circ$
Rule 5Arc length:   $\text{arc} = \dfrac{n}{360} \cdot 2\pi r$
Example A$r = 12$, $n = 45$:  $\dfrac{45}{360} = \dfrac{1}{8}$ of $24\pi$, so the arc is $3\pi$
Example Ba semicircular arc ($n = 180$) has length $\pi r$ — half the circumference
Rule 6Sector area:   $\text{sector} = \dfrac{n}{360} \cdot \pi r^{2}$
Example A$r = 6$, $n = 60$:  $\dfrac{1}{6}$ of $36\pi$, so the sector is $6\pi$
Example Ba sector is bounded by two radii and an arc, so its perimeter is $2r + \text{arc}$
Rules 5 and 6 share the fraction $\dfrac{n}{360}$ and differ only in what it multiplies: circumference for a length, area for a region.
Rule 7Tangents:   a tangent is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency
Example Aradius $6$ and tangent segment $8$ from an external point $P$ give $OP = \sqrt{36+64} = 10$
Example Bthe converse holds too: a line perpendicular to a radius at its endpoint on the circle is tangent there
Rule 8Inscribed triangles:   a side that is a diameter forces a right angle at the opposite vertex
Example A$PQ$ a diameter with $PR = 6$, $QR = 8$ gives $\angle R = 90^\circ$, so $PQ = 10$, $r = 5$, area $25\pi$
Example Bthe converse: if an inscribed triangle is right, its hypotenuse must be a diameter
A polygon is inscribed in a circle when all its vertices lie on the circle; the circle is then circumscribed about it. Circles sharing a center are concentric.
Moves that are not allowed

The statements below look reasonable but are false, and each one generates a standard wrong answer. Keep them in mind.

Case 1not allowed:   using a diameter where the formula wants a radius
This is the single most common circle error. A diameter of $10$ gives area $25\pi$, not $100\pi$ — substituting $d$ for $r$ multiplies the area by $4$ and the circumference by $2$.
Case 2not allowed:   $\pi r^2 \neq (\pi r)^2$, and $\pi \neq 3$ exactly
Square the radius only. And when a comparison is close, $\pi \approx 3.14$ decides it: $\dfrac{2\pi r}{3}$ versus $2r$ comes down to whether $\pi > 3$, which the rough estimate would call a tie.
Case 3not allowed:   treating an arc's degree measure as a length
A $60^\circ$ arc is not $60$ units long. Degrees describe how much of the circle you traverse; length requires multiplying that fraction by $2\pi r$.
Case 4not allowed:   reading a three-letter arc as one piece
Arc $RST$ travels through $S$, so in an inscribed regular polygon it may cover two sides, not one. Trace the path and count the equal pieces before converting to degrees.
Case 5not allowed:   subtracting radii and then squaring
For concentric circles of radii $5$ and $3$, the ring's area is $25\pi - 9\pi = 16\pi$, not $\pi(5-3)^2 = 4\pi$. Compute each area, then subtract.
Case 6not allowed:   confusing inscribed with circumscribed
A circle inscribed in a square has diameter equal to the square's side. A square inscribed in a circle has diagonal equal to the diameter. Decide which figure is inside before matching any length.
