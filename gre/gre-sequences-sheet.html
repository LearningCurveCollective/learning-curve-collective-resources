Sequence rules

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers built by a rule; $a_n$ means the $n$th term. The GRE gives sequence rules in two forms, and translating between them is most of the work. In the rules below, $a_1$ is the first term, $d$ is a constant difference, and $r$ is a constant ratio.

Rule 1Two ways to define a sequence:   explicit (jump to any term) or recursive (build up)
Example Aexplicit: $a_n = 3n - 1$ gives $2, 5, 8, 11, \ldots$ — want $a_{100}$? Plug in: $3(100) - 1 = 299$
Example Brecursive: $a_1 = 2$, $a_n = a_{n-1} + 3$ gives the same list — but $a_{100}$ needs $a_{99}$ first
The GRE's favorite sequence move is handing you a recursive rule and asking about a far-away term — which forces you to identify the type and find the explicit form.
Rule 2Arithmetic sequences (add $d$ each step):   $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)\,d$
Example A$4, 9, 14, 19, \ldots$ has $a_1 = 4$, $d = 5$, so $a_n = 4 + 5(n-1) = 5n - 1$
Example B$a_{20} = 5(20) - 1 = 99$ — no need to list twenty terms
Example Cthe test for arithmetic: subtract neighbors — a constant difference confirms it. $7, 3, -1, -5$: every difference is $-4$ ✓
This is $y = mx + b$ in disguise: $d$ is the slope, $a_1$ is the starting point, and the sequence is a straight line sampled at $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$
Rule 3Geometric sequences (multiply by $r$ each step):   $a_n = a_1 \cdot r^{\,n-1}$
Example A$3, 6, 12, 24, \ldots$ has $a_1 = 3$, $r = 2$, so $a_n = 3 \cdot 2^{\,n-1}$
Example B$r$ can shrink or alternate: $48, 12, 3, \ldots$ has $r = \tfrac{1}{4}$;   $2, -6, 18, \ldots$ has $r = -3$
Example Cthe test for geometric: divide neighbors — a constant ratio confirms it. $4, 6, 9$: differences change, but the ratio is $\tfrac{3}{2}$ throughout ✓
The exponent is $n - 1$, not $n$: after $n - 1$ multiplications you're at term $n$. And alternating signs — like $2, -6, 18, -54$ — are a dead giveaway for geometric with a negative $r$: adding a fixed $d$ can never flip the sign back and forth, but multiplying by a negative does it every step. Geometric sequences are where the GRE re-tests your exponent rules. And you've already met one in the wild: compound interest is a geometric sequence in banking costume — the yearly balances $P, \; P(1+r), \; P(1+r)^2, \ldots$ multiply by the same ratio every step. (Adding those balances up would make a geometric series — that's annuity math, and the GRE stays out of it.)
Rule 4Counting terms (the fencepost rule):   count $= \dfrac{\text{last} - \text{first}}{d} + 1$
Example Amultiples of 4 from 16 to 96: $\dfrac{96 - 16}{4} + 1 = 21$ terms
Example Bintegers from 12 to 45 inclusive: $45 - 12 + 1 = 34$ (not 33)
Rule 5Evenly spaced sums:   sum $=$ count $\times \dfrac{\text{first} + \text{last}}{2}$,   and mean $=$ median $= \dfrac{\text{first} + \text{last}}{2}$
Example A$1 + 2 + \cdots + 100$: pair the ends ($1 + 100$, $2 + 99$, \ldots) — 50 pairs of 101, so $5{,}050$
Example Bsum of $2 + 4 + \cdots + 100$: 50 terms $\times \dfrac{2 + 100}{2} = 50 \times 51 = 2{,}550$
Moves that are not allowed

The statements below look reasonable but are false, and each one generates a standard wrong answer. Keep them in mind.

Case 1not allowed:   two terms do not determine the sequence
$2, 6, \ldots$ could be arithmetic ($+4$: next is 10) or geometric ($\times 3$: next is 18) or neither. Never assume the type — the problem must state it or give three or more terms.
Case 2not allowed:   $a_n \neq a_1 + n\,d$
The correct form is $a_1 + (n-1)\,d$: reaching term $n$ takes $n - 1$ steps, not $n$. The off-by-one version is a standard answer choice on every arithmetic question.
Case 3not allowed:   eyeballing far-away geometric terms
A huge power multiplying a tiny term can land anywhere: $5^{15} \cdot 5^{-23} = 5^{-8}$, a very small number despite the $5^{15}$. Convert to one power and compare exponents — never judge by appearance.
Case 4not allowed:   count $\neq$ last $-$ first
The integers from 12 to 45 number $34$, not $33$ — subtracting counts the gaps, and there's one more fencepost than gaps. Add the $+1$ every time, deliberately.
Case 5not allowed:   "sequences grow"
A geometric sequence with $0 < r < 1$ shrinks toward zero, and one with $r < 0$ alternates sign every term. $C_{n+1} = \tfrac{1}{5} C_n$ is a sequence in freefall — treat the ratio's size and sign as information, not decoration.

Worked examples & practice

Everything on the reference tab runs on one idea: a sequence is a starting value plus a repeated step. Adding the same step makes it arithmetic (a line). Multiplying by the same step makes it geometric (a power). Every GRE sequence question is asking you to name the step and count how many times it happened.

The walkthrough below takes the rules one at a time — each with a worked example, the trap it pairs with, and a ten-second check. Reading a rule isn't knowing it; the check is the point.
