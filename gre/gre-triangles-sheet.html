Triangle rules

Every triangle has three sides and three interior angles, and almost every GRE triangle question is one of these eight rules in costume. Throughout, $a$, $b$, and $c$ are side lengths, with $c$ the hypotenuse whenever a right angle is present.

Rule 1The angles sum to $180^\circ$:   and an exterior angle equals the two remote interior angles added together
Example Aangles of $45^\circ$ and $70^\circ$ force the third to be $180 - 45 - 70 = 65^\circ$
Example Bthe exterior angle at a vertex with remote interiors $50^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ measures $110^\circ$ — because it is $180 - 70$, and the third angle is $70^\circ$
Rule 2The triangle inequality:   each side is less than the sum of the other two, so $\;|a - b| < c < a + b$
Example Asides $4$, $7$, and $12$ are impossible: $4 + 7 = 11 < 12$
Example Bwith two sides $6$ and $10$, the third side must satisfy $4 < c < 16$
Rule 3Equal sides face equal angles:   isosceles $\Leftrightarrow$ two equal angles; equilateral $\Leftrightarrow$ three $60^\circ$ angles
Example Aif two angles measure $50^\circ$ each, the sides opposite them are equal, and the third angle is $80^\circ$
Example Bthe ordering carries over: the longest side always sits opposite the largest angle
Rule 4Pythagorean theorem (right triangles only):   $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$
Example Alegs $6$ and $8$ give $c = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$
Example Bworth memorizing: $3\text{-}4\text{-}5$, $\;5\text{-}12\text{-}13$, $\;8\text{-}15\text{-}17$, and every multiple ($6\text{-}8\text{-}10$, $9\text{-}12\text{-}15$, $\ldots$)
$c$ is always the hypotenuse — the side opposite the right angle, and always the longest.
Rule 5The $45^\circ\text{-}45^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangle:   sides in the ratio $\;1 : 1 : \sqrt{2}$
Example Alegs of $5$ give a hypotenuse of $5\sqrt{2}$
Example Bthis is half a square cut along its diagonal — so a square of side $s$ has diagonal $s\sqrt{2}$
Rule 6The $30^\circ\text{-}60^\circ\text{-}90^\circ$ triangle:   sides in the ratio $\;1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$
Example Aa hypotenuse of $12$ gives a short leg of $6$ and a long leg of $6\sqrt{3}$
Example Bthis is half an equilateral triangle cut down the middle — so an equilateral triangle of side $s$ has height $\dfrac{s\sqrt{3}}{2}$
Match sides to angles, not to position: the short leg ($x$) is opposite $30^\circ$, the long leg ($x\sqrt{3}$) is opposite $60^\circ$, and the hypotenuse ($2x$) is opposite the right angle.
Rule 7Area:   $A = \dfrac{1}{2}\,b\,h$, where $h$ is perpendicular to the base $b$
Example Abase $15$ and height $6$ give $A = \dfrac{(15)(6)}{2} = 45$
Example Bin a right triangle the two legs serve as base and height, so $A = \dfrac{(\text{leg})(\text{leg})}{2}$
Any side can be the base; the height is the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex, and for an obtuse triangle it can fall outside the triangle entirely.
Rule 8Similar triangles:   equal angles $\Rightarrow$ proportional sides, with a scale factor $k$
Example Aif $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$ with $AB = 6$, $AC = 4$, and $DE = 9$, then $k = \tfrac{9}{6} = 1.5$ and $DF = 6$
Example Blengths scale by $k$, but areas scale by $k^2$ — doubling every side quadruples the area
Two triangles are congruent (identical) if SSS, SAS, or ASA matches; they are similar (same shape, any size) as soon as two pairs of angles match.
Moves that are not allowed

The statements below look reasonable but are false, and each one generates a standard wrong answer. Keep them in mind.

Case 1not allowed:   trusting the picture
GRE figures are not drawn to scale unless the problem says so. An angle that looks like $90^\circ$ is only $90^\circ$ if it is marked or stated. Read the labels, never the drawing.
Case 2not allowed:   $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ in a triangle that isn't right
The Pythagorean theorem requires a right angle. In a triangle with sides $4$, $5$, and $6$, it simply fails: $16 + 25 \neq 36$.
Case 3not allowed:   matching special-triangle sides by position instead of by angle
In a $30\text{-}60\text{-}90$, the $\sqrt{3}$ belongs to the leg opposite $60^\circ$. Assigning it to the short leg produces answers off by a factor of $\sqrt{3}$ — and those values are waiting in the answer choices.
Case 4not allowed:   areas of similar figures scale by $k$
They scale by $k^2$. Triangles with sides in the ratio $2:3$ have areas in the ratio $4:9$, not $2:3$.
Case 5not allowed:   treating a side as the height
The height must be perpendicular to the base. Only in a right triangle do two sides happen to be perpendicular to each other; anywhere else, a slanted side is longer than the true height.
Case 6not allowed:   assuming two sides determine the third
Sides $6$ and $10$ leave the third side anywhere in $4 < c < 16$. Unless the triangle is specified as right or isosceles, a range is the most you can know.

Worked examples & practice

Everything on the reference tab runs on one idea: a triangle's angles and its sides carry the same information. Know the angles and you know which sides are longest, which are equal, and — for the two special triangles — their exact ratios. Most GRE triangle questions are just a translation between the two.

The walkthrough below takes the rules one at a time — each with a worked example, the trap it pairs with, and a ten-second check. Reading a rule isn't knowing it; the check is the point.
