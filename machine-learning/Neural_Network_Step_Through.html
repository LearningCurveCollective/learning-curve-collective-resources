Step Through the Forward Pass

The network: two inputs $(x_1, x_2)$, three hidden neurons $(h_1, h_2, h_3)$ with sigmoid activations, one sigmoid output $o$. Pick a point, then press Next Step to watch the numbers flow: each arrow multiplies by its weight, each circle adds up its arrivals plus its bias, then squashes through $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$.

The weights are fixed to make each hidden neuron a line detector (see the Geometry Lab tab for why these particular values).

Index convention: $w^{[1]}_{i,j}$ is the layer-1 weight from input $x_i$ into hidden neuron $h_j$ (read the subscripts as from, to); $w^{[2]}_j$ is the layer-2 weight from $h_j$ into the output; a first subscript of $0$ marks a bias (the phantom input pinned to $1$). Superscript = layer.

The gold-boxed labels under each circle are the biases — four extra trainable constants, one owned by each neuron, added to that neuron's sum before the sigmoid. They have no arrows in the diagram (the standard convention hides them), but you can picture each as a weight on a phantom input pinned to the constant $1$.

  • Hidden biases ($-0.5$, $-0.5$, $+3$) position each detector line: $h_1$'s bias of $-0.5$ is what puts its line at $x_1 = 0.5$ instead of through the origin. Weights set a line's orientation; the bias slides it into place.
  • Output bias ($-5$) sets the firing threshold: the vote-sum $2h_1{+}2h_2{+}2h_3$ must exceed $5$ before $u > 0$. Without it, $u$ would be positive everywhere and the network could never say “no.”

Are biases trained? Yes — all 13 parameters (9 weights + 4 biases) are updated by gradient descent together. A bias is just a weight whose input is always $1$, so its backprop chain is identical to a weight's except the final factor is $ imes\,1$ instead of $ imes\,x_1^{(i)}$. Weights learn orientations; biases learn positions and thresholds — simultaneously.

What to notice as you step: the hidden layer is three logistic regressions run in parallel on $(x_1,x_2)$, and the output is one more logistic regression whose inputs are the hidden outputs $(h_1,h_2,h_3)$ instead of the raw coordinates. Networks are stacked logistic regressions — the same building block, used four times.

Try all four presets and compare $o$. Inside the triangle (A) and far outside (B, C, D) all land near $o \approx 0.25$ — these hand-picked weights are mediocre. Soft sigmoid votes near the lines are wishy-washy, so the “all three detectors agree” signal never gets strong. The Geometry Lab tab has a sharpness slider that fixes exactly this.

What Each Hidden Neuron Sees

What we're building, from the ground up. The circles live in a triangular island with crosses all around — and one logistic regression is one line, which can't enclose anything. The plan: build the triangle out of lines, since a triangle is three lines plus the logic “inside all three.”

One hidden neuron = one line + a side-choice. Each neuron's pre-activation $z_j = w^{[1]}_{0,j} + w^{[1]}_{1,j}x_1 + w^{[1]}_{2,j}x_2$ is zero along a line; the sigmoid paints the whole plane in two shades — high on the neuron's chosen side, low on the other. The triangle is never drawn explicitly — it emerges as the set of points passing all three tests at once: three half-plane stencils overlapped, and the triangle is where all three are open. (It generalizes: 4 neurons → quadrilaterals, $n$ → any convex polygon; more layers → unions of pieces, hence any shape.)

What is $k$? The slider multiplies every hidden-layer weight and bias by the same factor, so each neuron computes $\sigma(k z_j)$ instead of $\sigma(z_j)$. The lines do not move — $kz_j = 0$ exactly where $z_j = 0$ — only the transition sharpens: $k$ rescales distance-from-the-line into confidence, turning mumbled votes into decisive ones. Sliding $k$ up is approaching better weights by hand, not training:

  • Training = gradient descent choosing the 13 parameters from data, each free to move independently — lines rotating, sliding, votes reweighting, threshold adjusting. Direction and destination determined by the loss.
  • The $k$ slider = moving 9 of those parameters by hand in one rigidly coordinated way (all scaled together), lines frozen by construction. No data consulted, no loss descended — a demonstration knob, not a learning process.

That said, training on this data would push in a $k$-like direction: with the line placements already good, the gradients' main discovery is “be more confident about these boundaries,” i.e. grow the weight magnitudes — while also fine-tuning positions and the output layer. The slider isolates that one direction so you can watch it.

Watch what sharpness does. At $k=1$ the map is a blur — every point scores $o\approx 0.25$, inside or out. By $k=8$ a crisp triangle emerges: votes snap toward 0 / 1, the AND becomes reliable, and $o$ separates the regions. The limit $k\to\infty$ turns each sigmoid into the step function, whose perfectly binary votes make the triangle detector exact.

One label flip to note: if a dataset labels inside-the-triangle points as class 0, our $o$ (an inside-detector, high inside) still works: predict class 0 when $o > 0.5$ — equivalently, negate the output layer's weights and bias. Sign conventions are free; the geometry is the substance.

Swap in Step Activations — and Classify Everything

Here is the payoff of the whole triangle story. Replace every sigmoid with the step function $f(z) = 1$ if $z > 0$, else $0$ — the infinite-sharpness limit. Votes are now exactly 0 or 1, so the inside-vs-outside logic becomes exact: inside the triangle the vote sum is exactly 3; outside, at most 2.

The dataset below mirrors the classic setup: class-0 circles (blue) filling the triangular island, class-1 crosses (red) surrounding it. Since inside must be class 0, the output unit fires when the vote count falls short of 3 — a NAND rather than an AND:

Why this can hit 100% when the sigmoid version hovered at “everything ≈ 0.25”: hard votes leave no ambiguity — the sum is an integer, 3 inside and ≤2 outside, and one threshold separates them perfectly. The price, from the training tab: the step function's slope is zero everywhere, so no gradient exists — these weights can only be designed, never learned. Perfect confidence and perfect untrainability are the same coin.

T

What Training Actually Does

The setup. The Step-Through tab uses 13 hand-picked numbers. Training replaces the hand-picking: start from some initialization, measure how wrong the network is on the data, and nudge every one of the 13 parameters downhill on that wrongness. Repeat until the nudges become negligible.

The loss (this problem uses average squared loss):

$$\ell = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(o^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\big)^2$$

The gradient descent rule — applied to every parameter $w$ (all 9 weights and all 4 biases), simultaneously, each iteration:

$$w^{\,t+1} \;=\; w^{\,t} \;-\; \alpha\,\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial w}\bigg|_{w^{t}}$$

($t$ indexes the iteration; the gradient is evaluated at the current parameters $w^t$ — all 13 step simultaneously from the same shared values. You will also see this written $w := w - \alpha\,\partial\ell/\partial w$, assignment-style; identical meaning.)

where $\alpha$ is the learning rate. Thirteen partial derivatives are computed (that computation is backpropagation — the chain rule run along each parameter's path to the loss), then all thirteen parameters step at once, using the shared current values. Same simultaneous-update discipline as GLM gradient descent.

T

Backpropagation: A Worked Derivation

Here is one full gradient, derived from scratch — the update for $w^{[1]}_{1,2}$, the weight on the arrow from $x_1$ into $h_2$. This computation is backpropagation: the chain rule run along the weight's path to the loss.

Givens. Forward pass: $z_j = w^{[1]}_{0,j} + w^{[1]}_{1,j}x_1 + w^{[1]}_{2,j}x_2$,  $h_j = \sigma(z_j)$,  $u = w^{[2]}_0 + \sum_j w^{[2]}_j h_j$,  $o = \sigma(u)$. Loss: $\ell = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (o^{(i)} - y^{(i)})^2$.

Lemma (sigmoid derivative). $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ — derived on the Reference tab; cited below as (lemma).

Plan. $w^{[1]}_{1,2}$ influences $\ell$ through exactly one path:

$$w^{[1]}_{1,2} \to z_2 \to h_2 \to u \to o \to \ell$$

so we differentiate each link (five factors), multiply them (chain rule), average over examples, then plug into the GD rule. Observe the nesting $\ell = \ell\big(o\big(u\big(h_2\big(z_2(w)\big)\big)\big)\big)$, so by the chain rule of differentiation:

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial o}\cdot\frac{\partial o}{\partial u}\cdot\frac{\partial u}{\partial h_2}\cdot\frac{\partial h_2}{\partial z_2}\cdot\frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}}$$

1

Link 1: $\partial\ell/\partial o$. The loss for one example is $\ell = (o-y)^2$, so by the power rule:

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial o} = 2(o-y)$$

2

Link 2: $\partial o/\partial u$. Since $o = \sigma(u)$, by the lemma, written via the neuron's own output:

$$\frac{\partial o}{\partial u} = o(1-o)$$

3

Link 3: $\partial u/\partial h_2$. In $u = w^{[2]}_0 + w^{[2]}_1 h_1 + w^{[2]}_2 h_2 + w^{[2]}_3 h_3$, only the matching term survives differentiation:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial h_2} = w^{[2]}_2$$

4

Link 4: $\partial h_2/\partial z_2$. Since $h_2 = \sigma(z_2)$, by the lemma again, one layer down:

$$\frac{\partial h_2}{\partial z_2} = h_2(1-h_2)$$

5

Link 5: $\partial z_2/\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}$. In $z_2 = w^{[1]}_{0,2} + w^{[1]}_{1,2}x_1 + w^{[1]}_{2,2}x_2$, the coefficient of the target weight is $x_1$:

$$\frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}} = x_1$$

Now just apply the chain rule — multiply the five links, restore example indices, and average (differentiation passes through the sum; the 2 comes out front):

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}} = \frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(o^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\big)\,o^{(i)}\big(1-o^{(i)}\big)\,w^{[2]}_2\,h_2^{(i)}\big(1-h_2^{(i)}\big)\,x_1^{(i)}$$

Plug into the GD rule:

$$w^{[1],\,t+1}_{1,2} = w^{[1],\,t}_{1,2} - \alpha\,\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}} \qquad \blacksquare$$

Factor audit: (output error) × (output sigmoid’s slope) × (the wire $w^{[2]}_2$) × ($h_2$’s slope) × (the input that fed the weight). Note only $w^{[2]}_2$ appears — no sum over $j$ — because the path is unique: this weight touches the loss only through $h_2$.

The chain rule is doing all the heavy lifting. There is no new mathematics in backpropagation — every gradient in the roster below is just the chain rule applied along a path, with only three derivative species ever needed: a power rule (the loss), the sigmoid lemma (each activation), and “pick off the matching coefficient” (each linear sum). Backpropagation’s only extra idea is bookkeeping: paths through the network share their early links, so compute the shared prefix once (the error signal $2(o{-}y)\,o(1{-}o)$) and reuse it for every parameter. Chain rule + caching — that is the entire algorithm.

T

The Full Update Roster

Every parameter's gradient is a product of chain-rule factors along its path to the loss. The paths share most of their links — only the tail differs:

Parameter (count)Path tailGradient (per example)
$w^{[2]}_j$ — hidden→output weights (3)$u \to o \to \ell$$2(o{-}y)\cdot o(1{-}o)\cdot h_j$
$w^{[2]}_0$ — output bias (1)$u \to o \to \ell$$2(o{-}y)\cdot o(1{-}o)\cdot 1$
$w^{[1]}_{i,j}$ — input→hidden weights (6)$z_j \to h_j \to u \to o \to \ell$$2(o{-}y)\cdot o(1{-}o)\cdot w^{[2]}_j\cdot h_j(1{-}h_j)\cdot x_i$
$w^{[1]}_{0,j}$ — hidden biases (3)$z_j \to h_j \to u \to o \to \ell$$2(o{-}y)\cdot o(1{-}o)\cdot w^{[2]}_j\cdot h_j(1{-}h_j)\cdot 1$

Read down the last column: the shared prefix $2(o{-}y)\,o(1{-}o)$ is the output error signal computed once and reused by all 13; deeper parameters multiply on more links; and a bias is always its layer's weight formula with the final input replaced by $1$. For a concrete instance of row 3, see the worked backprop path on the Reference tab.

What each kind of parameter learns. As the updates run: the input→hidden weights rotate the three detector lines; the hidden biases slide those lines into position; the hidden→output weights decide how much each detector's vote counts (and its sign); the output bias sets how much total evidence is demanded before firing. Training is all four kinds negotiating simultaneously until the loss stops improving.

Connection to logistic regression: each row's update has the familiar shape (error signal) × (local input) — the output layer's rule is literally logistic-regression-like with $h$'s as features, except squared loss leaves the extra $o(1{-}o)$ factor that NLL would have cancelled. And unlike GLM training, this loss is not convex in the 13 parameters — hidden units can be permuted without changing the function, so the surface has multiple equivalent minima and no global-optimum guarantee of the kind convex models enjoy. Gradient descent still works in practice; it just loses its convexity warranty.

Saturation warning (why step functions can't be trained). Every hidden-layer gradient carries the factors $o(1{-}o)$ and $h_j(1{-}h_j)$ — the sigmoids' slopes. When a sigmoid saturates (output near 0 or 1), its slope is near zero and the gradient dies. Crank the Geometry Lab's sharpness slider and imagine training at $k=8$: crisp predictions, nearly-zero gradients. The limit is the step function: slope exactly zero everywhere, gradient descent completely blind — which is why step-activated networks must have their weights constructed by hand rather than learned.

3

The Forward Pass, Formally

Hidden layer (for j = 1, 2, 3)

$$z_j = w^{[1]}_{0,j} + w^{[1]}_{1,j}x_1 + w^{[1]}_{2,j}x_2, \qquad h_j = \sigma(z_j)$$

Three parallel “little logistic regressions” on the raw features. Each has its own bias $w^{[1]}_{0,j}$.

Output layer

$$u = w^{[2]}_{0} + \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{3} w^{[2]}_{j}h_j, \qquad o = \sigma(u)$$

One more logistic regression — but its inputs are $(h_1,h_2,h_3)$, the hidden layer's re-description of the point.

Sigmoid & its derivative

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}, \qquad \sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big)$$

The derivative identity powers every backprop slope factor below. Also: $1-\sigma(-z) = \sigma(z)$.

Loss (this problem uses squared loss)

$$\ell = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(o^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\big)^2$$

Not the usual NLL — so the convenient sigmoid/log cancellations from logistic regression do not occur here.

4

Parameter Census

Everything gradient descent tunes, in one count:

  • Input → hidden weights: $2 \times 3 = 6$  (the $w^{[1]}_{i,j}$)
  • Hidden biases: $3$  (the $w^{[1]}_{0,j}$)
  • Hidden → output weights: $3$  (the $w^{[2]}_{j}$)
  • Output bias: $1$  (the $w^{[2]}_{0}$)

$$\textbf{Total: } 6 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 13 \text{ parameters}$$

5

A Backprop Path, Worked

0

The plan. The weight $w^{[1]}_{1,2}$ (arrow from $x_1$ into $h_2$) touches the loss through exactly one path. The gradient is the chain rule multiplied along that path, link by link.

$$w^{[1]}_{1,2} \;\to\; z_2 \;\to\; h_2 \;\to\; u \;\to\; o \;\to\; \ell$$

$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \underbrace{2(o^{(i)}{-}y^{(i)})}_{\ell\to o}\cdot \underbrace{o^{(i)}(1{-}o^{(i)})}_{o\to u}\cdot \underbrace{w^{[2]}_{2}}_{u\to h_2}\cdot \underbrace{h^{(i)}_2(1{-}h^{(i)}_2)}_{h_2\to z_2}\cdot \underbrace{x^{(i)}_1}_{z_2\to w}$$

Every factor is a number you can point at in the Step-Through tab: the output error, the output sigmoid's slope, the wire $w^{[2]}_2$, $h_2$'s own slope, and the input that fed it. Note only $w^{[2]}_2$ appears — $h_1$ and $h_3$ are separate paths that this weight never touches.

Run the Numbers Yourself

Use the fixed weights from the Step-Through tab (h₁: bias −0.5, weights 1, 0 • h₂: bias −0.5, weights 0, 1 • h₃: bias 3, weights −1, −1 • output: bias −5, weights 2, 2, 2). Work each pass by hand before checking, then verify in the Step-Through tab.

1

Forward pass: compute $z_1, z_2, z_3$, then $h_1, h_2, h_3$, then $u$ and $o$ for the point $x = (3.5,\, 3.5)$.

$z_1 = -0.5 + 3.5 = 3.0$,   $z_2 = -0.5 + 3.5 = 3.0$,   $z_3 = 3 - 3.5 - 3.5 = -4.0$

$h_1 = \sigma(3.0) \approx 0.95$,   $h_2 \approx 0.95$,   $h_3 = \sigma(-4.0) \approx 0.02$

$u = -5 + 2(0.95 + 0.95 + 0.02) = -5 + 3.84 = -1.16$

$o = \sigma(-1.16) \approx \mathbf{0.24}$ — the diagonal detector $h_3$ vetoes hard, but note $o$ is barely different from point A's $0.25$.

2

Forward pass: same task for $x = (1.0,\, 0.3)$ (bottom edge of the plot, red region).

$z_1 = -0.5 + 1.0 = 0.5 \Rightarrow h_1 \approx 0.62$

$z_2 = -0.5 + 0.3 = -0.2 \Rightarrow h_2 \approx 0.45$

$z_3 = 3 - 1.0 - 0.3 = 1.7 \Rightarrow h_3 \approx 0.85$

$u = -5 + 2(0.62+0.45+0.85) = -5 + 3.84 = -1.16 \Rightarrow o \approx \mathbf{0.24}$. The point fails $h_2$'s “above $x_2 = 0.5$” test — a different detector vetoes than in problem 1, yet the output is identical.

3

Conceptual: problems 1 and 2 gave $o \approx 0.24$ for two very different red-region points, and the blue-region point A also gives $o \approx 0.25$. Explain mechanically why this network barely separates anything — and name the two fixes.

With $k=1$ sigmoids, points near any detector line produce lukewarm votes ($h \approx 0.4$–$0.7$), so the sum $h_1+h_2+h_3$ hovers near $\approx 1.9$ for almost everyone — inside points don't reach the $\approx 2.5$ the output bias $-5$ demands (with weights 2), and outside points aren't pushed much lower. The pre-activation $u$ barely moves, so $o$ barely moves.

Fixes: (1) sharpen the hidden units — scale up layer-1 weights/biases so votes commit toward 0/1 (the Geometry Lab slider; the $k\to\infty$ limit is the step function); (2) retune the output layer's weights/bias to the votes actually produced — which is precisely what gradient descent would do.

4

Backprop warm-up: for point A at step 10 of the Step-Through (so $o = 0.25$, $h_2 = 0.73$, $x_1 = 1.5$, $w^{[2]}_2 = 2$) and true label $y = 0$, evaluate every factor of $\dfrac{\partial \ell}{\partial w^{[1]}_{1,2}}$ for this single example, then multiply.

$2(o-y) = 2(0.25) = 0.50$;   $o(1-o) = 0.25 \times 0.75 \approx 0.19$;   $w^{[2]}_2 = 2$;   $h_2(1-h_2) = 0.73\times 0.27 \approx 0.20$;   $x_1 = 1.5$.

Product: $0.50 \times 0.19 \times 2 \times 0.20 \times 1.5 \approx \mathbf{0.057}$. A positive gradient, so a descent step decreases $w^{[1]}_{1,2}$ slightly — sensible, since the network over-predicted ($o = 0.25$ against $y = 0$) and this path contributed to it.
