Linear Algebra Core

The Four Subspaces & Row-Space Trap PS1-4d

Givens: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ (rows = training examples $x^{(i)\top}$), $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ parameter vector.

$$\operatorname{Row}(X) = \operatorname{Col}(X^\top) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d, \qquad \operatorname{Col}(X) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$$ $$\beta = X^\top v \text{ for some } v\in\mathbb{R}^n \iff \beta \in \operatorname{Row}(X)$$
The "GD trapped in Row($X$)" punchline: GD initialized at $\beta^{(0)}=0$ only ever adds multiples of $X^\top(\cdot)$, so every iterate stays in $\operatorname{Row}(X)$ (proof by induction: $0 = X^\top 0$; update keeps the form $\beta^{(t)} = X^\top v^{(t)}$). Limits preserve subspace membership ⇒ the solution GD finds lies in Row($X$). Combined with $X\hat\beta = y$: $(XX^\top)v = y \Rightarrow \hat\beta = X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}y$ = the min-norm solution.

Rank–Nullity ADDED: $\dim\operatorname{Row}(X) + \dim\operatorname{Null}(X) = d$. With $n < d$ and full row rank, $\dim\operatorname{Null}(X) = d-n \ge 1$ ⇒ a nonzero null vector exists ⇒ infinitely many interpolating solutions $\beta = \rho + \zeta$, $X\zeta = 0$.

Spectral Theorem — cite by name, don't prove

$A$ symmetric ($A = A^\top$, real entries) $\Longrightarrow$

$$A = Q\Lambda Q^\top, \quad Q^\top Q = QQ^\top = I, \quad \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d) \text{ real}$$

Real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors, always diagonalizable. Orthogonal maps preserve norms: $\|Q^\top v\| = \|v\|$.

Eigendecomposition mechanics

$$A^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top, \qquad A^k = Q\Lambda^k Q^\top$$

Inverse flips eigenvalues ($\lambda \mapsto 1/\lambda$), keeps the same eigenvectors. Powers act on eigenvalues only.

Rayleigh bound ADDED · PS1-1d

$$v^\top A v \le \lambda_{\max}(A)\,\|v\|^2 \quad (A = A^\top)$$

Proof: set $z = Q^\top v$; then $v^\top A v = \sum_i \lambda_i z_i^2 \le \lambda_{\max}\|z\|^2 = \lambda_{\max}\|v\|^2$. Load-bearing in the GD descent lemma.

Trace

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_i A_{ii} = \sum_i \lambda_i$$

Cyclic property: $\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(BCA) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB)$. Proof of $\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$: both equal $\sum_i\sum_j A_{ij}B_{ji}$.

Rotation invariance PS1-1c

$$\lambda_i(R A R^\top) = \lambda_i(A) \text{ for rotation } R$$

Rotating a quadratic objective rotates the contours but preserves eigenvalues — so GD step-size limits are rotation-invariant.

Worked Example — What Diagonalization Actually Looks Like NUMBERS

Givens: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ (symmetric). Characteristic polynomial: $(2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow \lambda_1 = 3,\; \lambda_2 = 1$. Eigenvectors: $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)^\top$ (orthonormal, as the spectral theorem promises).

$$A \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}}_{Q}\; \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{\Lambda}\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}}_{Q^\top}$$

Now every "mechanics" claim from this tab becomes arithmetic you can check:

Inverse flips eigenvalues

$$A^{-1} = Q\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac13 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}Q^\top = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac23 & -\tfrac13 \\ -\tfrac13 & \tfrac23 \end{pmatrix}$$

Same $Q$, eigenvalues $3, 1 \mapsto \tfrac13, 1$. Check: $A A^{-1} = I$. ✓

Powers act on eigenvalues

$$A^{2} = Q\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}Q^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$$

Multiply $A\cdot A$ directly to confirm. ✓

Trace & PD check

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = 2 + 2 = 4 = 3 + 1 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$$

Both eigenvalues $> 0$ ⇒ $A$ is PD ⇒ invertible, unique minimizer of $w^\top A w - b^\top w$ stories.

Rayleigh bound, live

$$v = (1,0)^\top:\;\; v^\top A v = 2 \;\le\; \lambda_{\max}\|v\|^2 = 3$$

Equality only when $v$ is the top eigenvector: $v = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top$ gives exactly $3$.

Same $Q$, very different matrix: the adjacency matrix $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ diagonalizes with the identical $Q$ but $\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$: $$v = (1,-1)^\top:\quad v^\top B v = -2 \;<\; 0$$ Symmetric, perfectly diagonalizable, not PSD. Diagonalizability and positivity are separate properties — the eigenvalue signs are the whole story.

PSD vs PD — definitions and why PD is load-bearing

Givens: $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ symmetric, quadratic form $v^\top A v$.

$$\text{PSD:}\quad v^\top A v \ge 0 \;\;\forall v \qquad\iff\qquad \lambda_i \ge 0 \;\forall i \;\;(\text{symmetric case})$$ $$\text{PD:}\quad v^\top A v > 0 \;\;\forall v \ne 0 \qquad\iff\qquad \lambda_i > 0 \;\forall i$$
★

PD $\Rightarrow$ invertible (proof by contradiction) ADDED · PS1-5a: if $Ax = 0$ for some $x \ne 0$, then $x^\top A x = 0$, contradicting $x^\top A x > 0$. So $\operatorname{Null}(A) = \{0\}$ and $A^{-1}$ exists. This is why $X^\top X + \lambda I$ ($\lambda > 0$) is always invertible: PSD + $\lambda I$ = PD.

Trap — PD, not PSD, is what buys uniqueness PS1-5a: PSD gives convexity (global min exists), but only PD gives a unique minimizer / invertibility. And symmetric $\not\Rightarrow$ PSD: the adjacency matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $\pm 1$.

Covariance Matrices

Givens: random vector $X \in \mathbb{R}^d$, $\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^\top]$.

$$v^\top \Sigma v = \operatorname{Var}(v^\top X) \ge 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Sigma \text{ is always PSD (and symmetric)}$$
  • $\Sigma$ invertible $\Rightarrow$ no zero eigenvalues $\Rightarrow$ $\Sigma$ is PD.
  • $\Sigma^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^\top$ is symmetric and PD too (eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i > 0$) — the precision matrix in the Gaussian density is well-behaved.

Matrix Calculus

Conventions

  • Vectors are columns. The gradient of a scalar w.r.t. a variable takes the variable's shape: $\nabla_w f \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ if $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$; $\nabla_W f$ has $W$'s shape.
  • Jacobian = outputs × inputs: $J_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial x_j$, so $J \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ for $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^m$.
  • Perturbation identity is ground truth: $f(x + \delta) \approx f(x) + \langle \nabla f(x), \delta\rangle$. When in doubt about a rule, expand to first order in $\delta$ and read the gradient off the linear term.

Vector-Variable Rules

Linear map / Jacobian

$$\frac{\partial (Aw)}{\partial w} = A$$

Jacobian of a linear map is the matrix itself.

Inner product

$$\nabla_w\, a^\top w = a$$

Gradient takes the variable's (column) shape.

Quadratic form — symmetrize trap

$$\nabla_w\, w^\top A w = (A + A^\top)\,w$$

Only collapses to $2Aw$ when $A$ is symmetric. Writing $2A\theta$ for general $A$ is a classic exam error.

Least-squares residual

$$\nabla_w \|Xw - y\|^2 = 2X^\top(Xw - y)$$

Residual first, then chain rule pushes $X^\top$ out front.

Log-det ADDED · PS3-4c

$$\nabla_S \log|S| = S^{-1}$$

Key step in the GMM M-step for $\Sigma$ (work with the precision $S=\Sigma^{-1}$; chain rule justifies switching variables since $\nabla_S \ell = 0 \iff \nabla_\Sigma \ell = 0$).

Gaussian quadratic ADDED · PS3-4c

$$\nabla_\mu (x-\mu)^\top S (x-\mu) = -2S(x-\mu)$$ $$\nabla_S (x-\mu)^\top S (x-\mu) = (x-\mu)(x-\mu)^\top$$

The two identities that generate the $\mu_j$ and $\Sigma_j$ EM updates.

Left/Right Law & "Column-Born, Row-Filed"

$$\nabla_A \left( u^\top A v \right) = u v^\top$$

The factor on the left of $A$ becomes the column, the factor on the right becomes the row. A transpose on the variable swaps the sides.

"Column-born, row-filed": in $Y = EXw$ stories, $\nabla_E Y_i = e_i (Xw)^\top$ — the output index is born as a column selector $e_i$, the input vector gets filed as a row. Verify any such formula with the shape test: the gradient must have exactly the variable's shape. The shape test never fails you on exam day.

Backprop compositions: for $\ell(g(h(w)))$, chain Jacobians right-to-left, then transpose once at the end to make shapes match: $\nabla_w \ell = J_h^\top J_g^\top \nabla \ell$. Every intermediate product must pass the shape test.

Flow-Through With Numbers NUMBERS

Givens: $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ (deliberately not symmetric), $\;w = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\;a = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Run every rule from the table above on these.

1

Inner product. $a^\top w = 2 - 1 = 1$ (scalar). Gradient: $\nabla_w\, a^\top w = a = (2, -1)^\top$ — same shape as $w$. Shape test ✓

2

Linear map. $Aw = \begin{pmatrix} 1+2 \\ 3+4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}$. Jacobian: $\partial(Aw)/\partial w = A$, a $2\times 2$ = outputs × inputs. ✓

3

Quadratic form — watch the trap fire. $w^\top A w = w^\top (3,7)^\top = 10$. The rule says $$\nabla_w\, w^\top A w = (A + A^\top)w = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 5 & 8 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix}$$ The lazy answer $2Aw = (6, 14)^\top$ is wrong. Ground-truth check by expanding to a scalar: $f(w) = w_1^2 + 5w_1 w_2 + 4w_2^2$, so $\partial f/\partial w_1 = 2w_1 + 5w_2 = 7$ and $\partial f/\partial w_2 = 5w_1 + 8w_2 = 13$. ✓ The symmetrize rule wins.

4

Least-squares gradient. $X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $\theta = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Residual $r = X\theta - y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$; then $$\nabla_\theta \|X\theta - y\|^2 = 2X^\top r = 2\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}$$ $X^\top$ pulls a length-3 residual back to a length-2 gradient — the shape test in action.

5

Left/right law. $u = (1,2)^\top$, $v = (3,1)^\top$: $$\nabla_A\, u^\top A v = uv^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 6 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$ $u$ (left of $A$) born as the column, $v$ (right of $A$) filed as the row; result has $A$'s shape. ✓

Regression Models

OLS

Givens: $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$, $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, objective $J(\theta) = \tfrac12\|X\theta - y\|^2$.

1

Name the residual. $r(\theta) = X\theta - y$, so $J = \tfrac12 r^\top r$.

2

Chain rule. $\nabla_\theta J = \left(\frac{\partial r}{\partial \theta}\right)^\top r = X^\top r$.

3

Set to zero. $X^\top(X\theta - y) = 0 \Rightarrow X^\top X\,\theta = X^\top y$ (normal equations).

$$\nabla J = X^\top(X\theta - y), \qquad \boxed{\theta^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y} \;\;(X^\top X \text{ invertible})$$
  • Projection interpretation: $X\theta^*$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto $\operatorname{Col}(X)$; the residual is $\perp$ to every column of $X$.
  • Why a closed form exists: stationarity condition is linear in $\theta$. Whenever $\nabla J = 0$ is linear in the parameter, you can solve in closed form; when it's transcendental (logistic), you can't.
USE IT FOR — predicting a continuous target from features: house price from square footage, exam score from study hours. The baseline every fancier regression is measured against.

Weighted Least Squares & LWR PS1-2

Givens: weights $w^{(i)} > 0$, $J(\theta) = \tfrac12\sum_i w^{(i)}(\theta^\top x^{(i)} - y^{(i)})^2 = (X\theta - y)^\top W (X\theta - y)$.

Trap: the $\tfrac12$ lives inside $W$: $W_{ii} = \tfrac{1}{2}w^{(i)}$, off-diagonals $0$. (Positive rescaling never changes the argmin, but state $W$ correctly.)
$$\nabla J = 2X^\top W X \theta - 2X^\top W y \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\theta^* = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y}$$
MLE $\iff$ WLS with inverse-variance weights ADDED · PS1-2a(iii): with heteroscedastic Gaussian noise $y^{(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta^\top x^{(i)}, (\sigma^{(i)})^2)$, maximizing the log-likelihood $\equiv$ minimizing $\sum_i \frac{(y^{(i)} - \theta^\top x^{(i)})^2}{2(\sigma^{(i)})^2}$, i.e. WLS with $w^{(i)} = 1/(\sigma^{(i)})^2$. Noisier points get down-weighted by their precision.

LWR: at each query $x$, solve WLS with Gaussian kernel weights $w^{(i)} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x^{(i)} - x\|^2}{2\tau^2}\right)$. Non-parametric, per-query solve. Bandwidth $\tau$: small $\tau$ → only neighbors matter → overfits noise; large $\tau$ → uniform weights → global linear fit → underfits. Validation error is U-shaped in $\tau$ PS1-2c.

USE IT FOR — WLS: when you know some measurements are noisier than others (weight by precision $1/\sigma^2$). LWR: a smooth trend whose shape changes across the input — the PS1 wavy sensor data, fuel efficiency vs speed.

Ridge Regression

Givens: $J_\lambda(\theta) = \tfrac12\|X\theta - y\|^2 + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$, $\lambda > 0$.

$$\boxed{\hat\theta_\lambda = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y}$$
  • Always invertible: $X^\top X$ PSD $+\; \lambda I$ ($\lambda>0$) $=$ PD $\Rightarrow$ invertible (Tab 1 lemma) $\Rightarrow$ unique minimizer regardless of $n$ vs $d$.
  • L2 = MAP with Gaussian prior PS2-3: $\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tfrac{1}{\lambda}I)$ prior + Gaussian likelihood ⇒ MAP estimate = ridge solution.
  • $\lambda = 0$ with singular $X^\top X$: define $\hat\theta_0 = (X^\top X)^+ X^\top y$ via the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse.
USE IT FOR — many correlated features or $d \gtrsim n$ (predicting an outcome from thousands of gene-expression levels with few patients); any time OLS is non-invertible, unstable, or overfitting.

Min-Norm Solutions & Implicit Regularization PS1-4

Givens: overparameterized regime $n < d$, $XX^\top$ invertible, noiseless labels $y = X\beta^*$.

All zero-cost solutions: $\;\beta = \rho + \zeta$, where

$$\rho = X^\top(XX^\top)^{-1}y \;\;(\text{min-norm, in Row}(X)), \qquad X\zeta = 0 \;\;(\zeta \in \operatorname{Null}(X))$$ $$\|\beta\|^2 = \|\rho\|^2 + \|\zeta\|^2 \;\;(\rho \perp \zeta, \text{ Pythagoras}) \;\Rightarrow\; \rho \text{ has strictly smallest norm}$$
  • GD from $0$ converges to the min-norm solution: iterates stay in Row($X$) (Tab 1), and the only zero-cost point in Row($X$) is $\rho$.
  • Empirically: validation error grows with solution norm — min-norm generalizes best PS1-4c.
Three implicit-regularization knobs ADDED · PS1-4f/g — all reach zero training error, but generalization differs:
  • Zero initialization → min-norm solution (the theorem above).
  • Initialization scale: in the quadratically parameterized model $f(x) = x^\top(\theta^{\odot 2} - \phi^{\odot 2})$, smaller init $\alpha$ generalizes better (val err $0.24 \to 0.031 \to 0.0045$ for $\alpha = 0.1, 0.03, 0.01$).
  • Batch size: smaller SGD batches (more gradient noise) find better-generalizing minima; batch $=n$ recovers full-batch GD exactly.
USE IT FOR — the exam story of why overparameterized models (deep nets) can generalize at all: the optimizer itself acts as a regularizer, silently picking the tame solution out of infinitely many interpolants.

GD Convergence — Quantitative Results PS1-1

Givens: update $\theta^{[t]} = \theta^{[t-1]} - \alpha\nabla J(\theta^{[t-1]})$.

SettingConvergence conditionKey fact
Scalar quadratic $J = \tfrac12\beta\theta^2$$0 < \alpha < 2/\beta$$\theta^{[t]} = \theta^{[0]}(1-\alpha\beta)^t$ (geometric); $\alpha = 1/\beta$ converges in one step
Diagonal quadratic $J = \tfrac12\sum_i\beta_i\theta_i^2$$0 < \alpha < 2/\max_i \beta_i$Coordinates decouple; the largest curvature dictates the step size
General convex, $\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^2 J) \le \beta_{\max}$$0 < \alpha < 1/\beta_{\max}$ (safe)Descent lemma below; strict decrease for $\alpha < 2/\beta_{\max}$

Iteration count ADDED · PS1-1a

$$T = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(\epsilon/|\theta^{[0]}|)}{\ln|1 - \alpha\beta|} \right\rceil$$

$T(\alpha)$ is U-shaped: decreasing on $(0, 1/\beta)$, increasing on $(1/\beta, 2/\beta)$. Near $2/\beta$: overshoot/oscillation across the valley; near $0$: tiny steps. Both logs are negative — watch inequality flips.

Descent lemma ADDED · PS1-1d

$$J(\theta^{[t]}) \le J(\theta^{[t-1]}) - \alpha\left(1 - \tfrac{\alpha\beta_{\max}}{2}\right)\|\nabla J\|^2$$

From Taylor + Rayleigh bound $v^\top\nabla^2 J\, v \le \beta_{\max}\|v\|^2$. Decrease + bounded below + MCT ⇒ $J$ converges (and $\|\nabla J\| \to 0$).

Trap 1 — monotone decrease $\ne$ convergence to the min. You need bounded below + Monotone Convergence Theorem to conclude $J$ converges — and even then only to some value (3 sightings across psets).

Trap 2 — stopping $\ne$ converging ADDED: (a) at $\alpha = 2/\beta_i$ exactly, a coordinate oscillates $\pm$ forever with $J$ constant, so a $|\Delta J| < \epsilon$ rule falsely reports convergence at a non-optimum PS1-1c. (b) A decaying rate $\alpha_t \propto 1/t^2$ makes $\|\Delta\theta\|$ shrink to zero because steps shrink, triggering the stopping rule without the iterates approaching a minimizer PS2-3c.

$\alpha$ failure modes: too small → slow crawl; $\in(1/\beta, 2/\beta)$ → oscillating but converging; $= 2/\beta$ → permanent oscillation; $> 2/\beta$ → divergence.

Classification & GLMs

Framework first: the exponential family is the machine that generates every classifier on this tab. Pick a distribution for $y$, set $\eta = \theta^\top x$, and logistic regression, Poisson regression, and softmax all fall out as special cases — same convexity proof, same gradient shape.

Exponential Family & GLMs — the Machine

$$p(y;\eta) = b(y)\exp\!\left(\eta^\top T(y) - a(\eta)\right)$$
  • $b(y)$: base measure (constant during optimization) • $T(y)$: sufficient statistic (usually $T(y)=y$) • $\eta$: natural parameter • $a(\eta)$: log-partition (normalizer).
  • Moments by differentiation (proved by differentiating $\int p = 1$ under the integral sign): $$a'(\eta) = \mathbb{E}[Y;\eta], \qquad a''(\eta) = \operatorname{Var}(Y;\eta)$$
Every GLM is convex ADDED · PS2-2c: for NLL $\ell(\theta)$ with $\eta = \theta^\top x$, $$H = a''(\theta^\top x)\, x x^\top \quad\text{(rank-1)}, \qquad z^\top H z = a''(\theta^\top x)\,(x^\top z)^2 \ge 0$$ since $a'' = \operatorname{Var} \ge 0$. PSD Hessian ⇒ convex NLL for any GLM — one proof covers linear, logistic, Poisson, all of them.

Universal GLM gradient (same "(error) × input" shape for every family):

$$\nabla_\theta \ell = \sum_i \left(y^{(i)} - h_\theta(x^{(i)})\right) x^{(i)}, \qquad h_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = g(\theta^\top x)$$

Logistic Regression — the Bernoulli GLM, Worked Out

Givens: Bernoulli $y \in \{0,1\}$ ⇒ canonical response is the sigmoid, so $h_\theta(x) = g(\theta^\top x)$, $g(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$. Everything below is the general machine specialized to one row of the table above.

$$g'(z) = g(z)(1-g(z)) \qquad \ell(\theta) = \sum_i \left[ y^{(i)}\log h_\theta(x^{(i)}) + (1-y^{(i)})\log(1-h_\theta(x^{(i)})) \right]$$ $$\nabla_\theta \ell = \sum_i (y^{(i)} - h_\theta(x^{(i)}))\,x^{(i)} \qquad \theta := \theta + \alpha\, \nabla_\theta\ell \;(\text{ascent})$$
  • Concave log-likelihood (NLL convex — instance of the $a''\,xx^\top$ theorem) ⇒ any stationary point is the global MLE… when one exists.
  • No closed form: $\nabla\ell = 0$ is transcendental in $\theta$ (sigmoids of $\theta$), unlike OLS.
  • Decision boundary $\theta^\top x = 0$ (where $h = \tfrac12$). ADDED It is scale-invariant: $c\theta$ ($c>0$) gives the same line — which is why L2 regularization shrinks $\|\theta\|$ (less confident probabilities) while barely rotating the boundary on non-separable data.
Separable-data pathology PS2-3: if a strict separator $\theta^*$ exists, then $J(c\theta^*) \to 0$ as $c \to \infty$ but $J > 0$ for every finite $\theta$ — the infimum is never attained, $\|\theta\| \to \infty$, no finite MLE. Not a numerical/rounding issue.

Fixes checklist nuances ADDED: L2 regularization ✓ (loss now blows up as $\|\theta\|\to\infty$); big-enough label/feature noise ✓ (destroys separability); different constant $\alpha$ ✗ (changes speed only); decaying $\alpha_t \propto 1/t^2$ ✗ (terminates without converging); rescaling features by $k$ ✗ (separability is scale-invariant).

USE IT FOR — binary decisions where you want a probability, not just a label: spam vs not-spam, disease present/absent, click/no-click. The default first classifier for almost any yes/no question.

Imbalanced Data PS2-4

Givens: positive fraction $\rho \in (0,1)$ small; confusion counts TP, TN, FP, FN; $A_1 = \frac{TP}{TP+FN}$ (recall on positives), $A_0 = \frac{TN}{TN+FP}$.

$$A = \rho A_1 + (1-\rho)A_0 \qquad \bar{A} = \tfrac12(A_0 + A_1)$$
  • Trivial classifier exposes accuracy ADDED: always predicting the majority class gets $A \ge 1-\rho$ (e.g. 99%+) but $\bar A = 50\%$ exactly. Accuracy weights classes by prior; balanced accuracy weights them equally.
  • Resampling $=$ reweighting: with $\kappa = \frac{\rho}{1-\rho}$, duplicating each positive $1/\kappa$ times makes the new prior $\rho' = \tfrac12$, and plain accuracy on the resampled set $=$ balanced accuracy on the original (duplication invariance). Equivalently: weight each example's loss by inverse class frequency.
  • Expected trade-off ADDED: after reweighting, $A_1$ jumps (0.57 → 0.91), $A_0$ dips, and plain accuracy drops while balanced accuracy rises — that's the point, not a failure.
USE IT FOR — fraud detection, rare-disease screening, spam with few positives: any task where one class is a sliver of the data and a do-nothing classifier already scores 99% accuracy.

Bias–Variance & Ensembles

The Picture — Error vs Model Complexity CHART

Left of the dashed line the model is too simple (bias dominates); right of it, too flexible (variance dominates). Total error $=$ bias$^2$ + variance + $\sigma^2$ sits in a U — the minimum is the capacity sweet spot.
Read it with the two-knob table below: moving right along the x-axis is the "capacity ↑" row (bias↓, variance↑). Adding data doesn't move you along this axis — it pulls the whole variance curve down and shifts the optimum to the right, which is why bigger datasets tolerate bigger models.

The Decomposition

$$\mathbb{E}\left[(y - \hat f(x))^2\right] = \underbrace{\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\right)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x))}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}$$
  • Bias, 3 levels: (i) informal — systematic error the model family can't escape; (ii) $\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)] - f(x)$ at a point; (iii) approximation error of the best-in-class hypothesis.
  • Variance = "redraw the dataset": how much $\hat f$ wobbles across independent draws of the training set of the same size. Expectations are over training sets.

Bootstrap & Bagging PS3-1d

  • Bootstrap: resample $n$ points with replacement. $P(\text{point excluded}) = (1-\tfrac1n)^n \to e^{-1}$, so each bootstrap set contains $\approx 1 - 1/e \approx 63.2\%$ unique points. Monte Carlo over bootstrap sets estimates an estimator's variance.
  • Bagging = variance killer for high-variance/low-bias models (deep trees). Useless for high-bias models — averaging biased models keeps the bias.
0

The plan. Expand the variance of a mean of $B$ identically distributed, pairwise-correlated trees and count terms.

1

Givens. $\operatorname{Var}(T_i) = \sigma^2$ for all $i$; $\operatorname{Corr}(T_i,T_j) = \rho$ so $\operatorname{Cov}(T_i,T_j) = \rho\sigma^2$ for $i \ne j$.

2

Expand. $\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac1B\sum T_i\right) = \tfrac{1}{B^2}\left[\sum_i \operatorname{Var}(T_i) + \sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(T_i,T_j)\right] = \tfrac{1}{B^2}\left[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\right]$ ($B(B-1)$ ordered pairs).

3

Simplify. Split into the $\rho$ part and the rest.

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac1B\textstyle\sum_i T_i\right) = \rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2}$$
The floor: as $B \to \infty$ the second term vanishes but $\rho\sigma^2$ remains — no amount of bagging removes correlated error. Random Forests exist to shrink $\rho$: random feature subsets at each split decorrelate the trees.
USE IT FOR — Random Forests on messy tabular data (mixed types, missing values, no feature engineering): the industry-default "just works" model for structured datasets.

Boosting & AdaBoost

  • Boosting = bias killer: sequentially fit weak learners to the same data, reweighted toward current mistakes. (Bagging resamples; boosting reweights — do not conflate.)
  • AdaBoost loop: train $h_t$ on weights $D_t$; weighted error $\varepsilon_t$; coefficient $\alpha_t$; upweight mistakes $D_{t+1}(i) \propto D_t(i)\,e^{-\alpha_t y^{(i)} h_t(x^{(i)})}$; final $H(x) = \operatorname{sign}\left(\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x)\right)$.
$$\alpha_t = \tfrac12 \ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}$$
  • $\varepsilon_t < \tfrac12 \Rightarrow \alpha_t > 0$; $\varepsilon_t > \tfrac12 \Rightarrow \alpha_t < 0$: a worse-than-chance learner gets a negative coefficient = inverted vote (still useful!).
  • Exponential-loss view: AdaBoost = greedy coordinate-wise minimization of $\sum_i e^{-y^{(i)} F(x^{(i)})}$.
USE IT FOR — combining many cheap, barely-better-than-chance rules into one strong classifier. Classic example: real-time face detection (Viola–Jones) built from thousands of simple edge-pattern stumps.

Trees, Clustering, EM

Decision Trees & Split Losses PS3-1

Givens: region $R$ with class proportions $p_k$; binary Gini $G(p) = 2p(1-p)$; misclassification $M(p) = \min(p, 1-p)$; split into $R_1, R_2$ with parent proportion $p_p = \lambda p_1 + (1-\lambda)p_2$, $\lambda = \frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}$.

Jensen split argument: $G''(p) = -4 < 0$ ⇒ $G$ strictly concave ⇒

$$\frac{n_1 G(p_1) + n_2 G(p_2)}{n_1+n_2} \le G(p_p), \text{ strict when } p_1 \ne p_2 \text{ and } \lambda \in (0,1)$$
  • Equality cases: an empty child ($\lambda \in \{0,1\}$) or $p_1 = p_2$ — these fail the strict-concavity hypotheses, so no contradiction.
  • Misclassification stalling: $M$ is piecewise linear; if both children share the parent's majority class, weighted child loss $=$ parent loss exactly (conservation of minority count: $N_{1}m_1 + N_2 m_2 = N_p m_p$). Splits that don't flip a majority give zero progress ⇒ why Gini/entropy are preferred.
  • Termination ADDED: any impure leaf (no-overlap data) contains two differing points ⇒ a non-vacuous split exists ⇒ regions strictly shrink ⇒ finite data ends at pure leaves, $G = 0$. Stalling blocks a particular split's progress, never termination.
Three-slot schema trap: purity criterion (which split wins) / tournament (search over $j, t$) / shrinkage (min-leaf-size or depth cap). "Shrinkage ensures termination," NOT improvement — the big recurring trap.

EM — the Jensen Sandwich PS3-4

0

The plan. Lower-bound the intractable $\log\sum_z$ with Jensen; make the bound tight at the current $\theta$; maximize the bound; conclude monotone improvement.

1

Lower bound (Jensen, log concave). $\log \sum_z Q(z)\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)} \ge \sum_z Q(z)\log\frac{p(x,z;\theta)}{Q(z)} =: \mathcal{L}(Q,\theta)$ — in class this is just "the lower bound from Jensen." (Papers call this exact object the ELBO, "evidence lower bound" — same thing, fancier name; don't let the acronym throw you on exam day.)

2

E-step = tightness. Choosing $Q(z) = p(z\mid x;\theta^{(t)})$ makes the ratio constant in $z$ — Jensen's equality condition — so the bound touches $\ell$ at $\theta^{(t)}$.

3

M-step = argmax of the bound. Then $\ell(\theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathcal{L}(Q,\theta^{(t+1)}) \ge \mathcal{L}(Q,\theta^{(t)}) = \ell(\theta^{(t)})$: monotone ↗. Bounded above ⇒ converges — but only to a local optimum; initialization matters (runs differ).

GMM E-step (Bayes + total probability) ADDED:

$$w_j^{(i)} = \frac{\phi_j\, \mathcal{N}(x^{(i)}; \mu_j, \Sigma_j)}{\sum_l \phi_l\, \mathcal{N}(x^{(i)}; \mu_l, \Sigma_l)}$$

M-step results (unsupervised): $\phi_j = \frac1n\sum_i w_j^{(i)}$,   $\mu_j = \frac{\sum_i w_j^{(i)} x^{(i)}}{\sum_i w_j^{(i)}}$,   $\Sigma_j = \frac{\sum_i w_j^{(i)} (x^{(i)}-\mu_j)(x^{(i)}-\mu_j)^\top}{\sum_i w_j^{(i)}}$

  • ADDED · PS3-4c Where the updates come from: $\mu_j$ via $\nabla_\mu(x-\mu)^\top S(x-\mu) = -2S(x-\mu)$; $\Sigma_j$ via precision reparam $S = \Sigma^{-1}$ and $\nabla_S \log|S| = S^{-1}$; $\phi_j$ via a Lagrange multiplier on $\sum_j \phi_j = 1$ (solve the multiplier by summing the stationarity condition over $j$; uses $\sum_j w_j^{(i)} = 1$).

Semi-Supervised EM PS3-4

Givens: $n$ unlabeled + $\tilde n$ labeled points, $\ell_{\text{semi}} = \ell_{\text{unsup}} + \alpha\,\ell_{\text{sup}}$.

  • Labeled examples enter every M-step update as $\alpha$-weighted hard counts ($\alpha \cdot 1\{\tilde z = j\}$ next to the soft $w_j^{(i)}$); denominators become $n + \alpha\tilde n$ style sums. Setting $\alpha = 0$ recovers plain EM.
  • Empirics ADDED: ~25 vs ~120 iterations, near-identical partitions across seeds, and 5 labels/cluster reliably recovers the high-variance component that unsupervised EM keeps absorbing into neighbors.
Trap: $\alpha$ is a fixed hyperparameter, not something you optimize in the M-step — the objective is linear in $\alpha$, so "optimizing" it degenerates ($\alpha \to \infty$ or $0$).
USE IT FOR — cluster discovery when labels exist but are scarce and expensive: tumor subtyping from gene-expression profiles where only a handful of biopsies have pathologist labels; a few anchors make the clustering fast, stable, and aligned with the real subtypes.

Probability & Inequalities

Inference Basics — Definitions First ADDED

Expectation

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \;\;\text{or}\;\; \int x\,p(x)\,dx$$

Linearity always holds: $\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}X + b\,\mathbb{E}Y$ — no independence needed. (Variance is the one that needs care.)

Variance

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2$$

$\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ — shifts vanish, scales square. Always $\ge 0$ (this fact alone proves GLM convexity).

Covariance

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Y$$

$\operatorname{Cov}(X,X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)$. Independence $\Rightarrow$ $\operatorname{Cov} = 0$, but $\operatorname{Cov}=0 \not\Rightarrow$ independent.

Correlation

$$\operatorname{Corr}(X,Y) = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}X}\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}Y}} \in [-1, 1]$$

Unitless, scale-free covariance. This $\rho$ is exactly the one in the bagging variance formula $\rho\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\sigma^2$.

Covariance matrix of a random vector $X \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with mean $\mu$ — an outer product, averaged:

$$\Sigma = \mathbb{E}\big[(X-\mu)(X-\mu)^\top\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}, \qquad \Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j), \qquad \Sigma_{ii} = \operatorname{Var}(X_i)$$

Symmetric by construction; PSD because $v^\top\Sigma v = \operatorname{Var}(v^\top X) \ge 0$ (Tab 1). It packs every pairwise covariance into one object — the $\Sigma$ in $\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\Sigma)$.

Operator glossary — $\odot$ is NOT the outer product:
  • Outer product $uv^\top$: column times row $\to$ a full matrix ($u \in \mathbb{R}^m, v \in \mathbb{R}^n \Rightarrow uv^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$). Where it lives: covariance $\Sigma$, GLM Hessian $a''\,xx^\top$, the left/right law $\nabla_A u^\top A v = uv^\top$, GMM $\Sigma_j$ updates.
  • Hadamard / element-wise product $u \odot v$ ("circle-dot"): same-shape arrays multiplied entry by entry, output the same shape. Where it lives: backprop $\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[2]}}W^{[2]\top}\right)\odot A \odot (1-A)$, the quadratically parameterized model $\theta^{\odot 2} - \phi^{\odot 2}$ (element-wise square).
  • Quick test: outer product of two vectors grows a matrix; $\odot$ never changes shape. Mixing them up fails the shape test instantly.

Jensen's Inequality

$$f \text{ convex:}\quad f(\mathbb{E}X) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \qquad\qquad f \text{ concave:}\quad f(\mathbb{E}X) \ge \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$$
  • Equality condition: $X$ constant (a.s.), or $f$ affine on the support — this is exactly what makes the EM E-step bound tight.
  • Workhorse example: $(\mathbb{E}X)^2 \le \mathbb{E}[X^2]$ with the gap equal to $\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2 \ge 0$.

Mean vs Median (L² vs L¹)

Givens: data $y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(n)}$; $F_2(c) = \sum_i (y^{(i)}-c)^2$; $F_1(c) = \sum_i |y^{(i)}-c|$.

  • L²: $F_2'(c) = -2\sum_i(y^{(i)}-c) = 0 \Rightarrow c = \bar y$; $F_2'' = 2n > 0$ ⇒ strictly convex ⇒ the mean is the unique minimizer.
  • L¹: away from data points, $F_1'(c) = \#\{y^{(i)} < c\} - \#\{y^{(i)} > c\}$ (sign count). Minimized where counts balance ⇒ any median; with even $n$, an interval of minimizers.
  • Counterexample to "always unique": data $\{0, 1\}$ — every $c \in [0,1]$ minimizes $F_1$.

Reference Facts ADDED

Multivariate Gaussian density

$$\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\Sigma) = \frac{\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12 (x-\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right)}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\,|\Sigma|^{1/2}}$$

Needed verbatim for GMM responsibilities and the E-step.

Variance of sums

$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\sum_i X_i\right) = \sum_i \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + \sum_{i\ne j}\operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j)$$

With $\operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\operatorname{Corr} = \operatorname{Cov}/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y)$ — the exact toolkit for the bagging formula.

Bootstrap 63%

$$\left(1 - \tfrac1n\right)^n \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} e^{-1} \approx 0.368$$

So a bootstrap sample covers $\approx 63.2\%$ of unique points in expectation.

Differentiation under the integral

$$\partial_\eta \!\int p(y;\eta)\,dy = \int \partial_\eta p(y;\eta)\,dy$$

Applied to $\int p = 1$: yields $a'(\eta) = \mathbb{E}[Y]$, and differentiating again, $a''(\eta) = \operatorname{Var}(Y)$.

Kernels, Exam Patterns & Neural Nets

Kernel Trick

  • Recipe: (1) rewrite the algorithm so features appear only via inner products $\langle\phi(x), \phi(x')\rangle$; (2) swap in $K(x, x')$. Never materialize $\phi$.
  • Representer / span argument: under SGD from $\theta = 0$, every update adds multiples of $\phi(x^{(i)})$, so $\theta = \sum_i \beta_i\, \phi(x^{(i)})$ always — predictions need only $\sum_i \beta_i K(x^{(i)}, x)$.
  • Linear kernel = no-op: $K(x,x') = x^\top x'$ is the identity feature map; "kernelizing" with it recovers the original algorithm exactly.
  • Kernelized logistic: substitute $\theta^\top\phi(x) = \sum_i\beta_i K(x^{(i)},x)$ into the sigmoid; the update becomes an update on $\beta$.
  • SVM dual is the native inner-product model; separability + max-margin + regularization tell one story: the margin term plays the role L2 plays for logistic on separable data.
USE IT FOR — nonlinear decision boundaries without hand-designing features, especially small-to-medium $n$ with large/implicit $d$: text classification with bag-of-words, bioinformatics sequences.

Runtimes & Linearity-in-What

  • SGD: $O(dmw)$ ($d$ features, $m$ examples per pass, $w$ passes); one epoch costs the same as one batch-GD step. SGD wants smaller / decaying $\alpha$ (gradient noise).
  • Linearity test: ask "linear in $\theta$ or linear in $x$?" — models can be linear in parameters with wildly nonlinear features (polynomials, $\sin x$). Normalizing inputs to the sphere changes gradients off the sphere but not predictions on it (parts d/e pattern).

Neural Networks PS2-5/6

The famous gradient (softmax + cross-entropy, logits $t$, one-hot $e_y$):

$$\nabla_t\, \ell_{CE} = p - e_y, \qquad p = \operatorname{softmax}(t)$$

Derivation: $\ell_{CE} = -t_y + \log\sum_s e^{t_s}$; $\partial_j(-t_y) = -\mathbb{1}\{j = y\}$; $\partial_j \log\sum_s e^{t_s} = p_j$.

Full 2-layer backprop set ADDED · PS2-5b (batch $B$; $Z^{[1]} = XW^{[1]}+b^{[1]}$, $A = \sigma(Z^{[1]})$, $Z^{[2]} = AW^{[2]}+b^{[2]}$, $\hat Y = \operatorname{softmax}(Z^{[2]})$):

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[2]}} = \tfrac1B(\hat Y - Y) \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial W^{[2]}} = A^\top \frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[2]}} \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial b^{[2]}} = \text{col-sum}\,\frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[2]}}$$ $$\frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[1]}} = \left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[2]}} W^{[2]\top}\right)\!\odot A \odot (1 - A) \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial W^{[1]}} = X^\top \frac{\partial J}{\partial Z^{[1]}}$$
Practical traps ADDED: subtract the row-max of logits before exponentiating in softmax (shift invariance, kills overflow); add $\epsilon$ inside CE logs; never regularize biases; never include the L2 penalty in the reported/plotted loss (it belongs in gradients only; Frobenius gradient is $2\lambda W$); touch the test set exactly once.
  • Step-function construction PS2-6b: each hidden step neuron = one half-plane; a triangle = AND of 3 half-planes via an output neuron counting $h_1+h_2+h_3 \ge 3$ (mind strict-vs-non-strict inequality at the boundary).
  • Linear-collapse impossibility PS2-6c: linear hidden activations compose to a single linear map ⇒ one line as decision boundary ⇒ can't shatter a surrounded cluster. This is the "why nonlinearities" argument.
USE IT FOR — perception problems where the features themselves must be learned: classifying MNIST digit images (the PS2 task), speech, anything where raw pixels/signals beat hand-built features.

PCA NEW SECTION · PS3-2

Two Views, One Vector

Givens: data $x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(n)}$ standardized (zero mean, unit variance per coordinate); unit vector $u$ ($u^\top u = 1$).

$$\arg\min_{\|u\|=1} \sum_i \left\|x^{(i)} - f_u(x^{(i)})\right\|^2 \;=\; \arg\max_{\|u\|=1} \sum_i (u^\top x^{(i)})^2 \;=\; \text{first principal component}$$
0

The plan. First find the closest point to $x$ on the line $\{\alpha u\}$ (the projection lemma). Then rewrite total reconstruction error so that minimizing it is visibly the same as maximizing projected variance.

1

Projection lemma. $r(\alpha) = \|x - \alpha u\|^2 = \|x\|^2 - 2\alpha\, u^\top x + \alpha^2$ (using $u^\top u = 1$). Then $r'(\alpha) = -2u^\top x + 2\alpha = 0 \Rightarrow \alpha^* = u^\top x$, and $r'' = 2 > 0$ (strictly convex, unique minimum). So $$f_u(x) = (u^\top x)\,u.$$

2

Pythagorean split. With $c = u^\top x$: $\|x - cu\|^2 = \|x\|^2 - 2c\,(u^\top x) + c^2 = \|x\|^2 - c^2$.

3

Swap min for max. $\sum_i \|x^{(i)} - f_u(x^{(i)})\|^2 = \underbrace{\sum_i \|x^{(i)}\|^2}_{\text{constant } K} - \sum_i (u^\top x^{(i)})^2$. Dropping the constant and flipping sign: minimizing reconstruction error $\equiv$ maximizing $\sum_i (u^\top x^{(i)})^2$.

4

Recognize the variance. Since the data have zero mean, $\frac1n\sum_i (u^\top x^{(i)})^2$ is the empirical variance of the projections — the quantity the first PC maximizes by definition. $\blacksquare$

Argmin toolkit on display: additive constants and positive scalings never move an argmin/argmax, and $\min(-f) = -\max(f)$. The zero-mean assumption is precisely what turns "sum of squared projections" into "variance."
  • $k$-dimensional version (state, don't prove): the best rank-$k$ subspace in the least-squares sense is the span of the top-$k$ principal components (eigenvectors of the empirical covariance with largest eigenvalues).
  • Connection to Tab 1: the empirical covariance $\frac1n X^\top X$ is symmetric PSD ⇒ spectral theorem gives the orthonormal PC basis for free.
USE IT FOR — compressing/visualizing high-dimensional data before anything else: projecting 784-dim MNIST to 2-D to see cluster structure, denoising, decorrelating features as preprocessing for other models.

Loss Functions → Models NEW SECTION

The master principle: a model is just $\arg\min_\theta \sum_i \ell(h_\theta(x^{(i)}), y^{(i)})$ over a hypothesis class. Choosing the loss is choosing the model — and almost every loss in this course is secretly the negative log-likelihood of a noise model: squared $\leftrightarrow$ Gaussian, absolute $\leftrightarrow$ Laplace, cross-entropy $\leftrightarrow$ Bernoulli/Categorical, weighted squared $\leftrightarrow$ heteroscedastic Gaussian. Add a penalty and MLE becomes MAP.

Cross-Entropy, Properly Defined ADDED

Givens: true distribution $p$, predicted distribution $q$ over $K$ classes.

$$H(p, q) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k \log q_k \qquad\qquad H(p,q) = H(p) + \operatorname{KL}(p \,\|\, q)$$
  • One-hot labels: $p = e_y$ collapses the sum to a single term: $\ell_{CE} = -\log q_y$ — "penalize the log-probability you gave the true class." Since $H(e_y) = 0$, minimizing CE $=$ minimizing KL $=$ pushing $q$ toward the label.
  • Binary case ($K = 2$, $q_1 = h$): recovers $-[y\log h + (1-y)\log(1-h)]$ — logistic regression's loss is CE with a sigmoid $q$.
  • CE $=$ NLL: summing $-\log q_{y^{(i)}}$ over data is exactly the negative log-likelihood of a categorical model — so "train with cross-entropy" and "do MLE" are the same sentence.
  • Log-sum-exp form + gradient: with logits $t$, $\ell_{CE} = -t_y + \log\sum_s e^{t_s}$ and $\nabla_t\,\ell_{CE} = p - e_y$ (derivation in Tab 8). Numerically: subtract $\max_s t_s$ before exponentiating.
Surrogate-loss picture: 0–1 loss is the target; CE, hinge, and exponential loss are the differentiable stand-ins that upper-bound it. Each surrogate's shape leaves a fingerprint on the model: CE → probabilities, hinge → margins & sparsity in support vectors, exponential → aggressive reweighting of mistakes (AdaBoost).

Trap Watch List

The distilled "watch list" — every recurring trap from the psets in one place. Scan this the night before.

1
Shrinkage ensures termination, not improvement. Depth caps / min-leaf-size stop the tree; they don't make splits better.
2
Monotone decrease $\ne$ convergence. Need bounded below + Monotone Convergence Theorem — and even then $J$ converges to some value, not necessarily the min. (3 sightings.)
3
Stopping $\ne$ converging. ADDED Two flavors: $|\Delta J| < \epsilon$ fooled by a constant-$J$ oscillation at the critical step size (PS1-1c); $\|\Delta\theta\|$ rules trivially triggered by decaying learning rates (PS2-3c).
4
PD, not PSD, buys uniqueness/invertibility. PSD gives convexity; PD gives one answer. And symmetric $\not\Rightarrow$ PSD (adjacency matrix, eigenvalues $\pm1$).
5
$\nabla_w w^\top A w = (A + A^\top)w$. Writing $2A\theta$ requires symmetry — say so or lose the point.
6
$\alpha$ is fixed in semi-supervised EM. The objective is linear in $\alpha$; "optimizing" it degenerates.
7
Dummy-index collisions & function-argument smuggling. Don't reuse a summation index inside a nested sum; don't let $\theta$ sneak inside a function you're differentiating "as constant."
8
"Always unique" → hunt a tiny counterexample. L1 minimizers on $\{0,1\}$: an entire interval. Two-point datasets kill many "always" claims.
9
Boosting $\ne$ resampling. Boosting reweights the same data (bias killer); bagging resamples (variance killer).
10
WLS: the $\tfrac12$ lives inside $W$. ADDED $W_{ii} = \tfrac12 w^{(i)}$ when matching $J = (X\theta-y)^\top W(X\theta-y)$ to $\tfrac12\sum w^{(i)}(\cdot)^2$.
11
Never regularize biases; never report the penalty in the loss. ADDED L2 enters gradients only ($2\lambda W$ Frobenius / $\lambda\theta$ vector); plotted loss is the raw objective.
12
