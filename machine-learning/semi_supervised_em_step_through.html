1

Two Datasets, One Model

The economic fact behind semi-supervised learning: labels are expensive, data is cheap. A hospital holds 50,000 cell scans and a pathologist who can annotate 40 a day. So the realistic dataset is a mountain of unlabeled points and a molehill of labeled ones.

The running example for this whole worksheet: tumor screening. Each scan is summarized by two measured features — say, average nucleus size and cell density — so every scan is a point in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The hidden variable $z$ is the cell type: benign or malignant. The unlabeled mountain is the 50,000 scans nobody has examined; the labeled molehill is the handful the pathologist has read and tagged. Every plot, formula, and button in the tabs ahead is this one scenario.

In symbols:

Unlabeled ($n$ large):   $\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}\}$ — we observe $x$, the label $z$ is hidden.

Labeled ($\tilde n$ small):   $\{(\tilde x^{(1)}, \tilde z^{(1)}), \dots, (\tilde x^{(\tilde n)}, \tilde z^{(\tilde n)})\}$ — both observed. The tilde is pure bookkeeping: it marks “from the labeled dataset,” nothing more.

Purely supervised learning uses only the molehill and discards the mountain; purely unsupervised learning uses the mountain but cannot attach meaning to what it finds. Semi-supervised learning is the refusal to waste either.

The model’s story of how data is born

A Gaussian mixture model (GMM) tells a two-stage generative story about every point:

Stage 1: nature secretly rolls a weighted die to pick a cluster: $z \sim$ the mixing weights. In the running example: the tissue simply is benign or malignant — that fact exists before any measurement.

Stage 2: given the roll, $x$ is drawn from that cluster’s Gaussian bell — its typical location (mean), spread and tilt (covariance). In the example: a malignant cell’s nucleus size and density scatter around the malignant profile; a benign cell’s around the benign one.

Fitting the model means recovering the bells and the die’s weights — the parameters $\theta$ — from data where the roll $z$ was usually not recorded: learning what benign and malignant cells look like in feature space, plus how common each type is, mostly from scans whose diagnosis nobody wrote down. It is the K-means cloud upgraded twice over: each cluster is a full ellipse with shape and size instead of a bare centroid, and membership is graded — a point between two bells belongs 70/25/5, not 100/0/0.

Notation: $p(x, z; \theta)$ is the joint (roll and draw); $p(x;\theta) = \sum_z p(x, z; \theta)$ is the marginal — if you didn’t see the roll, you account for the point by summing over every roll it could have been. That sum-inside is where all the trouble in tab 4 comes from.

Common misconception: the clusters found are not automatically your categories. An unsupervised mixture fit finds whatever structure dominates the data — which might be “bright vs. dim lighting” rather than “healthy vs. malignant.” And even a perfect fit returns anonymous clusters: the likelihood is unchanged by permuting cluster names, so the data alone can never say which ellipse is the malignant one. These two failures — wrong structure, nameless structure — are precisely what a handful of labels repairs (tab 2).

Unlabeled Data Draws the Map; Labels Write the Place Names

The puzzle: the pathologist annotated six scans — three benign (green), three malignant (orange). How could six annotations plus 190 unread scans beat a classifier trained on six annotations alone? Because the unread scans, en masse, reveal where the two cell populations actually live in feature space — their shapes, their extents, and the low-density valley between them (few cells are ambiguous between the two profiles). The annotations then do the one job the cloud cannot: say which population is the malignant one.

What a handful of labels buys

Names. The ellipse containing the pathologist’s malignant examples is the malignant cluster — the permutation symmetry of the unsupervised fit is broken, and the model’s clusters acquire clinical meaning. Without this, a perfect fit still can’t answer the only question that matters: which cluster do I biopsy?

Steering. Labels pull the fit toward the categorization you care about, rather than whatever structure happens to dominate the data (lighting, background, sensor noise).

Anchoring. Labels penalize local optima in which a labeled point would sit absurdly far from its own named cluster — steering EM away from valleys that merge or split the true categories.

The load-bearing assumption. All of this works only if the model’s clusters align with your categories — equivalently, if the true class boundary runs through low-density territory. When the categories cut through a dense blob, or the dominant structure is the wrong one, the mountain of unlabeled data confidently reinforces the wrong map, and semi-supervised learning can do worse than discarding the unlabeled data. More data only helps if the model’s story about the data is roughly true.

3

Semi-Supervised EM, One Half-Step at a Time

What you are watching below: the model learning what benign and malignant cells look like — each ellipse is one cell type’s typical feature profile, refined lap by lap, guided by 190 unread scans and six pathologist annotations. The machinery is the same alternation as K-means, run softly and uphill. The objective being climbed is the semi-supervised log-likelihood

$$\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}(\theta) = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{z^{(i)}} p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}_{\ell_{\text{unsup}}:\ \text{fractional voters}} \;+\; \alpha \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde n} \log p(\tilde x^{(i)}, \tilde z^{(i)}; \theta)}_{\ell_{\text{sup}}:\ \text{committed voters}}$$

E-step (soft Assign): for each unlabeled point, compute its posterior responsibilities $Q_i(z) = p(z \mid x^{(i)}; \theta)$ — graded beliefs, not hard walls. The labeled points need no beliefs: they know. M-step (soft Update): refit the ellipses with every point voting — unlabeled points fractionally by belief, labeled points fully and immovably for their named cluster, at $\alpha\times$ volume. Watch $\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}$ in the readout: it must rise (or stall) with every full step — monotone ascent, the mirror image of K-means’ descending $J$.

Same skeleton as K-means, three upgrades. Hard assignment (nearest centroid) became a posterior distribution; the centroid (a mean) became a full Gaussian (mean + covariance + weight); descent of a distortion $J$ became ascent of a likelihood $\ell$. The chicken-and-egg survives intact: good beliefs need good parameters, good parameters need good beliefs — so alternate, and the theory tab proves each lap can only help. And the local-minima story survives too: re-clicking Initialize can land a different fit, which is exactly why the labels’ anchoring matters.

4

Three Objectives, One Structural Problem

Unsupervised: the hard one

$$\ell_{\text{unsup}}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{z^{(i)}} p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)$$
The 50,000 unread scans. A log of a sum: because the diagnosis $z$ is hidden, each scan’s probability sums over both cell types it could be — and the log cannot pass through a sum. This single structural annoyance is the entire reason EM exists.

Supervised: the easy one

$$\ell_{\text{sup}}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{\tilde n} \log p(\tilde x^{(i)}, \tilde z^{(i)}; \theta)$$
The pathologist’s annotated scans. No inner sum: the diagnosis $\tilde z$ is written down, so there is nothing to sum over — plug in the actual joint. This term is directly maximizable and never needs EM’s machinery.

The weighted marriage

$$\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}(\theta) = \ell_{\text{unsup}}(\theta) + \alpha\,\ell_{\text{sup}}(\theta)$$

$\alpha$ is a hyperparameter — chosen before the run, not learned; the same status as $k$ in K-means. It is the trust dial between the pathologist and the population — how loudly each expert annotation speaks against the anonymous evidence of the unread scans:

$\alpha = 0$: the labels are decoration — plain unsupervised EM, anonymous clusters.  •  $\alpha$ huge: the mountain is nearly ignored — back to few-point supervised fitting with its shaky boundary.  •  In between: labels typically get upweighted beyond their headcount, precisely because $\tilde n \ll n$ and each label carries semantics no unlabeled point can.

Try all four $\alpha$ buttons in tab 3 and watch the ellipses renegotiate.

The E and M steps, labeled

E-step — for each unlabeled $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, set beliefs to the current posterior:

$$Q_i^{(t)}(z^{(i)}) := p(z^{(i)} \mid x^{(i)}; \theta^{(t)})$$

The E-step runs only over unlabeled points: the labeled ones don’t need beliefs about $\tilde z$ — they know.

M-step — refit parameters against beliefs plus labels:

$$\theta^{(t+1)} := \arg\max_\theta \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \sum_{z^{(i)}} Q_i^{(t)}(z^{(i)}) \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i^{(t)}(z^{(i)})} \right) + \alpha \left( \sum_{i=1}^{\tilde n} \log p(\tilde x^{(i)}, \tilde z^{(i)}; \theta) \right) \right]$$

First bracket: the fractional voters, each unlabeled point’s possible $z$-values weighted by belief — this $\log\frac{p}{Q}$ form is the tractable lower bound (ELBO) built in tab 5, and its purpose is that the log now hits a product, dissolving the log-of-sum problem. Second bracket: the committed voters, entering unmodified at $\alpha\times$ volume — this term was never hard, so it never needed a surrogate.

Superscript discipline is the trap of this topic. The M-step deliberately mixes $Q^{(t)}$ (old beliefs) with the new $\theta$ being optimized. Every $Q$ and every $\theta$ carries its iteration index, always — writing $Q^{(t+1)}$ where $Q^{(t)}$ belongs silently breaks the convergence proof in tab 5.

5

Why Every Lap Can Only Help (Part (a)’s Skeleton)

0

The plan. Build a tractable surrogate that (i) sits below $\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}$ everywhere, (ii) touches it at the current $\theta^{(t)}$, and (iii) is exactly what the M-step maximizes. Then “true objective $\ge$ surrogate at new point $\ge$ surrogate at old point $=$ true objective at old point” gives monotone ascent in three moves. The same theorem shape as K-means’ convergence — run uphill.

1

Name the surrogate. For any distributions $Q = \{Q_i\}$ over the hidden $z$’s, define the lower bound (ELBO) and the full M-step objective:

$$\mathcal{L}(Q, \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{z^{(i)}} Q_i(z^{(i)}) \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z^{(i)}; \theta)}{Q_i(z^{(i)})}, \qquad F(\theta) = \mathcal{L}(Q^{(t)}, \theta) + \alpha\,\ell_{\text{sup}}(\theta)$$

The M-step is by definition $\theta^{(t+1)} = \arg\max_\theta F(\theta)$. The supervised term enters $F$ unmodified — it never needed a surrogate.

2

Lemma A (the bound): $\ell_{\text{unsup}}(\theta) \ge \mathcal{L}(Q, \theta)$ for every $Q$ and $\theta$.

The engine is Jensen’s inequality for the concave $\log$: for a random variable $W$, $\log \mathbb{E}[W] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log W]$ — the log of an average beats the average of the logs. Inside each troublesome $\log \sum_z$, multiply and divide by $Q_i(z)$ to expose an expectation, then push the log inside at the price of “$\ge$”:

$$\log \sum_{z} p(x^{(i)}, z; \theta) = \log \sum_{z} Q_i(z)\,\frac{p(x^{(i)}, z; \theta)}{Q_i(z)} \;\ge\; \sum_{z} Q_i(z) \log \frac{p(x^{(i)}, z; \theta)}{Q_i(z)}$$

Sum over $i$ and Lemma A is proved. The multiply-and-divide-by-$Q$ move is the single most reusable trick in this corner of ML — it converts a log-of-sum into an expectation, which Jensen then converts into a tractable sum-of-logs.

3

Lemma B (tightness — the load-bearing equality condition): with the E-step’s choice $Q_i^{(t)}(z) = p(z \mid x^{(i)}; \theta^{(t)})$, the bound is exact at $\theta^{(t)}$:   $\mathcal{L}(Q^{(t)}, \theta^{(t)}) = \ell_{\text{unsup}}(\theta^{(t)})$.

Derivation: substitute the posterior into the ratio and use $p(x, z) = p(z \mid x)\,p(x)$ to collapse it:

$$\frac{p(x^{(i)}, z; \theta^{(t)})}{p(z \mid x^{(i)}; \theta^{(t)})} = p(x^{(i)}; \theta^{(t)}) \quad \text{— no } z \text{ left inside the log}$$

Then $\sum_z Q_i(z) = 1$ finishes it. This lemma is why the E-step chooses the posterior specifically: any other $Q$ leaves a gap between surrogate and truth, and the chain in Step 4 would leak.

4

The sandwich chain — part (a)’s answer

Three moves, each citing exactly one thing:

$$\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}(\theta^{(t+1)}) \;\underset{\text{Lemma A}}{\ge}\; F(\theta^{(t+1)}) \;\underset{\text{M-step argmax}}{\ge}\; F(\theta^{(t)}) \;\underset{\text{Lemma B}}{=}\; \ell_{\text{semi-sup}}(\theta^{(t)}) \qquad \blacksquare$$

The supervised term rides through every link untouched — it appears identically on both sides of each comparison, so no inequality is ever applied to it. (The argument never even uses $\alpha \ge 0$: $\alpha\,\ell_{\text{sup}}$ is only carried, never bounded.)

5

The coda. Monotone ascent plus a bounded-above objective (when it is) gives convergence of the objective values — to a stationary point, not the global maximum. Different initializations flow into different fits: exactly the K-means caveat, and exactly why the labels’ anchoring in tab 2 earns its keep. Picture the surrogate $F$ as a floor pressed up against the true objective at the current point: maximizing the floor cannot take you below where floor touches ceiling, so every lap rises or stalls. In the running example: each lap refines the benign and malignant profiles, and this theorem is the guarantee that a lap can never make the combined fit — population evidence plus pathologist — worse than it was.

The whole proof as one picture: touch, climb the bound, repeat

The picture is a curve and a family of touch-at-a-point bounds. $\ell_{\text{semi-sup}}$ is the intractable curve we want to climb; the surrogate $\mathcal{L}(Q, \cdot) + \alpha\,\ell_{\text{sup}}$ is a tractable floor lying entirely beneath it (Lemma A). The E-step chooses the one $Q$ that makes the floor touch the curve at the current position (Lemma B) — that is the entire job of setting $Q_i$ to the posterior. The M-step then walks to the top of the floor. Since the walk started at a touch point and the floor never pokes above the curve, wherever it lands the curve is at least as high as where it began — the sandwich, drawn.

The K-means correspondence, made exact. K-means is “hard EM”:

soft posterior $Q_i$  ↔  all-or-nothing nearest-centroid assignment  •  Gaussian (mean + covariance + weight)  ↔  bare centroid  •  ascend $\ell$  ↔  descend $J$  •  ELBO touching at $\theta^{(t)}$  ↔  each half-step exactly minimizing its block. One flip to keep sign discipline about: every inequality in this worksheet points $\ge$; every one in the K-means worksheet pointed $\le$.

Check Yourself

1

Whose beliefs? The E-step runs only over $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ — the unlabeled points. State precisely why the labeled points are excluded.

The E-step computes beliefs about a hidden variable — a posterior $p(z \mid x)$ is only meaningful when $z$ is unobserved. For a labeled point, $\tilde z$ is data, not a hypothesis: its “posterior” would be a point mass on the recorded label. Mechanically, its full joint $\log p(\tilde x, \tilde z; \theta)$ enters the objective directly with no inner sum, so there is nothing for a $Q$ to smooth over.

2

The dial’s endpoints. Describe what the algorithm becomes at $\alpha = 0$, and what failure mode returns as $\alpha \to \infty$.

$\alpha = 0$: the supervised term vanishes and this is exactly classical unsupervised EM — clusters fit but anonymous, with the permutation symmetry (and its label-switching ambiguity) fully restored. $\alpha \to \infty$: the unlabeled term becomes negligible, and the fit is driven by $\tilde n$ points alone — the shaky few-sample boundary of tab 2’s left panel, with the mountain of structure wasted. The useful regime is between, usually with labels upweighted beyond headcount because $\tilde n \ll n$.

3

The anonymous-clusters problem. A perfectly fit unsupervised GMM finds three clean clusters in cell-scan data. Why can no amount of additional unlabeled data identify which cluster is malignant — and what is the one-word name for the symmetry responsible?

The likelihood is invariant under permutation of the cluster indices: relabel clusters $1 \leftrightarrow 3$, swap their parameters, and every probability the model assigns to the data is unchanged. Since all data enters through that likelihood, no quantity computable from unlabeled data can distinguish the relabelings — the identification is not merely hard but information-theoretically impossible. One labeled malignant example breaks the symmetry instantly: the cluster containing it is the malignant one.

4

When the mountain hurts. Give a concrete scenario in which adding unlabeled data makes the semi-supervised fit worse than using the labels alone, and name the assumption being violated.

Example: the scans cluster overwhelmingly by lighting condition (bright vs. dim), while malignancy cuts across both lighting groups. The unlabeled mountain pulls the ellipses onto the lighting structure; the few labels cannot out-vote it at moderate $\alpha$; the resulting boundary tracks lighting, not pathology — worse than a small supervised fit that at least aimed at the right target. Violated assumption: cluster–category alignment — that the model’s clusters correspond to your classes, i.e., that the true class boundary runs through low-density territory.

5

Hard EM. Modify the E-step so the algorithm becomes (a generalization of) K-means. What is gained and what is lost by the modification?

Replace the posterior with a point mass on its argmax: $Q_i(z) = 1$ for the single most probable cluster, $0$ elsewhere — hard assignment. With shared spherical covariances and equal weights, the resulting alternation is K-means. Gained: speed and simplicity (no responsibilities to carry). Lost: the graded information near boundaries — a 51/49 point votes as if it were 100/0, so uncertainty is destroyed rather than propagated, and the tight ELBO-touches-truth guarantee weakens (hard EM ascends a different, cruder surrogate). The soft version keeps every point’s honest ambivalence on the books.
