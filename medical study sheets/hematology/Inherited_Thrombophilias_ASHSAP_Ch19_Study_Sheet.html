One-Page Cheat Sheet

This sheet covers ASH-SAP Chapter 19, Inherited thrombophilias (Samuelson Bannow, p. 217–225): the five inherited thrombophilias, their epidemiology and mechanisms, how each is assayed and what corrupts the assay, and the 2023 ASH testing recommendations with the per-1000 arithmetic behind them. If you can explain every tile below in your own words, you know the chapter.

Test only if it changes management p. 217, 222

Extended anticoagulation, prophylaxis (pregnancy, cancer, minor transient risk), or the estrogen decision. Risks of testing: cost · false reassurance · anxiety · poor test performance. Overtesting >> undertesting (Choosing Wisely 2016: no testing after VTE with a major transient risk factor). All but one ASH 2023 recommendation is conditional; shared decision-making rules.

Zygosity · penetrance · first vs recurrent p. 217–218

Heterozygous < homozygous; compound heterozygous = het FVL + het PGM. Penetrance (variant prevalence vs disease prevalence) is incomplete for all. Thrombophilias drive first VTE, not recurrence — recurrence follows the provoking circumstances.

Ancestry p. 218–219

FVL / PGM / compound = European (FVL 3–8% Europeans, 1.2% African Americans; PGM Southern European, 2% US, 0.5% African American; rare in native African and Asian groups). East Asian VTE patients: PS 8.3%, PC 7.1%, AT 3.8%. Homozygous AT deficiency is incompatible with life.

Mechanisms p. 219

FVL: Arg506 aPC-cleavage site lost → factor Va persists. PGM: G→A outside coding region → normal prothrombin at ~133%. PC: vit K–dependent zymogen that cleaves Va + VIIIa; homozygous = neonatal DIC / purpura fulminans. PS: cofactor for aPC and TFPI; free = active, C4b-BP-bound = inactive (60:40). AT: serpin vs IIa, Xa, IXa (XIa, XIIa); heparin ×1000; defects at reactive or heparin-binding site.

Compounding risk p. 219–220

First-degree relative with VTE: 2–4×, independent of any thrombophilia (young / unprovoked / >1 relative = more); unknown effect on recurrence. Pregnancy: het PGM 3–15×, het FVL 5–8×, homo FVL 17–34×, FVL + PGM 8–47×. Counsel progestin-only (non-depot MPA) over CHCs; patch and ring carry the same risk as the pill.

Table 19-2 pregnancy prophylaxis p. 220

Homo FVL, FVL + PGM: ante + post, any family history. Homo PGM: ante + post with FH; post only without. AT: ante + post with FH; none without. PC, PS: post only, with FH only. Het FVL, het PGM: none. Antepartum is rare because daily VTE risk is far higher postpartum.

Table 19-4 what corrupts the assay p. 221

Acute VTE → PC, PS, AT low (consumption). Heparin → AT low. Warfarin → PC, PS low. DOAC → function-based PC/PS/AT assays. Pregnancy → free PS and AT low, aPC sensitivity down. Liver disease → AT, PC, PS low. Chemo (MTX, L-asparaginase, 5-FU) → AT, PC, PS. Verify every abnormal functional result on a new sample.

Assay of choice p. 221–222

FVL: clot-based aPC-resistance ratio (aPTT/PT/RVV ± aPC), confirm in FV-depleted plasma, genotype all positives (estrogen raises aPCR); or PCR first-line. PGM: genetic only (factor II activity insensitive). PC: chromogenic (snake venom, OD). PS: clot-based activity (most sensitive) or free antigen (misses type II). AT: chromogenic anti-Xa/IIa with UFH. Type I = activity = antigen; type II = activity < antigen; AT IIa reactive site, IIb heparin-binding (low risk). Never measure AT on heparin.

VTE patients: test or not? p. 223–224

Test (→ indefinite AC if +): nonsurgical major transient factor; pregnancy/postpartum; COC-associated; CVT or splanchnic VT where AC would otherwise stop. Don’t test: surgery-provoked (10/1000 recurrence vs 5–15/1000 bleeds); unprovoked (100/1000 — anticoagulate anyway); unspecified; CVT/splanchnic where AC continues regardless.

No VTE: family history, estrogen, pregnancy, cancer p. 223–225

Minor transient risk factor (immobility, minor injury, illness, infection) + FH of VTE: test for AT/PC/PS (50/1000 risk; ~20 prevented vs 2.18 bleeds); FVL/PGM (15/1000; ~5 prevented) → no recommendation. CHCs: strong NO for general population; NO for HRT, unknown or FVL/PGM family thrombophilia; YES for FH + AT/PC/PS deficiency (10.49–19.39 fewer VTE/1000). Pregnancy planning: FH + homo FVL / homo PGM (sibling) / FVL + PGM / AT → test, ante + post; PC/PS → either. Cancer: ambulatory low/intermediate risk + first-degree FH → test, prophylaxis.

How ASH 2023 decided p. 222–223

Numerical ledger: VTE prevented vs major bleeds caused, at low (5) vs high (15) bleeds per 1000/yr. Ignored: health anxiety, nonmajor bleeding (HMB in up to 70% of anticoagulated menstruating people), relatives, preference, lifestyle. Evidence low quality / high bias → conditional, except the strong rec against general-population COC testing.

Numbers to know. Prevalence: AT 0.02% (1 in 500–5000), PC 0.2% (1 in 500–600; homozygous 1 in 1 million), PS 0.03–0.13% (1 in 800–3000, true value unknown), FVL het 3–7% (3–8% Europeans, 1.2% African Americans; homozygous 1 in 500–1600), PGM het 0.7–4% (2% US, 0.5% African American; homozygous 1 in 4000), compound 0.01% (2.4–3.9% North African, none West/South African). East Asian VTE: PC 7.1%, PS 8.3%, AT 3.8%. Mechanism: PGM prothrombin 133%; PS bound:free 60:40; heparin potentiates AT 1000-fold; FVL site Arg506. Family history 2–4×. Pregnancy: het PGM 3–15×, het FVL 5–8×, homo FVL 17–34×, FVL + PGM 8–47×. Bleeding risk 5 (low) / 15 (high) per 1000/yr. Recurrence: surgery 10/1000 yr 1 (test-and-continue: 4 fewer VTE, 2–7 more bleeds); unprovoked 100/1000. Pregnancy/COC VTE: 21 prevented vs 2–7 bleeds (13 of 21 in FVL/PGM). Guidelines: Choosing Wisely 2016; ASH thrombophilia testing 2023; 1 strong recommendation.

1

Foundations & Epidemiology

The four ideas that frame the whole chapter p. 217–218

Before any individual thrombophilia makes sense, the chapter insists on four framing concepts. Every later recommendation (test / don’t test, prophylaxis / no prophylaxis) is built on them.

Thrombophilia = hypercoagulable state

A hereditary or acquired predisposition to develop thrombosis. This chapter covers the inherited ones: factor V Leiden (FVL), prothrombin gene mutation 20210 (PGM), protein C (PC), protein S (PS) and antithrombin (AT) deficiency. Testing recommendations have “fluctuated in recent years”, so the hematologist must know the nature, limitations and interpretation of the tests, not just the list. p. 217

Zygosity: heterozygous < homozygous; “compound / double heterozygous”

For FVL and PGM, heterozygotes are at lower risk than homozygotes. Compound (double) heterozygosity is the term, especially in the obstetric literature, for someone heterozygous for both FVL and PGM — it behaves like a high-risk thrombophilia (first-VTE RR 7–20). A test will try to get you to treat “heterozygous FVL” and “homozygous FVL” as the same thing; they are not. p. 217

Penetrance (and why it is incomplete)

Penetrance is the gap between the prevalence of the genetic feature and the prevalence of the actual disease state. It is incomplete for all inherited thrombophilias: most carriers never clot, and in most cases only a small percentage ever will. Contrast with prevalence (how many people carry the variant) — a common exam swap. p. 217–218

Risk of a FIRST VTE vs risk of RECURRENCE

Inherited thrombophilias matter far more for the first event than for recurrence. Recurrence is dictated by the circumstances of the initial event: was there a provoking factor, how strong was it, and was it reversible? This is why testing after a first VTE has limited utility in many scenarios. Compare Table 19-1’s two risk rows: first-event RR up to 20, recurrence RR mostly 1–2. p. 218

Epidemiology by ancestry p. 218–219

The nuance the chapter adds beyond Table 19-1 is who carries each variant. The pattern: FVL, PGM and their compound state are European-ancestry variants; PC, PS and AT deficiency are rarer overall but over-represented among East Asian VTE patients.

Factor V Leiden

Heterozygous FVL: 3–8% of European-origin populations, 1.2% of African Americans, rarely found in native African and Asian populations. Homozygous FVL: 1 in 500 to 1 in 1600 people of European origin.

Prothrombin 20210 mutation

Most common in Southern European ancestry; 0.7–4% across Europe. In the United States: 2% of the general population and 0.5% of African Americans; rare in other non-European populations. Homozygous PGM: about 1 in 4000 of European heritage.

Compound / double heterozygosity (FVL + PGM)

Follows the same European pattern. Only 2.4–3.9% of individuals with these variants are from North African countries, none from West or South African countries, and Native Americans have a similarly low prevalence.

Protein C deficiency

1 in 500 to 1 in 600 in the general population; homozygous or double-heterozygous PC deficiency about 1 in 1 million (by calculation). Among East Asian VTE patients: ~7.1%.

Protein S deficiency

Reported 1 in 800 to 1 in 3000, but the true prevalence is not known because the normal range of PS is hard to establish and diagnosis is difficult. Among East Asian VTE patients: 8.3% — the highest of the three deficiencies.

Antithrombin deficiency

1 in 500 to 1 in 5000. Variants are typically heterozygous, because homozygous AT deficiency is almost always incompatible with life. Among East Asian VTE patients: 3.8%.

Exam framing. “A patient is heterozygous for FVL. How much does this change their risk of a first VTE, and of recurrence?” Answer from Table 19-1: first VTE RR 3–5 (and remember carriers are common, 3–7% of the general population, so most never clot — incomplete penetrance); recurrence RR only ~1.4, so the provoking circumstances of the first event, not the FVL, drive the decision about anticoagulation duration. Then contrast with homozygous FVL (RR 9–12) and compound FVL + PGM (RR 7–20).

Common exam trap: reading a high prevalence as a high risk, or a high first-event risk as a high recurrence risk. Heterozygous PGM is found in up to 4% of Europeans yet carries a recurrence RR that includes values below 1 (0.7–1.4). Only AT deficiency (1.9–2.6) and compound heterozygosity (up to 4.3) push recurrence risk meaningfully above baseline. (p. 218)

Pathophysiology & Compounding Risk

Every inherited thrombophilia is either a broken brake (PC, PS, AT deficiency; FVL) or a heavier accelerator (PGM). p. 219

The three natural anticoagulant systems are the protein C pathway (aPC with its cofactor PS cleaves factors Va and VIIIa), antithrombin (a serpin that neutralizes thrombin and the activated serine proteases), and TFPI (which PS also assists). FVL does not lower anticoagulant levels at all — it makes the substrate resistant. PGM does not touch any brake — it simply produces more prothrombin. A. Factor V Leiden — substrate resistance p. 219

Mechanism
  • A point mutation abolishes the Arg506 cleavage site on factor V, so activated protein C cannot bind to and inactivate factor V. “Perhaps one of the best understood mechanisms of thrombophilia.”
  • Consequence: factor Va continues to circulate in its activated form in increased quantities — this increase is the presumed immediate cause of the thrombotic potential. The brake (PC) is intact; the substrate ignores it. That is why the screening test is a functional aPC-resistance assay, not a PC level (Tab 3).
Clinical shape
  • Common (3–7% heterozygous), modest first-VTE risk (RR 3–5), near-baseline recurrence (1.4). Homozygotes: RR 9–12. Strong interaction with estrogen and pregnancy (below).

B. Prothrombin 20210 mutation — a quantity problem p. 219

Mechanism
  • A single missense G→A mutation that occurs outside the coding region of the prothrombin gene. The prothrombin molecule itself is not fundamentally altered; it is produced in excess, ~133% of normal, and that excess raises thrombosis risk.
  • Because the protein is normal, a factor II activity level is not a sensitive screen — genetic testing is the primary method (Tab 3).
Clinical shape
  • Heterozygous RR 2–3 for first VTE, recurrence RR 0.7–1.4. In pregnancy, heterozygous PGM raises VTE risk 3–15-fold; data on homozygotes are limited.

C. Protein C deficiency — the brake on Va and VIIIa p. 219

Mechanism
  • Like FVL, risk comes partly from inadequately inactivated factor Va — but PC is also responsible for inactivating factor VIIIa, so the deficiency removes a second brake.
Severity spectrum (a favourite exam item)
  • Severe (homozygous): disseminated intravascular coagulation and purpura fulminans shortly after birth.
  • Moderately severe: presents later, still with an increased risk of DIC in addition to VTE.
  • Heterozygous: highly variable presentation, mainly an increased VTE rate; many are asymptomatic.

D. Protein S deficiency — two cofactor jobs, two circulating states p. 219

Mechanism
  • PS has 2 roles: (1) cofactor for activated protein C, and (2) cofactor for tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), where it facilitates inactivation of factor Xa and factor VIIa.
  • PS exists in 2 states: free/active and bound to a carrier protein (C4b-binding protein)/inactive. The typical bound-to-free ratio is 60:40 and shifts with physiology (acute phase, pregnancy lower free PS). p. 221
Three ways to be PS-deficient
  • Low levels of both free and bound forms; or decreased free PS only; or decreased activity with normal antigen levels of both forms (this last one is what a free-antigen assay misses — Tab 3).
  • Severe PS deficiency may present in newborns with a poorly described prognosis; the majority have mildly decreased levels.

E. Antithrombin deficiency — the heparin-dependent serpin p. 219, 221–222

Mechanism
  • AT works by forming and clearing complexes with serine proteases — thrombin, factor Xa and factor IXa (the assay section adds XIa and XIIa). The process is inefficient in isolation and powerfully potentiated when heparan sulfate or a heparin drug binds the AT–protease complex (activity increased 1000-fold by heparin).
  • Deficiency is most commonly an altered protein structure with impaired function at the reactive site, the heparin-binding site, or both — the basis of the type IIa / IIb split in Tab 3.
Clinical shape
  • Rarest (0.02%), strongest (first-VTE RR 5–10, the only deficiency with recurrence RR clearly > 1.9). Homozygosity is almost always incompatible with life.
  • Patients may display resistance to heparin drugs because thrombin, factor X and factor IX go unhindered without AT to potentiate.

Compounding of risk factors p. 219–220

“Inherited thrombophilias do not exist in isolation, and VTE is commonly a result of an accumulation of risk factors.” Some added factors (family history) apply to all thrombophilias; others (pregnancy, exogenous estrogen) interact more strongly with specific ones.

Family history of VTE

A first-degree relative with VTE raises risk 2- to 4-fold. Risk rises further if the relative’s VTE was at a young age, was unprovoked, or if there is more than 1 affected first-degree relative. Crucially this risk is independent of a known inherited thrombophilia — so a negative thrombophilia panel must not be used to reassure someone with a strong family history. Lifestyle modification and avoiding estrogen-based contraceptives in favour of progestin-only formulations are still advisable. Whether family history predicts recurrence (and so should affect anticoagulation duration) is not known.

Pregnancy and exogenous estrogen — the fold-increases

In pregnancy: heterozygous PGM 3–15-fold, heterozygous FVL 5–8-fold, homozygous FVL 17–34-fold, compound FVL + PGM 8–47-fold; homozygous PGM: data too limited to conclude. Counsel known thrombophilia carriers and those with a family history to limit exogenous estrogen, favouring progestin-based, non–depot medroxyprogesterone formulations over combined hormonal contraceptives (CHCs). Note the ASH guideline suggests not testing to guide COC use in people with a family history of VTE with FVL or PGM — but many clinicians favour progestin-only for any first-degree relative of a VTE patient regardless of testing. p. 220

Antepartum vs postpartum prophylaxis

Postpartum or antepartum prophylaxis may be recommended for certain thrombophilias, but antepartum recommendations are rare (Table 19-2). The reason, from p. 225: the daily risk of VTE is considerably higher postpartum than antepartum, so not everyone who merits postpartum prophylaxis also benefits antepartum.

Table 19-2 — ASH thromboprophylaxis in pregnant individuals with thrombophilias p. 220

Read it as tiers. Tier 1 (homozygous FVL, compound FVL + PGM): prophylaxis antepartum and postpartum regardless of family history. Tier 2 (homozygous PGM, AT deficiency): both, but only with a positive family history (homozygous PGM still gets postpartum without). Tier 3 (PC, PS deficiency): postpartum only, and only with a family history. Tier 4 (heterozygous FVL or PGM): no prophylaxis on genotype alone.

Assays & Test Interference

Ground rules for every thrombophilia assay p. 220–221

“Accurate interpretation of thrombophilia testing depends upon an understanding of the properties and limitations of available assays.” Three rules recur through the whole section:

  1. Assay variation ranges from minor to broad. Minor = slight reagent differences between kits. Broad = genetic testing vs functional assays. Both the assay limitation and the patient’s circumstances must be considered to avoid misleading results.
  2. Functional assays have biological and analytical variability, so any abnormal functional result must be verified on a new sample. Genetic tests (FVL, PGM) do not need this.
  3. Genotyping is not routinely performed for AT, PC or PS deficiency because of the large number of causative mutations. These three are diagnosed functionally (activity assays), then antigen for subtyping.

Table 19-4 — circumstances that make thrombophilia testing inaccurate p. 221

These are the “abnormal result without a true thrombophilia” situations from the Key Points. The pattern to memorize: consumption (acute VTE, heparin) lowers levels, vitamin K antagonism lowers PC and PS, DOACs corrupt clot-based functional assays, pregnancy lowers free PS and AT and blunts aPC sensitivity, and liver disease / chemotherapy lower synthesis.

A. Factor V Leiden and prothrombin gene mutation p. 221

FVL: functional screen, then genotype
  • Two autosomal inherited coagulation factor variants raise VTE risk: FVL and PGM.
  • Sensitive commercial clot-based screening assays demonstrate resistance of factor Va cleavage by activated PC. Coagulation testing is activated with aPTT, PT or Russell’s viper venom reagents, run with and without added aPC, and the clotting times are expressed as a ratio. An abnormally low ratio = aPC resistance.
  • Specificity is improved by repeat testing of positive plasma after dilution with factor V–depleted plasma, which minimizes the impact of deficiencies, anticoagulants and high factor VIII levels.
  • aPC resistance is also increased substantially by elevated or exogenous estrogen — a phenotypic positive that is not FVL.
  • Genotyping should be performed on all aPC-resistant patients to determine heterozygous vs homozygous. Depending on patient and setting factors (e.g., insurance coverage) many clinicians and labs use mutation-specific PCR as the first-line test instead.
PGM: genetics only
  • PGM is associated with elevated prothrombin levels, but measuring factor II activity is not a sensitive screening test; genetic testing is the primary method.

The factor V Leiden testing pathway on p. 221: functional aPC-resistance screen → confirm in factor V–depleted plasma → genotype every aPC-resistant patient. PGM skips the functional step entirely.

B. Protein C deficiency p. 221

Biology recap
  • PC is a vitamin K–dependent zymogen that, once activated, controls coagulation by cleaving factors Va and VIIIa.
Preferred screen: chromogenic activity assay
  • PC is activated with snake venom; activity correlates with hydrolysis of a synthetic peptide and the change in optical density (OD).
  • Clot-based PC activity assays are an alternative but may be inaccurate because of variations in factor VIII and PS levels, FVL, inhibitory antibodies, and some anticoagulants.
  • Abnormally low activity: repeat, then may add PC antigen to classify type I (activity = antigen) vs type II (activity < antigen). Clinical significance of subclassification is unclear.
  • Chromogenic assays detect type I and type II only if the defect is in the catalytic active site; clot-based assays can also pick up abnormalities in additional PC domains.

C. Protein S deficiency p. 221

Why PS is the hardest assay
  • PS is nonfunctional when bound to complement 4b–binding protein and functional when free; free PS is the aPC cofactor. Typical bound:free = 60:40, varying with conditions; free/functional PS falls in acute-phase states and pregnancy.
  • Clot-based PS activity assays are the most sensitive screening tests, but suffer the same inaccuracies as clot-based PC testing (FVIII, FVL, antibodies, anticoagulants).
  • Alternative screen: free PS antigen concentration avoids those confounders but is insensitive to type II PS deficiency (low activity, normal free antigen).
  • Practice varies: some labs screen with activity, some with free antigen, some with both.

D. Antithrombin deficiency p. 221–222

Screen and subtype
  • AT is a serpin inhibiting thrombin and factors IXa, Xa, XIa and XIIa; activity is increased 1000-fold by heparin.
  • Most sensitive screen: chromogenic activity assay quantifying AT inhibition of factor Xa or IIa in the presence of unfractionated heparin.
  • Abnormally low: repeat, then AT antigen → type I (activity = antigen) vs type II (activity < antigen). Type I is more common than type II in symptomatic kindreds; significance of subclassification is unclear.
  • Subclassifying type II requires the chromogenic assay without heparin: type IIa = reactive-site defect; type IIb = heparin-binding defect. Type IIb carries a low thrombosis risk, but progressive AT activity assays are not readily available and typically not performed.
The heparin-resistance trap
  • AT deficiency is often suspected as a cause of heparin resistance (no therapeutic aPTT despite large heparin doses). But measuring AT during heparin therapy is rarely helpful: decreased levels are expected on heparin, so a false diagnosis may be made.

Memory hook — who lowers what. Heparin → AT (consumption, and the assay uses heparin anyway). Warfarin → PC and PS (both vitamin K–dependent; PC is the vitamin K–dependent zymogen). Pregnancy → free PS (more binding protein), AT, and aPC sensitivity. Acute VTE → PC, PS, AT (consumption of all three). Liver disease and chemo (MTX, L-asparaginase, 5-FU) → PC, PS, AT (synthesis). Common exam trap: ordering an AT level to explain heparin resistance, or a PC/PS level on a warfarinized patient, or a full panel during the acute VTE. Each yields a low value that is expected in that setting. The chapter’s rule: verify abnormal functional results on a new sample, drawn when the confounder is gone. (p. 221–222)

When to Test: ASH 2023 & Treatment Implications

Test only when the result will change management — and judge “change management” by VTE events prevented versus major bleeds caused, per 1000 patients. p. 217, 222

This is the chapter’s spine. Testing is “not without risk”: a negative test in a high-risk person (extensive family history) invites inappropriate risk-taking (choosing CHCs over safe progestin-only options; skipping prophylaxis for surgery). A positive test for a low-risk thrombophilia (heterozygous FVL or PGM) creates health anxiety in someone still far more likely never to clot, and may expose them to bleeding from prophylaxis in an unproven setting. “Overtesting is far more common than undertesting” — hence the 2016 ASH Choosing Wisely item: do not test adults with VTE in the setting of major transient risk factors. That item has never changed because no evidence supports altering management or anticoagulation duration in that setting.

Table 19-3 — reasons for and against testing p. 220

Reasons FOR testing (3)

  1. Influence on the decision to offer thromboprophylaxis.
  2. Influence on the duration of anticoagulation.
  3. A possible explanation (for patient and physician) of why thrombosis occurred.

Reasons AGAINST testing (8)

  1. Lack of therapeutic consequences even if positive.
  2. Suboptimal test performance (false positives/negatives) or misinterpretation.
  3. Poor medical advice based on results.
  4. Anxiety if positive.
  5. False sense of security if negative.
  6. Cost.
  7. Lack of impact for asymptomatic first-degree relatives (possible exception: those contemplating estrogen use or pregnancy).
  8. Impact on ability to obtain life or health insurance.

When a thrombophilia is identified, accurate education for the patient and family is critical; online resources named in the chapter include clotconnect.org and stoptheclot.org. p. 220

How the 2023 ASH thrombophilia-testing guideline was built p. 222–223

The arithmetic
  • Recommendations rest on a numerical comparison: future VTE events prevented by continuing anticoagulation (or otherwise changing management) in test-positive people vs additional major bleeds from continued anticoagulation, dichotomized by high vs low bleeding risk (low = 5, high = 15 major bleeds per 1000 patients per year). The mirror image — bleeds prevented and recurrent VTEs caused by stopping anticoagulation after a negative test — was also counted.
What was deliberately left out
  • Health anxiety; nonmajor bleeding including clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding and heavy menstrual bleeding (affects up to 70% of anticoagulated menstruating individuals); implications for relatives; patient preference; lifestyle costs of anticoagulation (profession, sports). This is why shared decision-making remains the central principle.
Evidence quality and recommendation strength
  • VTE-prevention estimates came from a meta-analysis of studies most of which individually showed no significant increase in recurrence with inherited thrombophilia; the studies are low quality with high risk of bias. So the panel made only conditional recommendations (low / very low certainty) — with one exception: the strong recommendation against testing to guide oral contraceptive use in the general population. Even where a meta-analysis finds differences, they are “often quite small”.

Individuals with a history of VTE p. 223

Rationale: identify those whose recurrence risk is high enough to need lifelong / indefinite anticoagulation. The answer depends entirely on how the first VTE was provoked.

The VTE-history half of Figure 19-1 as a decision tree, with the per-1000 arithmetic from p. 223 that produced each branch. AC = anticoagulation; COC = combined oral contraceptive.

The per-1000 arithmetic, worked p. 223, 225

Givens (all per 1000 patients)

Major bleeding on anticoagulation: low risk = 5 / yr, high risk = 15 / yr. Recurrence after surgery-provoked VTE = 10 in year 1; after unprovoked VTE = 100 in year 1. VTE risk with a minor transient risk factor: 50 (family history + AT/PC/PS deficiency) vs 15 (family history + FVL/PGM).

Surgery-provoked VTE, test-and-continue strategy: $4$ fewer recurrences vs $2$ (low bleeding risk) to $7$ (high bleeding risk) more major bleeds per 1000 — unfavourable, so do not test.

Pregnancy / postpartum / COC-associated VTE: $21$ recurrences prevented vs $2$–$7$ bleeds per 1000 per year — favourable, so test. $13/21 \approx 62\%$ of those prevented events sit in FVL/PGM carriers.

Family history + minor transient risk factor, prophylaxis strategy: AT/PC/PS deficiency prevents $19.70$–$21.25$ VTE vs $2.18$ extra major bleeds per 1000 risk episodes, i.e. $\dfrac{19.70}{2.18} \approx 9.0$ to $\dfrac{21.25}{2.18} \approx 9.7$ VTE prevented per bleed caused → test. FVL/PGM prevents only $4.84$–$5.04$ ($\approx 2.2$–$2.3$ per bleed) → no recommendation for or against.

Family history + AT/PC/PS deficiency, considering oral CHCs: avoidance in test-positives yields $10.49$–$19.39$ fewer VTE per 1000 → test. The panel could not estimate unplanned pregnancies, labeling, or lost treatment options (heavy menstrual bleeding, endometriosis, PMDD).

Planning pregnancy, family history of VTE + homozygous FVL / homozygous PGM (specifically in a sibling) / compound FVL + PGM: test, and if the result matches the relative’s, offer antepartum + postpartum prophylaxis: $9.05$–$19.35$ VTE per 1000 prevented, with $1.05$–$2.09$ major bleeds per 1000 on the other side of the ledger.

Every ASH 2023 suggestion in this chapter reduced to the same picture: green (VTE prevented by acting on a positive test) against copper (major bleeds caused). Where copper rivals green — surgery-provoked VTE — testing is not suggested. Figures from p. 223 and 225.

Individuals with NO VTE but a family history of VTE or thrombophilia p. 223

Rationale: identify people who will benefit from short-term thromboprophylaxis during a minor transient risk factor — defined as immobility, minor injury, illness or infection. The panel weighed recurrence risk by thrombophilia and by first- vs second-degree relative. The recommendation hinges on which thrombophilia runs in the family: with a family history of VTE, test for AT, PC or PS deficiency (risk 50 / 1000; favourable ratio), but neither for nor against testing when the family thrombophilia is FVL/PGM (15 / 1000) or unknown. Figure 19-1 adds: a first-degree relative with AT/PC/PS deficiency but no VTE still merits testing (for the known thrombophilia). p. 224

Individuals with estrogen-based risk factors and those planning pregnancy p. 223–225

Rationale and timing

Identify those who benefit from non-estrogen options such as progestin-only contraception. Results are not immediate and the risky situations (pregnancy, minor risk factors) can be anticipated, so testing in late adolescence or early adulthood rather than waiting for the high-risk moment is practical. Nonoral CHCs (patch, vaginal ring) carry VTE risk comparable to oral CHCs even though the panel wrote about oral contraceptives.

The ONE strong recommendation, and its weaker sibling

Strong: thrombophilia testing is NOT indicated for individuals in the general population who wish to begin oral CHCs. Weaker (conditional): suggest against testing individuals who wish to begin hormone replacement therapy. Also against: family history of heterozygous FVL/PGM but no VTE; family history of VTE with unknown thrombophilia; family history of FVL/PGM and VTE when considering COCs/HRT.

Where testing IS suggested before estrogen

Family history of VTE with known AT, PC or PS deficiency, considering oral CHCs (or HRT): avoiding CHCs in those who test positive prevents 10.49–19.39 VTE per 1000. Also: women with a family history of VTE + a known thrombophilia considering COCs (test specifically for that thrombophilia).

Planning pregnancy

Identify those who benefit from short-term prophylaxis antepartum or in the higher-risk postpartum period. Suggested: family history of VTE + homozygous FVL, homozygous PGM (specifically in a sibling), or compound heterozygosity → test and offer antepartum + postpartum prophylaxis if the result matches (9.05–19.35 VTE / 1000 prevented; 1.05–2.09 major bleeds / 1000). Antithrombin deficiency in the family → test, antepartum + postpartum. PC or PS deficiency in the family → “either test or do not test” for antepartum and postpartum guidance (Figure 19-1), while Table 19-2 gives PC/PS carriers with a family history postpartum prophylaxis only. Second-degree relatives with FVL + PGM or AT deficiency, in a woman with VTE → test for postpartum prophylaxis.

Exam framing. “Which patient should be offered thrombophilia testing?” Ask three questions in order. (1) Would a positive result change something — duration of anticoagulation, prophylaxis, or estrogen choice? If not (surgery-provoked VTE, unprovoked VTE, general-population COC or HRT), do not test. (2) If yes, does the per-1000 ledger favour acting? Pregnancy/COC-associated VTE (21 vs 2–7) and family history + AT/PC/PS deficiency (~20 vs 2.18) do; family history + FVL/PGM (~5 vs 2.18) is a wash. (3) Is the sample interpretable right now? Not during acute VTE, heparin, warfarin, DOAC, pregnancy, liver disease or chemotherapy. Then remember every answer but one is a conditional recommendation, so shared decision-making decides.

5

Examples & Applications

The chapter has no named trials; its examples are the clinical situations it walks through. Each card is one of those situations, tagged with the concept it tests. The goal is to recognize the setting and produce the chapter’s answer, not to memorize the vignette.

The neonate with skin necrosis and DIC p. 219

Homozygous protein C deficiency · severity spectrum

A newborn develops purpura fulminans and disseminated intravascular coagulation shortly after birth. This is the presentation of severe (homozygous) PC deficiency: without PC, neither factor Va nor factor VIIIa is inactivated. Moderately severe deficiency presents later but still carries DIC risk; heterozygotes mostly present with VTE or nothing at all.

Severe PS deficiency can also present in newborns, but the chapter says its prognosis is “poorly described” and most PS-deficient people have only mildly decreased levels; the DIC + purpura fulminans picture is specifically attached to PC. Homozygous AT deficiency is “almost always incompatible with life”, so it does not present as a living neonate with purpura fulminans.

“We can’t get the aPTT therapeutic” p. 219, 222

AT deficiency · heparin resistance · Table 19-4

A patient on a heparin infusion fails to reach a therapeutic aPTT despite seemingly large doses. AT deficiency is a classic explanation (heparin only works by potentiating AT 1000-fold), but the chapter’s point is what not to do: measuring AT levels during heparin therapy is rarely helpful, because heparin itself lowers AT by consumption and a false diagnosis of inherited deficiency may follow.

Off heparin and away from the acute VTE: a chromogenic anti-Xa or anti-IIa activity assay in the presence of unfractionated heparin; repeat if low; then antigen to call type I (activity = antigen) or type II (activity < antigen). Type II can be split by the no-heparin chromogenic assay into IIa (reactive site) vs IIb (heparin-binding, low thrombotic risk), though this is rarely done.

DVT three weeks after hip replacement p. 222–223

Surgery-provoked VTE · Choosing Wisely 2016 · per-1000 ledger

A strong, reversible provoking factor. Recurrence risk is 10 per 1000 in the first year, lower than the 5–15 per 1000 per year risk of major bleeding on anticoagulation, so long-term anticoagulation is unfavourable whatever a panel shows. Testing-and-continuing would give 4 fewer recurrences but 2–7 more major bleeds per 1000. Both Choosing Wisely (2016) and ASH 2023 say do not test.

That is a nonsurgical major transient risk factor, which sits in the “testing suggested” column of Figure 19-1: test, and offer indefinite anticoagulation if a thrombophilia is found. The surgical vs nonsurgical distinction is the near-miss the exam will use.

Unprovoked pulmonary embolism in a 45-year-old p. 223

Unprovoked VTE · testing suggested against

Recurrence after unprovoked VTE is 100 per 1000 in the first year in an unselected population — high enough to exceed any bleeds prevented by stopping anticoagulation in those who test negative. Since the result cannot lower the recurrence estimate enough to justify stopping, the panel suggests against testing. Management (indefinite anticoagulation) is decided by the unprovoked nature of the event, not by genotype.

Both are “test won’t change management” — but from opposite ends. After surgery the recurrence risk is so low that a positive test still would not justify continuing; after an unprovoked event the recurrence risk is so high that a negative test would not justify stopping. Recurrence is dictated by the circumstances of the first event (p. 218).

DVT while taking a combined oral contraceptive p. 223

Estrogen-provoked VTE · testing suggested · 21 vs 2–7

Here the ledger flips: long-term anticoagulation in test-positives prevents 21 recurrent VTE per 1000 per year, while indefinite anticoagulation causes only 2–7 major bleeds per 1000 per year. So ASH suggests testing, with indefinite anticoagulation for those with a thrombophilia. The same logic covers VTE provoked by pregnancy or the postpartum period. Most of the prevented events (13 of 21) would be in FVL or PGM carriers simply because those mutations are common, with the specific but poorly understood estrogen interaction adding to it.

Not during the acute VTE (PC, PS, AT consumed), not on heparin (AT low), not on warfarin (PC, PS low), and with caution on a DOAC (function-based assays); exogenous estrogen itself raises aPC resistance, so a positive functional FVL screen must be genotyped. Genetic tests for FVL/PGM are unaffected by all of this.

A 22-year-old wants the pill; her mother had an unprovoked DVT at 30 and has AT deficiency p. 219–220, 225

Family history + AT/PC/PS deficiency · COC decision · false reassurance

This is the estrogen scenario where ASH does suggest testing: family history of VTE with a known AT, PC or PS deficiency, considering oral CHCs. Avoiding CHCs in those who test positive prevents 10.49–19.39 VTE per 1000. Testing in late adolescence / early adulthood, before the decision point, is exactly what the chapter calls practical.

Not automatically. A first-degree relative with VTE raises her risk 2–4-fold independently of any thrombophilia, more so because the relative’s clot was young and unprovoked. The chapter warns against providing false reassurance and still advises favouring progestin-only (non–depot medroxyprogesterone) formulations; many clinicians do so for any first-degree relative of a VTE patient regardless of testing. Note the contrast: had the mother’s thrombophilia been heterozygous FVL or PGM, ASH suggests against testing to guide COC use.

Planning pregnancy; her sister is homozygous for FVL and had a postpartum PE p. 220, 225

Pregnancy planning · Table 19-2 · antepartum + postpartum

Family history of VTE plus homozygous FVL in a sibling is a named “testing suggested” scenario. If she shares the sister’s result, ASH suggests antepartum and postpartum thromboprophylaxis, preventing an estimated 9.05–19.35 VTE per 1000 against 1.05–2.09 major bleeds per 1000. Table 19-2 agrees: homozygous FVL gets both antepartum and postpartum prophylaxis even without a family history, because the pregnancy risk is 17–34-fold.

Figure 19-1 says “either test or do not test” to guide antepartum or postpartum prophylaxis when the family thrombophilia is PC or PS deficiency. If she is tested and positive with a positive family history, Table 19-2 gives postpartum prophylaxis only — the daily VTE risk is considerably higher postpartum than antepartum, so postpartum prophylaxis benefits more people than antepartum. And note the timing trap: free PS falls during pregnancy, so test before conception or interpret with care.

Brother has heterozygous FVL; patient will be immobilized for a minor injury p. 223–224

Minor transient risk factor · family history + FVL/PGM · no recommendation

Minor transient risk factors are immobility, minor injury, illness or infection. With a family history of VTE, prophylaxis in test-positives prevents ~20 VTE per 1000 if the family deficiency is AT, PC or PS (risk 50 / 1000) but only 4.84–5.04 per 1000 for FVL/PGM (risk 15 / 1000), against 2.18 extra major bleeds. Hence: test for AT/PC/PS deficiency with a family history, but no recommendation for or against testing when the family history is FVL/PGM or unknown.

$\dfrac{4.84 \text{ to } 5.04}{2.18} \approx 2.2$–$2.3$ VTE prevented per bleed caused, versus $\approx 9$–$9.7$ for AT/PC/PS. Small enough, with low-certainty evidence, that the panel left it to shared decision-making.

Low free protein S found in the second trimester p. 221

Table 19-4 · PS biology · verify on a new sample

Pregnancy increases the C4b-binding protein, shifting PS from free/active toward bound/inactive, so free PS falls in pregnancy (as in other acute-phase states); AT also falls and aPC sensitivity decreases. A low free PS antigen here is expected physiology, not an inherited deficiency. The chapter’s rule for every functional assay applies: verify an abnormal result on a new sample outside the confounding state.

The free PS antigen assay: type II PS deficiency has low activity but a normal free antigen level. Clot-based PS activity is the most sensitive screen but inherits the PC clot-based confounders (FVIII, FVL, antibodies, anticoagulants). Labs choose activity, free antigen, or both.

Ambulatory colon-cancer patient starting chemotherapy; father had a DVT p. 221, 224

Cancer · first-degree family history · chemotherapy interference

Figure 19-1’s cancer row: for ambulatory patients at low or intermediate VTE risk with a family history of VTE in first-degree relatives, ASH suggests testing and thromboprophylaxis for those with a thrombophilia. The assay caveat matters for an oncologist: methotrexate, L-asparaginase and 5-fluorouracil can depress AT, PC and PS, so functional results drawn on treatment may be falsely low.

The genetic ones: FVL genotyping (or mutation-specific PCR) and PGM genetic testing. The three deficiencies are diagnosed by functional activity assays, which is precisely where chemotherapy, liver disease, acute VTE and anticoagulants introduce error.

Unprovoked DVT in a patient of East Asian ancestry p. 218–219

Epidemiology by ancestry · which thrombophilia to expect

FVL and PGM are European-ancestry variants and rarely found in Asian populations. Among East Asian VTE patients the deficiencies dominate instead: PS deficiency 8.3%, PC deficiency 7.1%, AT deficiency 3.8%. Compare 1.2% heterozygous FVL and 0.5% PGM in African Americans, and near-zero compound heterozygosity in West and South African populations.

No — an unprovoked VTE is still a “testing suggested against” scenario because recurrence risk (100 / 1000 in year 1) already mandates anticoagulation. Ancestry changes the pre-test probability of which thrombophilia, not the management logic.
