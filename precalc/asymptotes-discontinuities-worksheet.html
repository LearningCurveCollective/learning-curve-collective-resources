The Order, Every Time

1. Factor the top and the bottom completely.
$\dfrac{x^2 - x - 6}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+2)}{(x-3)(x-2)}$
2. Cancel matching factors: those are holes. Plug the $x$ into the simplified function to get the $y$.
$(x-3)$ cancels. Hole at $x = 3$, $y = \dfrac{3+2}{3-2} = 5$: the point $(3, 5)$
3. Leftover bottom $= 0$: those are vertical asymptotes.
$x - 2 = 0$: VA at $x = 2$
4. Star players decide the end behavior: keep the biggest term on top and bottom, simplify, plug in a big number.
$\dfrac{x^2}{x^2} = 1$: HA at $y = 1$
5. Zeros (simplified top $= 0$) and $y$-intercept ($f(0)$).
$x + 2 = 0$: zero at $x = -2$. $f(0) = \dfrac{-6}{6} = -1$

End behavior is two questions

$$\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = ? \qquad \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = ?$$

Plug a big positive number into the star fraction, then a big negative one. With an HA, both answers are the HA. With a slant, read them off the slant line.

Limit at a point: the two-runners test

$$\lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{limit exists}$$

One runner from the left, one from the right. At a hole they meet (limit = the hole's $y$). At a VA they never meet (limit does not exist).

Common Mistakes

  • Calling every zero of the bottom a vertical asymptote. Factor and cancel first. If it cancels, it's a hole.
  • Reporting a hole as just an $x$. A hole is a point: plug the $x$ into the simplified function for the $y$.
  • Writing the slant asymptote as a number. If the quotient is $x - 7$, the asymptote is the line $y = x - 7$. Writing $y = -7$ would be a horizontal line, and a different answer.
  • Looking for a slant when there's already an HA. A function can't have both. It can have a vertical asymptote with either.
  • Thinking the graph can never cross an HA. It can, in the middle. The HA only describes the far left and far right.
  • Including an asymptote in an interval. $f(2)$ is undefined at a VA, so it's neither less than, greater than, nor equal to anything. Always an open bracket there.

2

Holes and Vertical Asymptotes

A hole is a pothole. A vertical asymptote is a wall.

Why this matters

Both happen where the bottom of the fraction is zero, so both are places the function has no value. The difference is what the graph does next to them.

The two-runners test. Put one runner on the graph to the left of the trouble spot and one to the right, and have them walk toward it. At a hole they arrive at the same spot, a single missing point: the pothole. The limit exists and equals that point's $y$-value. At a vertical asymptote one runner shoots up and the other shoots down (or both shoot the same way), and they never meet: the wall. The limit does not exist.

The runners meet at the open circle: limit exists
The runners never meet: limit does not exist
Concept

Factor top and bottom completely. A factor that appears in both cancels: that's a hole at the $x$ that makes it zero, and its $y$-value comes from plugging that $x$ into the simplified function. Whatever is left on the bottom, set it equal to zero: those are the vertical asymptotes.

Factor$\dfrac{x^2 - x - 6}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+2)}{(x-3)(x-2)}$
Cancel: hole$(x-3)$ cancels, so hole at $x = 3$. $y = \dfrac{3+2}{3-2} = 5$. Hole at $(3, 5)$
Leftover bottom $= 0$: VA$x - 2 = 0$, so VA at $x = 2$
Hole at (3, 5), VA at x = 2, HA at y = 1

In limit language: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 5$ (the runners meet at the pothole), and $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist (the wall). The little $+$ and $-$ in $\lim_{x \to 2^+}$ and $\lim_{x \to 2^-}$ are just which runner you're talking about.

Grading note: if a question asks where the hole is, "$x = 3$" is full credit. If it asks for the point, or for the limit as $x \to 3$, you need the $y$: plug into the simplified function.

End Behavior: the Star Player

Far out, only the biggest term matters

Why this matters

Picture a race between every term in the function. Near the start line they're all bunched up and everything matters. But zoom out to $x = 1{,}000{,}000$: the $x^2$ term is a trillion, the $3x$ term is three million, and the $+1$ is still $1$. The fastest runner has left everyone else so far behind that you can cross the others off without changing the picture.

So end behavior asks one thing: who is the star player on top, who is the star player on the bottom, and what happens when you divide them?

Concept

End behavior is always two questions: what happens to $y$ as $x$ runs to $+\infty$, and what happens as $x$ runs to $-\infty$. Answer each one separately.

Star playersKeep only the highest-power term on top and on the bottom. Cross off everything else: $\dfrac{3x}{x^2 + 1} \approx \dfrac{3x}{x^2}$
Simplify$\dfrac{3x}{x^2} = \dfrac{3}{x}$
Plug in a big number$\dfrac{3}{\text{huge}} \to 0$. Then a big negative number: $\dfrac{3}{-\text{huge}} \to 0$ from below. HA at $y = 0$

Three things can happen when you simplify the star fraction, and they're the three cases in the chart:

  • A number over $x$-something (bottom won): a fixed number over a huge number is $0$. HA at $y = 0$.
  • Just a number (tie): the $x$'s cancelled, so the function levels off at that number. HA at $y = \tfrac{\text{top coefficient}}{\text{bottom coefficient}}$.
  • $x$-something is left over (top won): plugging in a big number gives a big answer, $\pm\infty$. No horizontal asymptote. If the top won by exactly one degree there's a slant asymptote instead (next tab); by more than one, nothing.

Calculator check. If you have a calculator, just plug in a big number, say $1{,}000{,}000$, and then $-1{,}000{,}000$. If the screen shows something like 2.7E-13, that E- means "divided by a lot of tens": it's $0$. If it shows overflow or a huge E+ number, that's $\infty$. A number that stays put, like $1.99999$, is your HA.

One rule that saves work: a function can't have a horizontal asymptote and a slant asymptote. If the star fraction gives you a number, you're done; don't go looking for a slant. (A vertical asymptote can happen alongside either one.)

Slant Asymptotes

Top wins by exactly one: the graph leans on a line

Why this matters

Divide $x^2 - 5x + 3$ by $x + 2$ and you get $x - 7$ with a remainder of $17$:

$$\frac{x^2 - 5x + 3}{x + 2} = (x - 7) + \frac{17}{x + 2}$$

Look at that leftover piece, $\dfrac{17}{x+2}$. Far out, it's a fixed number over a huge number: it goes to $0$. So far out, the function is the line $y = x - 7$, give or take nothing. That's why the graph leans on the line: not because of a rule, but because the remainder fades away.

Red: VA at x = −2. Gold: slant y = x − 7
Concept

Check degrees first. Top degree $= $ bottom degree $+ 1$? Then there is a slant asymptote (and no horizontal one). Find it by long division (or synthetic division when the bottom is $x - k$). The quotient is the line; throw the remainder away.

$$\frac{\text{top}}{\text{bottom}} = \underbrace{\text{quotient}}_{\text{the slant line}} + \frac{\text{remainder}}{\text{bottom}}$$

Write the whole line. The answer is $y = x - 7$, in $y = mx + b$ form. Writing $y = -7$ would name a horizontal line, which is a different (and wrong) answer. Don't label it $g(x)$ either, since $g(x)$ is the curve; the slant is its own line, so $y = \ldots$ or a new letter.

End behavior comes free. Once you have the slant line, plug a big positive number into it: $x - 7 \to +\infty$. Big negative: $\to -\infty$. The function does the same thing.

Find the slant asymptote of $\;g(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 5x + 3}{x + 2}$

Simplify the star fraction$\dfrac{x^{2}}{x} = x$
Plug in a big positive number$x \to \infty$
Plug in a big negative number$x \to -\infty$
Top wins by exactly one: slant asymptote. Divide$\dfrac{x^{2} - 5 x + 3}{x + 2} = x - 7 + \dfrac{17}{x + 2}$
Keep the quotient, ignore the remainder$$\boxed{\text{slant: } y = x - 7}$$
Worked Example: no VA, no HA, and still a slant

Inequalities from a Sketch

For which $x$-values are the $y$-values what the inequality wants?

Why this matters

That sentence is the whole topic. $f(x) < 2$ is not asking about $x < 2$. It's asking: shade every part of the graph whose $y$-value is below $2$, then tell me the $x$-values underneath the shading. Say it that way every time and the rest is bookkeeping.

Concept

When there's no graph given, sketch one from the boundaries:

1. BoundariesZeros (top $= 0$) and undefined points (bottom $= 0$). Mark them on a number line.
2. MultiplicityOdd multiplicity: the graph crosses zero there. Even: it bounces off and the sign stays the same.
3. Test a pointPlug in one easy $x$ (usually $0$) to see whether you start positive or negative. That picks between the two possible shapes.
4. ShadeShade the pieces whose $y$-values match the inequality.
5. Report the $x$'sInterval notation, left to right, joined with $\cup$. Closed bracket at a zero when the inequality has "or equal to"; open at a zero otherwise; always open at an undefined point and at $\pm\infty$.
Shaded where y ≥ 0: [−2, 3] ∪ [4, ∞)
Shaded where y < 0: (1, 3), open at both ends

Why is the asymptote always open? Because $f(3)$ in the right-hand graph is undefined: it isn't less than $0$, greater than $0$, or equal to $0$. It's nothing, so it can't be in the answer.
