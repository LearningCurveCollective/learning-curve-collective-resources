Order of Operations for Factoring

1. GCF? Pull it out first, every time. Then count the terms that are left.
$6x^2 + 9x = 3x(2x+3)$
2. Two terms. Both squares: $(a-b)(a+b)$. Both cubes: SOAP. Sum of squares: stop, it doesn't factor.
$x^2 - 9 = (x-3)(x+3)$
$x^3 + 8 = (x+2)(x^2-2x+4)$
3. Three terms. Multiply, Add, Split, Group. (When $a = 1$ the two numbers drop straight into $(x+m)(x+n)$.)
$2x^2 + 7x + 3 = (2x+1)(x+3)$
$x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x+2)(x+3)$
4. Four terms. Group in pairs, factor each pair, factor out the matching parentheses.
$x^3 + 3x^2 + 2x + 6 = (x+3)(x^2+2)$
5. Look again. Each factor gets the same checklist. $x^2 - 9$ or $x^3 + 8$ sitting inside your answer means you're not done.
$(x+2)(x^2-9) = (x+2)(x-3)(x+3)$

The Patterns

GCF

$$ab + ac = a(b + c)$$

Biggest number, lowest power of each variable, and a matching binomial counts too: $3x(x+2) - 7(x+2) = (x+2)(3x-7)$.

Grouping (4 terms)

$$ax + ay + bx + by = a(x+y) + b(x+y) = (a+b)(x+y)$$

If the two binomials don't match, try the other pairing or pull a negative out of the second pair.

Multiply → Add → Split → Group

$$ax^2 + bx + c:\quad mn = ac,\quad m + n = b$$

Split $bx$ into $mx + nx$, then group. Sign rule: $ac$ negative: one of each sign; $ac$ positive: both take $b$'s sign.

Difference of Squares

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$$

Works for numbers too: $23 \cdot 17 = (20+3)(20-3) = 391$.

Sum / Difference of Cubes

$$a^3 \pm b^3 = (a \pm b)(a^2 \mp ab + b^2)$$

SOAP: Same, Opposite, Always Positive. The quadratic never factors further.

Perfect Square (a quadratic)

$$a^2 \pm 2ab + b^2 = (a \pm b)^2$$

The Add step still finds it ($m = n$): this is just the shortcut when you spot it.

Three things that are not factoring.

  • $x^2 + 9 \ne (x+3)(x-3)$: that multiplies back to $x^2 - 9$. A sum of squares stays put.
  • $(a - b)^3 \ne a^3 - b^3$. Use SOAP; the middle sign is opposite the binomial's sign.
  • Stopping at $(x+2)(x^2 - 9)$. Every factor gets checked again.
Always check by multiplying back. FOIL / distribute your answer. If it doesn't rebuild the original, something slipped: usually a sign.

Greatest Common Factor

Pull out everything the terms share

Why this matters

Try this: solve $x^2 = 5x$. Most people divide both sides by $x$, get $x = 5$, and move on. But plug in $x = 0$: $0 = 0$. It works too, and dividing by $x$ quietly deleted it.

Factoring never loses an answer: $x^2 - 5x = 0$ becomes $x(x-5) = 0$, and now both solutions are sitting right there. Pulling out a GCF is the first thing you do to any expression, because it's the move that keeps every answer alive.

Concept

The GCF is the largest number that divides every coefficient, times the lowest power of each variable that appears in every term. Divide each term by the GCF to get what goes inside the parentheses. If the leading term is negative, pull the negative out too.

$$ab + ac = a(b + c) \qquad\text{check: distribute } a \text{ back in}$$
Worked Example

Factor: $\;12x^3 - 18x^2 + 6x$

Number GCF of $12, 18, 6$$6$
Lowest power of $x$ present in every term$x$
Divide each term by $6x$$12x^3 \div 6x = 2x^2,\quad -18x^2 \div 6x = -3x,\quad 6x \div 6x = 1$
Result$$\boxed{6x(2x^2 - 3x + 1)}$$

Don't drop the $1$: three terms in, three terms out. (And $2x^2 - 3x + 1$ factors further: you'll be able to finish it after the $ax^2 + bx + c$ tab: $6x(2x-1)(x-1)$.)

Worked Example: binomial GCF

Factor: $\;5x(x - 4) + 3(x - 4)$

The common factor is the whole parentheses $(x-4)$$(x-4)(\;5x + 3\;)$
Result$$\boxed{(x-4)(5x+3)}$$

This exact move is the last step of factoring by grouping: see the next tab.

Factoring by Grouping

Pair, factor each pair, then treat the matching parentheses as one thing

Why this matters

A puzzle. Two whole numbers $x$ and $y$ satisfy $xy + x + y = 11$. What are they?

You could guess for a while. Or add $1$ to both sides and watch four terms fall into pairs:

$$xy + x + y + 1 = 12 \;\Rightarrow\; x(y+1) + 1(y+1) = 12 \;\Rightarrow\; (x+1)(y+1) = 12$$

Now it's a question about the factor pairs of $12$, and the answers fall out: $(1,5),\ (2,3),\ (3,2),\ (5,1)$. Competition mathematicians call this "Simon's Favorite Factoring Trick," and it shows up in real number theory problems. Grouping isn't just a factoring step: it turns a guessing game into a lookup.

Concept

With four terms, split them into two pairs and factor the GCF out of each pair. If both pairs leave the same parentheses, you're one step from done.

The $u$-substitution. That last step is the one people get stuck on, so name the repeated parentheses. If both pairs leave $(x+4)$, write $u = x + 4$. Now the expression is just $x^2 u - 3u$, and $u$ is an ordinary common factor: $u(x^2 - 3)$. Put $x+4$ back in for $u$ and you're finished. Once this feels automatic you can skip writing $u$, but the move is the same either way.

$$ax + ay + bx + by = a(x+y) + b(x+y) = a\,u + b\,u = u(a+b) = (x+y)(a+b)$$

If the parentheses don't match: try pulling a negative out of the second pair, or reorder the terms and pair differently.

Worked Example

Factor: $\;x^3 + 4x^2 - 3x - 12$

Pair$(x^3 + 4x^2) + (-3x - 12)$
Factor the GCF out of each pair. Pull $-3$ (not $3$) so the parentheses match$x^2(x + 4) - 3(x + 4)$
Same parentheses in both. Let $u = x + 4$$x^2\,u - 3\,u$
$u$ is a common factor: pull it out$u(x^2 - 3)$
Substitute back $u = x + 4$$$\boxed{(x+4)(x^2-3)}$$
Check by distributing$x\cdot x^2 = x^3,\quad x\cdot(-3) = -3x,\quad 4\cdot x^2 = 4x^2,\quad 4\cdot(-3) = -12$ ✓
Worked Example: when the first pairing fails

Pair as written$(2x^3 - 6) + (3x^2 - 4x) = 2(x^3 - 3) + x(3x - 4)$: no match
Reorder by degree, then pair$2x^3 + 3x^2 - 4x - 6 = (2x^3 + 3x^2) + (-4x - 6)$

Quadratics: $x^2 + bx + c$

Signs first, then factor the last term

Why this matters

Every quadratic that factors was built by multiplying two parentheses. Factoring is detective work: someone multiplied two numbers and only showed you the result. Multiply $(x+3)(x+4)$ and watch where the clues land:

$$(x+3)(x+4) = x^2 + \underbrace{7}_{3+4}x + \underbrace{12}_{3\cdot4}$$

The last term is the fingerprint of the product of the two numbers. The middle term is the fingerprint of their sum. Read both fingerprints and you've caught them.

$c$ positive, $b$ negative: $(-)(-)$
$c$ negative: $(+)(-)$
2. Factor the last term. List the factor pairs of $|c|$, top to bottom, starting from $1$
pairs of 12

3. Pick the pair. Same signs: which pair adds to $|b|$? Different signs: which pair has a difference of $|b|$? (The bigger one takes $b$'s sign.)
4. Fill in and FOIL to check$(x + m)(x + n)$

Why does this work? The two numbers multiply to $c$ and add to $b$, exactly as the FOIL above shows. If no pair works, the quadratic doesn't factor (it's prime).

Quadratics: $ax^2 + bx + c$

Multiply → Add → Split → Group

Why this matters

Where does "multiply $a \cdot c$" come from? It's not a rule someone made up. Suppose the answer is $(px + q)(rx + s)$ and FOIL it:

$$(px+q)(rx+s) = pr\,x^2 + (ps + qr)\,x + qs$$

The middle coefficient $b$ is secretly two pieces added together, $ps$ and $qr$. Multiply those two pieces and you get $(pr)(qs) = ac$. So the two numbers you're hunting in the Add step are real objects: they're the two halves of the middle term, and you know their product and their sum. Splitting the middle just writes $b$ as those two halves again, and grouping rebuilds the parentheses.

Concept

Same idea as $x^2 + bx + c$, with one extra move at the front: because $a \ne 1$, you factor $a \cdot c$ instead of just $c$. The signs are decided the same way, from $ac$ and $b$.

Signs$ac$ negative: one $+$, one $-$ (bigger takes $b$'s sign). $ac$ positive: both take $b$'s sign.
Multiply$a \cdot c$
Addfind two numbers that multiply to $ac$ and add to $b$
Splitrewrite $bx$ as $mx + nx$: four terms now
Grouppair, factor each pair, factor out the matching parentheses. Stuck on that last step? The $u$-substitution on the Grouping tab makes it mechanical.

If there's a GCF, pull it out before you start: the numbers get much smaller. The order of the two final factors doesn't matter.

Difference of Squares

$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$

Why this matters

This pattern is why online shopping is safe. Every time you buy something, your card number gets locked with a giant number $N$ that's the product of two secret primes. The lock only holds because nobody can find those two primes.

Here's the attack. Pick any number and try to write it as a difference of squares. Say $N = 6059$: its square root is about $77.8$, so try $a = 78$.

$$78^2 - 6059 = 6084 - 6059 = 25 = 5^2$$

That's it. $6059 = 78^2 - 5^2 = (78-5)(78+5) = 73 \cdot 83$. Cracked in one step, no trial division. This is Fermat's factorization method, and it breaks any lock whose two primes are close together. So the engineers who build encryption have a rule: the two primes must be far apart. A rule that exists because of the pattern on this tab. The Bonus tab has the two-line proof of why it always works.

Concept

Two terms, subtracted, both perfect squares. Take the square root of each to get $a$ and $b$: coefficients, variables, and even whole binomials count: $9x^2 = (3x)^2$, $x^4 = (x^2)^2$, $(x-3)^2$ is already a square.

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b) \qquad\qquad a^2 + b^2 \text{ does not factor}$$
Worked Example

Factor completely: $\;x^4 - 16$

$a = x^2,\ b = 4$$(x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4)$
$x^2 - 4$ is another difference of squares$(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)$
$x^2 + 4$ is a sum of squares: stop$$\boxed{(x-2)(x+2)(x^2+4)}$$
Practice

Sum & Difference of Cubes

$a^3 \pm b^3 = (a \pm b)(a^2 \mp ab + b^2)$: SOAP

Why this matters

Is $1001$ prime? It looks like it could be. But it's $10^3 + 1$, a sum of cubes, so the pattern hands you a factor for free:

$$1001 = (10 + 1)(100 - 10 + 1) = 11 \cdot 91 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13$$

Now look at what you actually proved. $n^3 + 1 = (n+1)(n^2 - n + 1)$ for every $n$, so $n^3 + 1$ is never prime once $n > 1$. Infinitely many numbers, and one line of algebra rules out all of them. Factoring patterns aren't just for homework; they're how you prove things about numbers you'll never write down.

Concept

Cube-root each term to get $a$ and $b$, then SOAP: the binomial takes the Same sign as the problem, the quadratic's middle term takes the Opposite sign, and its last term is Always Positive.

Cubes to know cold: $1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000$: and $x^3, x^6, 8x^3, 27x^3$.
Worked Example

Factor: $\;27x^3 - 8$

Cube roots$a = 3x,\ b = 2$

Result$$\boxed{(3x-2)(9x^2+6x+4)}$$

The quadratic from SOAP never factors further: don't try the Add step on it.

Challenge Factoring: Factor Completely

Every factor gets the checklist again

Why this matters

On the Squares & Cubes tab, primes were the secret ingredient in a lock: multiply two of them and you get a lock number $N$. The rule of thumb is simple: bigger primes, tougher safe. So finding a brand-new giant prime is like discovering a stronger metal than anyone has ever had.

That giant on the right is $2^{p} - 1$ with $p = 136{,}279{,}841$. Here $p$ is just the exponent: how many times you multiply $2$ by itself before subtracting $1$. Small examples: $p = 2$ gives $2^2 - 1 = 3$; $p = 3$ gives $7$; $p = 5$ gives $31$; $p = 7$ gives $127$. All prime.

Why that shape, and where does factoring come in? Try an exponent that is not prime, like $12$:

$$2^{12} - 1 = (2^6)^2 - 1^2 = (2^6 - 1)(2^6 + 1) = 63 \cdot 65$$

It split. And it always will: if the exponent can be factored, so can $2^{\text{exponent}} - 1$ (keep going with $63 = (2^2)^3 - 1$, a difference of cubes, and you land on $4095 = 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 13$). So "look at every factor again" hands prime hunters a rule: only bother with exponents $p$ that are themselves prime: $2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \ldots$ That skips most of the number line. (One catch: a prime $p$ doesn't guarantee a prime. $2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \cdot 89$. It only tells you where it's worth looking.) The rest of the story, who hunts these giants and why, is on the Bonus tab.

Concept

"Completely" means no factor in your answer can be factored again. After each move, look at every factor and run the cheat-sheet checklist on it: GCF? two terms? three? four? The factors that are truly done are: linear factors like $(x - 3)$, sums of squares like $x^2 + 4$, SOAP quadratics, and quadratics where the Add step finds no pair.

Fermat's Factorization Method

That button you just pressed? Here's what it was doing. Given an odd number $N$: start at $a = \lceil\sqrt{N}\rceil$ (round the square root up). Compute $a^2 - N$. If it's a perfect square $b^2$, then $N = a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$ and you're done. If not, increase $a$ by $1$ and try again.

It's fastest when $N$'s two factors are close together: which is exactly why real encryption (RSA) is built from two primes that are far apart.

Every Odd Number Is a Difference of Squares

Still cracking codes. This is the guarantee that the pick can never fail on an odd $N$: every odd number can be written as $a^2 - b^2$, so a perfect square always shows up eventually. (The careful engineer's lock didn't break this rule; it just needed $451$ tries.) The proof is two lines.

0

The plan. Write a general odd number using a variable, then rewrite it as one square minus another.

1

Name an odd number. Every odd number is one more than an even number, so it can be written as $2k + 1$ for some whole number $k$.

2

Expand two consecutive squares. By the difference-of-squares pattern with $a = k+1$ and $b = k$:

$$(k+1)^2 - k^2 = \big((k+1) - k\big)\big((k+1) + k\big) = (1)(2k+1) = 2k + 1$$
$$\boxed{\;2k + 1 = (k+1)^2 - k^2\;}$$

So every odd number is a difference of two consecutive squares: $7 = 4^2 - 3^2$, $\;25 = 13^2 - 12^2$, $\;101 = 51^2 - 50^2$. In particular, every odd prime is a difference of squares.

The one exception, and why. $2$ is the only prime that isn't a difference of squares. Reason: $a^2 - b^2 = (a-b)(a+b)$, and $a - b$ and $a + b$ are always both even or both odd (they differ by $2b$). Both odd: the product is odd. Both even: the product is a multiple of $4$. So $2, 6, 10, 14, \dots$ (anything of the form $4k + 2$) can never be written as $a^2 - b^2$.

Naming note: this is not Fermat's Last Theorem (that's about $a^n + b^n = c^n$). It's the small lemma underneath Fermat's factorization method.

Prime Hunting: Forging Better Locks

Is prime hunting how you hack the far-apart locks? No. Cracking a lock and finding a prime are opposite games. A lock is two unknown primes multiplied together, and no factoring trick (Fermat's included) can pull two far-apart 300-digit primes back out of their product. That's the whole reason the lock holds. Prime hunting goes the other way: you already know the number, and you're asking one question about it: prime, or not?

So why hunt? Think of primes as the metal locks are forged from. Every new giant prime is a stronger metal than anyone has ever had, even if today's locks don't need anything that big yet. And it turns out $2^p - 1$ numbers are the easiest giants to test:

  • They have their own speed test. A trick called the Lucas-Lehmer test answers "prime or not?" for $2^p - 1$ without ever trying to factor it. Nothing like it exists for a random $41$-million-digit number, which is why every record prime for the last $70$ years has had this shape.
  • Each one comes with a bonus prize. Whenever $2^p - 1$ is prime, the number $2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)$ is a perfect number: it equals the sum of its own smaller divisors. $p = 2$ gives $2 \cdot 3 = 6 = 1 + 2 + 3$. $p = 3$ gives $4 \cdot 7 = 28 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14$. Euclid proved this connection about $2{,}300$ years ago, and every new prime found extends his list.
  • It's a team sport. The search (called GIMPS) runs on the spare time of thousands of volunteers' computers around the world. It's such a brutal workout that it has caught bugs in real CPUs that no other test found. Anyone with a laptop can install it and join the hunt.

And the factoring from the Challenge tab is the treasure map: because $2^n - 1$ always splits when $n$ isn't prime, the hunters only test prime exponents $p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \ldots$ and skip everything else.

Mental Multiplication

Two numbers the same distance from a round number are a difference of squares waiting to happen:

$$23 \times 17 = (20+3)(20-3) = 400 - 9 = 391 \qquad\qquad 52 \times 48 = (50+2)(50-2) = 2500 - 4 = 2496$$

And the two-numbers method from Tab 3 in reverse: $(x+3)(x+4)$ has product $12$ and sum $7$: so $103 \times 104 = 100^2 + 7\cdot100 + 12 = 10{,}712$.

Simon's Favorite Factoring Trick

An equation like $xy + x + y = 11$ has three terms and won't group. Add the right constant to both sides so the left side becomes four terms that group into $(x + \square)(y + \square)$. Then the two factors are a factor pair of the right-hand side.

$$xy + x + y = 11 \;\Rightarrow\; xy + x + y + 1 = 12 \;\Rightarrow\; (x+1)(y+1) = 12$$

Positive-integer solutions come from the factor pairs of $12$ with both factors $\ge 2$: $(x+1, y+1) \in \{(2,6),(3,4),(4,3),(6,2)\}$, so $(x,y) \in \{(1,5),(2,3),(3,2),(5,1)\}$.
