The Big Trade-off slide 7

Ecological validity (behavior is real, in its natural setting) vs reactivity (people change behavior when observed — the Hawthorne effect). More naturalistic → more valid but harder to control; more control/visibility → more reactivity.

Covert observation is OK only if… slide 6

(1) setting is genuinely public, (2) participants are anonymous, (3) no expectation of harm, (4) no feasible way to answer the question with consent in advance. All four.

Three settings, three flavors slides 3, 6, 8

Public / naturalistic → no interference, high ecological validity.
Participant → researcher joins the group, rich data, loses objectivity.
Controlled / overt → lab or private setting, gatekeeper, can manipulate.

Sampling rules (WHAT you watch) slide 9

Ad libitum – anything that happens.
Behavior – one specific behavior of interest.
Scan – whatever is happening at a set instant.
Focal – one individual in the group.

Recording rules (HOW you log it) slide 9

Scan sampling – check at fixed intervals → frequency (how often).
Continuous sampling – record start/stop of every bout → duration (how long).

Three analysis questions slide 10

Who showed the behavior?
How often did it occur? (frequency)
How long did it last? (duration)

Structured (checklists, pre-defined categories, tally tables) vs unstructured (field notes).

Percent agreement · Cohen’s $\kappa$ · Pearson’s $r$ · intraclass correlation (ICC).

No manipulation and no control over extraneous variables → you can describe and correlate, and generate hypotheses, but you cannot say X caused Y.

Convenience sampling (whoever is there, not a statistical sample) · findings are time- and context-specific · observer bias · reactivity.

What Qualitative & Observational Research Is

The seven spokes of qualitative research slide 3

The lecture opens with a hub-and-spoke diagram. The center is “Qualitative Research”; the spokes are the properties that distinguish it from a tightly controlled experiment.

Subjectivity → positionality & reflexivity

The researcher is a person with a background and a viewpoint. Positionality = where you stand relative to what you study (insider/outsider, identity, assumptions). Reflexivity = actively documenting and examining those biases so they don’t quietly steer the interpretation.

Naturalistic setting → public, controlled, or participant

You study behavior where it normally happens. The lecture then splits this into three settings: public (open, no expectation of privacy), controlled (lab or private, with a gatekeeper), and participant (you join the group).

Contextualization

Meaning depends on context. The same behavior (a smile, a fast walk) is interpreted differently in a bowling alley vs a lab, in Switzerland vs Mexico. Findings are reported with their setting, not stripped of it.

Inductive reasoning

Observation first, theory second. You watch, notice patterns, then build hypotheses to test later under controlled conditions. The lecture calls this “the watching and the wondering.” Contrast with deductive: start from a theory and test a prediction.

Flexibility

The plan can adapt as you learn. A coding scheme or focus can be revised mid-study when the field shows you something you didn’t anticipate. (This is a strength for discovery and a threat to reliability — that’s why audit trails matter, see Tab 3.)

Richness and depth → thick description, “small worlds”

Deep detail about a narrow slice of life rather than shallow data on a huge sample. Thick description = a detailed enough account of context and participants that a reader can judge whether the findings would transfer elsewhere.

Interpretation and meaning-making

Data are non-numerical (notes, descriptions) and the researcher’s job is to interpret them. But they can be numerically coded — e.g. tally headphones / earphones / none, or code smiles as social vs non-social.

What it demands of the observer slide 3

  • Strong observational skills and detailed note-taking — the notes are the data.
  • Comfort with data that start out as words and get coded into numbers only later.
  • Self-awareness (reflexivity) about how your own position shapes what you notice and how you read it.

Exam framing. If asked “why do observational research at all when experiments give causation?” the lecture’s three answers (slide 6) are: (1) it captures behavior that would not occur, or would occur differently, in a lab; (2) it works where manipulation would be unethical or impractical; (3) it generates hypotheses for later controlled testing.

Types of Observation, Ethics & the Trade-offs

A. Naturalistic observation in public places slide 6

Defining features
  • Public setting with no expectation of privacy.
  • No manipulation or researcher interference.
  • High ecological validity: captures behavior that wouldn’t occur (or would occur differently) under lab conditions.
  • Useful where manipulation is unethical or impractical; generates hypotheses (inductive, exploratory).
Limitations to always list
  • Convenience sampling — whoever happens to be there, not a statistically representative sample.
  • No control over extraneous variables → no causal claims.
  • Findings can be time- and context-specific.
  • Observer bias and reactivity.

B. Participant observation slide 8

Defining features

The researcher becomes part of the group and observes from inside its natural daily environment. Can be covert (the group doesn’t know) or overt (they do). Usually needs a gatekeeper — someone who controls access to the group.

Strengths
  • In-depth understanding, rich data, natural setting.
Costs and risks (the lecture lists many — know them)
  • Observer bias and reactivity; loss of objectivity (you start seeing things the group’s way).
  • Not generalizable; time-consuming and expensive.
  • Hard to maintain privacy and confidentiality.
  • Power dynamics and exploitation; could involve deception.
  • Cannot establish cause and effect.

Lecture examples: Rosenhan’s “On being sane in insane places” and Festinger, Riecken & Schachter’s When Prophecy Fails (1956).

C. Overt observation in private or laboratory settings slide 8

  • Requires a gatekeeper (school, clinic, parent).
  • Not unobtrusive — participants know they’re watched, so reactivity is the price.
  • May involve manipulations and allows experimental control — the one setting where you can start moving toward causal inference.

Lecture image: two observers with clipboards watching a preschool story-time.

The Major Trade-off

Ecological validity  vs  Reactivity slide 7

Ecological validity: how well the observed behavior represents behavior in real life. Highest when nobody interferes and nobody knows they’re being watched.

Reactivity: people (and animals) change their behavior because they are being observed. The lecture’s label for this is the Hawthorne effect — “observation changes behavior.”

Every design choice slides along this line. Making yourself visible (overt), entering a lab, or using a clipboard buys control and consent but costs naturalness. Staying hidden buys naturalness but raises the ethical bar (see below) and forfeits control.

Where the three observation types sit on the validity–reactivity line. Control and consent increase left-to-right; naturalness decreases.

2c

Ethics

APA standards for conducting research slide 5

  1. Institutional approval — IRB (humans) or IACUC (animals)
  2. Informed consent
  3. Avoid deception
  4. Voluntary participation; no coercion
  5. Debriefing
  6. Anonymity / confidentiality
  7. Meet standards of validity and reliability
  8. Researcher assumes responsibility
  9. No plagiarism or fabrication

When is covert observation permissible? slide 6

Covert observation skips informed consent, so it is allowed only when all four hold:

  1. The setting is genuinely public.
  2. Participants are anonymous — no personal identification.
  3. There is no expectation of harm to participants.
  4. There is no feasible way to answer the research question with consent obtained in advance.

Common exam trap: a study that meets three of the four is not “mostly fine.” Filming identifiable faces, or observing in a semi-private space (dorm lounge, locker room), breaks the conditions even if nobody is harmed.

3

Sampling, Recording & Data Quality

Two separate decisions slide 9

The lecture splits “how to make observations” into a sampling rule (what/whom you watch) and a recording rule (how you log it over time). Keep them separate on the exam — the word “scan” appears in both lists and students mix them up.

Sampling rules — WHAT you watch

  1. Ad libitum — note anything that occurs during the period.
  2. Behavior sampling — watch for one specific behavior of interest, whoever does it.
  3. Scan — what is everyone doing at a specific instant.
  4. Focal — follow only one individual in the group.

Recording rules — HOW you log it

  1. Scan sampling — check at predetermined intervals; tick if present.
  2. Continuous sampling — record every occurrence from start to stop.

Horse ethogram video in the course files uses a timer every 30 s — that’s scan sampling.

Scan sampling → frequency

Concept

Observe at fixed time points (every 2 min for 20 min in the lecture example). At each mark, record whether the behavior is happening. You get how often the behavior occurs — a count out of the number of checkpoints — but not how long any bout lasted.

Slide 9 scan-sampling example. Analysis = number of intervals in which the behavior occurred: $4/10 = 0.40$ of checkpoints.

Continuous sampling → duration (and frequency)

Concept

Record the behavior the whole time: when each bout starts, when it stops. You get when and for how long — duration — and you can still count bouts for frequency. More information, more labor.

Slide 9 continuous-sampling example. Analysis = proportion of the observation period spent in the behavior.

$$\text{Proportion of time} = \frac{225 + 120 + 70 + 235}{20 \times 60} = \frac{650}{1200} = 0.5417 \approx 54\%$$

Frequency from the same record = 4 bouts. Mean bout length = $650/4 = 162.5$ s. Scan sampling could never give you that last number.

Recording and analyzing slide 10

Structured recording = checklists, rating scales, behavior categories defined in advance (e.g. the tally table: headphones / earphones / none × wireless / wired). Unstructured recording = open field notes.

Whatever you record, the analysis answers three questions:

Who?

Who showed the behavior — which individuals, groups, categories.

How often?

Frequency of the behavior — counts, proportions of people or of checkpoints.

How long?

Duration of the behavior — only available with continuous recording.

The sample figure (N = 804 walkers: 103 headphones, 247 earphones, 454 none) is a “who / how often” result presented as a bar chart with a full caption — that is the model for your Project 1 figure.

3b

Data Quality in Qualitative Research

Qualitative work can’t lean on p-values to prove it’s trustworthy, so it uses these practices instead slide 11:

Audit trail (careful note-taking)

A detailed log of how data were collected, coded, and analyzed, so another researcher could retrace every step.

Triangulation — three kinds

Researcher triangulation: the team cross-checks each other’s data. Data triangulation: multiple informants (children plus teachers) or multiple measures. Method triangulation: multiple data sources (observation plus interview).

Thick description

Deeply detailed accounts of the context and participants so readers can judge transferability to other settings.

Reflexivity

Researchers actively document and examine their own biases, background, and assumptions to stop them skewing interpretation.

Inter-rater reliability

Do two coders see the same thing? Reported as percent agreement, Cohen’s $\kappa$, Pearson’s $r$, or intraclass correlation.

Member checking

Return transcripts, notes, or initial findings to participants to verify their experiences or words were represented accurately.

Memory hook: the six practices map onto the threats. Observer bias → reflexivity + inter-rater reliability. “Did you just make this up?” → audit trail + member checking. “Is it just your one angle?” → triangulation. “Would it hold anywhere else?” → thick description.

4

Examples & Classic Studies as Illustrations

Each example below is tagged with the concept it illustrates. The point is not the details of the studies but recognizing which type of observation, which ethical situation, and which limitation each one represents.

Geller, Russ & Altomari (1986) — beer drinking among college students slide 4

Naturalistic · covert · public setting

Observers in bars recording drinking behavior without announcing themselves. Check it against the four covert-observation conditions: public setting (bar), anonymous patrons, no harm, and no way to get consent without changing the behavior. High ecological validity — you could not stage “realistic” bar drinking in a lab. Uses behavior sampling (the target behavior is drinking) and a structured, quantitative record.

Convenience sample (whoever was in those bars, those nights); time- and context-specific (one campus, one era); no causal claims (you can describe that group drinking is heavier, not why); possible observer bias in judging what counts as a “drink.”

Kraut & Johnston (1979) — social and emotional messages of smiling slide 4

Naturalistic · ethological · hypothesis-testing in the field

An “ethological approach” means observing behavior in its natural habitat the way an animal behaviorist would. Smiles were coded by whether the person was facing others (social context) vs. facing the pins (emotional context). Illustrates contextualization (the same smile means different things depending on who it is directed at) and numerically coding non-numerical data. Also a nice case of high ecological validity: bowling-alley smiles would not look the same in a lab.

Coding smiles from a distance is exactly where inter-rater reliability matters — two observers must agree on what counts as a smile and whether the bowler was facing friends. Any such study should report percent agreement or Cohen’s $\kappa$.

Levine & Norenzayan (1999) — pace of life in 31 countries slides 4, 12

Naturalistic · cross-cultural · unobtrusive measures

Three unobtrusive field measures per country — walking speed, postal clerk speed, and public clock accuracy — combined into an overall pace index by averaging $z$-scores. Switzerland ranked fastest overall; Mexico slowest. Walking speed is a textbook covert public observation (a pedestrian on a street has no expectation of privacy and is anonymous). Shows how observational data can be made fully quantitative and compared across contexts.

Reading the index (Table 1): smaller numbers = faster. The overall index is the mean of the three $z$-scores, so a negative index means faster than the 31-country average.

$$\text{Pace index}_{c} = \frac{z_{\text{walk},c} + z_{\text{postal},c} + z_{\text{clock},c}}{3}$$

Convenience sampling (one or a few cities stand in for a whole country); time- and context-specific (one season, one downtown block, weather); no causal claims about why pace differs; possible reactivity if pedestrians noticed a stopwatch. Note how the table’s ranks disagree across measures (Netherlands: 2nd in walking, 25th in clock accuracy) — a reason to triangulate with several measures rather than trust one.

Rosenhan (1973) — “On being sane in insane places” slide 8

Participant observation · covert · deception

Pseudo-patients were admitted to psychiatric hospitals and observed from inside as patients. The lecture’s example of covert participant observation. Ticks nearly every risk on the participant-observation list: deception, privacy/confidentiality of real patients, power dynamics, loss of objectivity, not generalizable, and no causal claim. Also the clearest case of why the four covert-observation conditions exist — a hospital ward is not a genuinely public setting.

Festinger, Riecken & Schachter (1956) — When Prophecy Fails slide 8

Participant observation · covert · reactivity

Researchers joined a small doomsday group to watch what happened when the prophecy did not come true. Illustrates the gatekeeper problem (getting in), deception, and a subtle form of reactivity: when several new “believers” join a tiny group, the observers themselves change the group they are observing.

Jane Goodall and the Hawthorne cartoon slides 6, 7

Ecological validity vs reactivity

The slides pair Goodall sitting among chimpanzees with the Hawthorne cartoon (people behave normally until a telescope points at them, then start juggling). Goodall’s method is the ideal of naturalistic observation — but her presence, and later provisioning with bananas (the box in the photo), is the textbook illustration of an observer altering what is observed. The two images are the trade-off.

Preschool story-time with clipboards slide 8

Overt observation · private setting · gatekeeper

Two adults with clipboards watching children being read to. Children are minors in a private setting, so this must be overt with consent from a gatekeeper (school and parents). It is not unobtrusive, but it permits control — you could vary the story or the reader and compare. This is the setting where the design starts to look experimental.

Headphones on campus (N = 804) slide 10

Structured recording · scan sampling · Project 1 model

A tally table with categories fixed in advance (headphones / earphones / none × wireless / wired) filled in from walkers passing a spot — a scan-style count of who showed the behavior and how often. The bar chart with a numbered, captioned figure is the format your group report should copy.
