What This Section Actually Tests

ACT Science is a chart-reading test in a lab coat. Almost everything you need is in the passage, graph, table, or experiment. But expect a small handful of questions that require basic outside science knowledge.

Three habits cost the most time here, and all three are easy to fix: reading the passage before the questions, overthinking a chart that only asked for a direction, and doing exact math when the choices are a mile apart. This guide replaces each one with a faster move.

Part 1 of 5. This guide teaches the five core data moves. Advanced question types, experimental design and last questions, and Conflicting Viewpoints each get their own guide.

Format (enhanced ACT, 2025+)

40 questions • 40 minutes • 7 passages (6 scored, 1 unscored). Optional section with its own score; averaged with Math for a STEM score; not in the composite.

Two kinds of passages, not three

Conflicting Viewpoints (one passage, scientists disagreeing in prose) gets its own approach. Everything else — tables, graphs, experiment write-ups — is handled by the five moves below, whatever the test calls it.

Pace

One minute per question. Know the pace; do not chase it yet.

No calculator

Every calculation is built for rounding in your head. Ugly arithmetic means you are being more precise than the test wants.

Paper or online, every move here works. On paper you use a pencil. Online you use the highlighter, the option eliminator, and the edge of your scratch paper. Where a move is done differently on screen, the lesson says so.
2

The Five Moves: CHART

Five moves handle nearly every data question on the test. Remember them as CHART.

C
Circle
Circle the figure, unit, or label the question names before you look anywhere. Every question starts here.
H
Hop
Two figures in one question? Find the variable they share and hop across it.
A
Advance
Data stops, question keeps going? Draw the pattern past the edge.
R
Round
Round hard. The choices are spaced so rough math lands on one of them.
T
Trend
Up, down, flat, or no pattern. One series at a time.

CHART is how you remember the moves. The tabs teach them in the order you use them, most common first: Circle (every question) → Trend → Advance → Hop → Round.

3

How to Attack a Passage

The workflow, every passage, every time

1
Skip the passage text. Go to Question 1. Do not read the intro paragraph first. You will read it later only if a question sends you there.
2
Circle the target. Which figure or table? Which value, unit, or label? Circle it on paper; highlight it on screen.
3
Go to that spot on the figure and read. Find the axis or column the target lives on, then move across to the value the question wants.
4
Match to a choice and move on. If the choices are ranges, only one should fit. If two fit, you misread something — recheck the target, not the whole passage.
5
Reset when you feel lost. The moment you catch yourself trying to understand why the experiment works, stop. Reread the question from the first word and circle the target again.
Questions get harder inside a passage. The first two or three questions are usually direct lookups; the last one or two ask you to combine studies or apply the results to a new situation. Bank the easy ones fast. If the last question of a passage is eating your clock, pick your best guess, flag it, and go.
The most common time leak: reading a Research Summaries passage in full “to understand the setup.” You almost never need the setup. You need the column header and the row the question points to.
4

Pacing Targets

Use the practice section in this guide with the built-in timer. Your goal ladder:

  • First pass: finish all 40 questions with a clock running. Do not rush. Write down your time.
  • Second pass (a different set): under 50 minutes, using the five moves deliberately.
  • Test-ready: 40 questions in 40 minutes with time to revisit one or two flagged questions. If you test with extended time, scale the target to your accommodation.

Speed comes from doing less, not moving faster. Every move in this guide removes a step you were probably doing out of habit.

1

Circle: Mark the One Thing the Question Names

Read any ACT Science question and you will find it does two things: it tells you where to look (“According to Figure 2…”, “Based on Table 1…”) and it hands you one specific thing to find — a number with a unit, a trial number, a label, a species name. That specific thing is your target. Circle it. Once you have it, you are no longer searching the page; you are walking to a known address.

The habit to build: circle the target (on paper) or highlight it (online) before your eyes leave the question. Then find that exact word, unit, or number on the figure. Numbers live on axes and in table cells; units live in axis labels and column headers; names live in legends and row labels.

Circle the target, then walk to it

Concept

The target is almost always the thing in the question with a unit attached or a label that also appears in a legend, row, or column. Find it on the page first; read the value second.

Worked Example

According to Figure 1, at a depth of 20 m, the water temperature of Lake C is closest to:

  1. A. 9 °C
  2. B. 12 °C
  3. C. 15 °C
  4. D. 17 °C

Circle two things: “Lake C” and “20 m.” Lake C is a label, so it lives in the legend: find it and note which line it is (the triangles). 20 m is a unit of length, and the only axis in meters is the horizontal one.

Now walk: 20 on the bottom axis, straight up to the Lake C line only, then straight left to the temperature axis: about 15 °C. Answer C.

Every wrong choice is a real reading from the figure — 9 °C is Lake B at 20 m, 12 °C is Lake A at 20 m, 17 °C is Lake C at 10 m. The test does not punish you for reading badly; it punishes you for reading the wrong line. Circle the label before the number.

Notice what you did not do: you did not think about lakes, thermoclines, or why water gets colder with depth. Circle, axis, read.

2

Two Questions Before You Look Up

Circling the target is the quick version of the move. Here is the full version. Before your eyes leave the question, answer two small questions: Where am I looking? and What am I looking for? The first sends you to the right table, figure, or paragraph. The second tells you the exact word, unit, or number to scan for once you get there. It takes about two seconds, and it turns every lookup into a short, confident walk instead of a search.

Question 1: Where am I looking?

  • “According to Figure 2…” / “Based on Table 1…” — the figure or table named. This is the most common pointer and your starting point for most questions.
  • “Based on the results of Study 2…” — the data from that study only.
  • “Based on Study 2…” — could be that study’s data or its write-up. Check the data first.
  • “According to the passage…” / “Based on the information provided…” — the answer is in the text, not the figures. This is the one time you read the paragraphs.

Question 2: What am I looking for?

  • A unit — m, s, kg, mL, °C, kPa. The single most reliable target on the test; it points straight to a column header or axis label.
  • A science term that sticks out — pH, buret, thermocouple, yield. Any phrase that reads differently from the ordinary words of the question is a label you can go find.
  • An axis label or column header quoted in the question.
  • The answer choices themselves. When the stem is vague, compare the choices: the words they share point at the column or figure you need.

Where am I looking? What am I looking for?

Concept

Say both answers to yourself before you move your eyes. The first sends you to a table, a figure, or the text. The second is the unit, term, or label you will scan for once you get there.

Worked Example

Experiment 1
A student measured how dissolved salt changes the electrical conductivity of water. She poured 250 mL of distilled water into a beaker and placed a conductivity probe in it. She then added table salt (NaCl) one gram at a time, stirring until each gram dissolved, and recorded the conductivity after each addition (Table 1). Each gram of NaCl contains approximately 17 mmol of NaCl.

Starting with the final solution from Experiment 1, the student added an unknown liquid, Liquid Q, 10 mL at a time, and recorded the conductivity after each addition (Table 2).

  3. C. No; as the volume of Liquid Q increased, conductivity increased.
  4. D. No; as the volume of Liquid Q increased, conductivity decreased or stayed the same.

Based on the passage, if the student had added 3 g of NaCl in Experiment 1, approximately how many mmol of NaCl would have been in the beaker?

  3. C. 51 mmol

Question 1. Where am I looking? “Based on Table 1.” What am I looking for? “mass of NaCl added” and “conductivity” — the two column headers. Run down the conductivity column as the NaCl column rises: 0.1 → 2.0 → 3.9 → 5.7. Increased only, Answer A.

Question 2. Where? “Results of Experiment 2” — Table 2 only. What? “Liquid Q” and “conductivity.” The column goes 5.7 → 4.1 → 2.8 → 2.8: it fell, then flattened. The hypothesis said it would rise, so the answer starts with No, and the reason has to match the data. Answer D. On yes/no questions, the first word comes from comparing the claim to the data; the second half is just the trend you already read. Eliminate any choice whose reason contradicts the table, even if its yes/no is right.

Question 3. Where? “Based on the passage” — this one is in the text. What? “mmol,” a unit that appears nowhere in either table. Scan the paragraphs for it: “each gram of NaCl contains approximately 17 mmol.” Three grams is about 3 × 17 ≈ 50. Answer C, 51 mmol. The 250 in choice D is the water volume from the same paragraph — a tempting number if you grab the first figure you see, which is exactly why the “mmol” target matters.

Why this is worth the two seconds. Going back to the passage without a target usually means reading the setup, then the tables, then the setup again. Going back with a target turns the same trip into a five-second scan — and that saved minute adds up across seven passages.

Trend: Read the Direction, Not the Details

A trend question sounds like this: “As temperature increased, the reaction rate…” and the choices are some version of increased only / decreased only / stayed constant / no general trend, sometimes with increased, then decreased (or the reverse) in the mix. The question is asking for a shape, not a number.

Three rules keep these fast:

  • One series at a time. Put your finger on the line or bar group the question names and ignore the rest of the figure. Most trend mistakes come from reading the wrong series.
  • Left to right, or top to bottom. On a graph, follow the series from left to right as the x-variable grows. In a table, run your finger down the column the question names and say the direction out loud in your head: up, up, up.
  • “Only” means every step. “Increased only” is false if the series dips even once. A rise followed by a fall is “increased, then decreased.” Wobbling with no direction is “no general trend.”

Isolate the series, then say the shape

Concept

Decide the direction for one line or one bar group at a time. Cover the others with your hand if it helps. Then match the shape to the wording exactly — “only” is a strict word.

Worked Example

According to Figure 2, as temperature increased from 10 °C to 60 °C, the reaction rate of Enzyme A:

  1. A. increased only.
  2. B. decreased only.
  3. C. increased, then decreased.
  4. D. decreased, then increased.

According to Figure 2, as temperature increased from 10 °C to 60 °C, the reaction rate of Enzyme B:

  1. A. increased only.
  2. B. decreased only.
  3. C. increased, then decreased.
  4. D. decreased, then increased.

Enzyme A (circles): trace it left to right. It climbs to a peak near 40 °C and then falls off. Rose, then fell: increased, then decreased — Answer C.

Enzyme B (squares): every step from 10 °C to 60 °C is higher than the last. Increased only — Answer A.

Two questions, one figure, two different answers. That is why you isolate one series before you decide anything.

According to Figure 3, from 2022 to 2024, annual rainfall in Town Q:

  1. A. increased only.
  2. B. decreased only.
  3. C. remained constant.
  4. D. varied, but with no general trend.

According to Figure 3, from 2022 to 2024, annual rainfall in Town S:

  1. A. increased only.
  2. B. decreased only.
  3. C. remained constant.
  4. D. varied, but with no general trend.

Town Q: 90 → 75 → 60. Down every year: decreased only, Answer B.

Town S: 40 → 65 → 50. Up, then down. With only two steps you could call that “increased, then decreased,” but that is not offered; among the choices given, the honest description is varied with no general trend, Answer D. (Town R is the “remained constant” case: 55, 55, 55. Town P is the “increased only” case.)

Tables: isolate the block, then reorder if you must

Concept

Big tables usually test one thing at a time: a few rows change the length, the next few change the mass, and so on, with everything else held steady. To read a trend, use only the rows where the variable in the question changes and ignore the rest. Then, if the column you are reading is not in order (5, 9, 2, 7…), put it in order in your head before you decide whether the other column goes up or down.

Advance: Push the Pattern Past the Edge of the Data

Some questions ask about a value the figure never shows: a temperature past the end of the axis, a trial that was never run, a mass between two rows of a table. The data stops; the question does not. Your job is to continue the pattern honestly and see which answer choice the continuation lands in.

  • On a line graph, extend it. Paper: put your pencil on the last two or three points and draw the line out in the same direction with the same curve. Online: lay the edge of your scratch paper along the last two points and see where the edge crosses the value the question asks about — or count the step: read the last two points, take the change, and add it once more (a little more if the curve is steepening, a little less if it is flattening). Either way, do not eyeball it in your head. A straight line stays straight; a curve that is bending upward keeps bending upward.
  • On a table, continue the step. If each row adds about the same amount, add it again. If the question asks for a value between two rows, the answer sits between those two rows’ results.
  • Exactly one choice fits. Extension questions almost always use range choices (“less than 10,” “between 10 and 20”…). Your extended value should land inside one range. If it seems to fit two, you extended in the wrong direction or read the wrong series — go back to the target you circled. Online: use the option eliminator before you extend. If the line is falling, every range at or above the last data point is already gone.

Extend the line: pencil, paper edge, or count the step

Concept

Extend from the last two or three data points, keeping the same slope and the same bend — with a pencil on paper, the edge of your scratch paper on screen, or by adding the last step once more. Then read where the extension sits relative to the range choices.

Worked Example

According to Figure 1, if the trend continued, the water height in Tank A at 12 minutes would most likely be:

  1. A. less than 10 cm.
  2. B. between 10 cm and 30 cm.
  3. C. between 30 cm and 50 cm.
  4. D. greater than 50 cm.

According to Figure 1, if the trend continued, the water height in Tank C at 12 minutes would most likely be:

  1. A. less than 60 cm.
  2. B. between 60 cm and 70 cm.
  3. C. between 70 cm and 80 cm.
  4. D. greater than 80 cm.

Tank A is a straight line dropping 10 cm every 2 minutes. At 10 min it reads 30 cm; two more minutes takes it to about 20 cm. Only one range holds 20: between 10 and 30 cm, Answer B.

Tank C is curving upward, and getting steeper. From 8 to 10 min it rose from 42 to 60 — about 18 cm. Extending with the same (or steeper) bend, 12 min lands somewhere near 80 or above. It cannot be less than 60 (the line is rising) and the bend rules out a mere 10-cm gain. Greater than 80 cm, Answer D.

Notice the chart’s axis stops at 80. That is deliberate — the question is testing whether you will keep the curve going past the frame.

Try It

According to Figure 1, if the trend continued, the water height in Tank B at 14 minutes would most likely be:

  1. A. less than 12 cm.
  2. B. between 12 cm and 18 cm.
  3. C. between 18 cm and 24 cm.

Traps built for eyeballers. The wrong choices on extension questions are usually the last value shown on the figure (the answer if you stop at the edge) and the value you would get by extending in a straight line when the curve is actually bending (or vice versa). Keep the bend. Keep the direction. Draw it or count it.

Hop: Cross Two Figures Through the Variable They Share

Some questions name two figures or tables at once: “Based on Figures 1 and 2…”, “According to Tables 1 and 2…”. That phrasing is a flag. It means the value you are given lives in one figure, the value you want lives in the other, and one variable appears in both. That shared variable is the bridge. The hop is the move; the bridge is what you cross to make it.

The hop has three steps and works like the transitive property in math (if A relates to B and B relates to C, then A relates to C):

  1. Circle the target in the question and locate which figure it lives in.
  2. Read across to the bridge — the variable that also shows up in the other figure. Look for a matching axis label or column header.
  3. Carry the bridge value into the second figure and read off the answer.

How to spot the bridge fast: scan the axis labels and column headers of both figures for the one that repeats. It is usually on the same axis in both graphs, or it is the last column of one table and the first column of the next.

The hop, drawn out

Question: “Based on Figures 1 and 2, 6 days after a rain, the growth rate of Species X was closest to…” The gold label is the bridge — the one axis both figures share.

1  Circle the target: “6 days.” Days live only in Figure 2, so start there.
2  Read up to the line and across: 6 days → 26% soil moisture.
3  Hop. Soil moisture is on both figures — carry 26% to Figure 1’s x-axis.
4  Read up to Species X and across: about 4 mm/day. Done.

Two graphs, one shared axis

Concept

Start in the figure that contains the target. Read to the shared variable. Carry that value to the other figure and finish.

Worked Example

Based on Figures 1 and 2, 6 days after a rain, the growth rate of Species X was closest to:

  1. A. 2 mm/day
  2. B. 4 mm/day
  3. C. 6 mm/day
  4. D. 8 mm/day

Circle: “6 days after a rain.” Days live only in Figure 2, so start there.

Bridge: soil moisture appears on the y-axis of Figure 2 and the x-axis of Figure 1 — that is the shared variable. In Figure 2, 6 days → about 26% moisture.

Carry it over: in Figure 1, go to 26% on the x-axis and read Species X (circles): about 4 mm/day. Answer B.

Three reads, no science. The only thing you had to notice was that “soil moisture” appears in both figures.

Try It

Based on Figures 1 and 2, when the growth rate of Species Y was 5 mm/day, the number of days since the last rain was closest to:

  1. A. 3
  2. B. 5
  3. C. 7
  4. D. 9

This one hops the other way. Circle: 5 mm/day for Species Y, which lives in Figure 1. Species Y (squares) reads 5 mm/day at 30% moisture. Carry 30% to Figure 2: the line hits 30% at 5 days. Answer B. The hop works in either direction; just start wherever the target lives.

Pore size, Answer B — it is the only column that appears in both tables. Naming the bridge before you hop is how you keep this question type under 45 seconds.

Round: Make the Zeros Cancel

There is no calculator on this section, and the test writers know it. When a question needs arithmetic, the answer choices are spaced far enough apart that a rough estimate lands on exactly one of them. Your job is to get roughly right fast, not exactly right slowly.

The instinct that costs you time is rounding timidly. If the number is 1,184 and you round it to 1,180, you have saved nothing. Round to 1,200. If the choices are 1.2, 12, 120, and 1,200, you could round it to 1,000 and still get the question right. Round until the zeros cancel.

The rule: Before you divide or multiply, replace every number with the nearest friendly one — a multiple of 10, 25, 50, or 100 — then check that your rough answer sits closest to only one choice. If two choices are still close, round less. If none are, you probably slipped a decimal place.

Make the zeros cancel

Concept

Rewrite the calculation with friendly numbers, cancel zeros, then divide or multiply. Check against the choices only after you have a rough number.

Worked Example
Table 1
CartDistance rolled (cm)Time (s)
A1,1844.2
B2,3715.9
C3,8467.1

According to Table 1, approximately how many meters did Cart A roll?

  1. A. 1.2 m
  2. B. 12 m
  3. C. 120 m
  4. D. 1,200 m

Circle: Cart A, distance = 1,184 cm. You need centimeters → meters, and 100 cm = 1 m.

Round 1,184 to 1,200. Then $\dfrac{1200}{100} = 12$. The zeros cancel and the division is instant. Answer B.

The choices differ by a factor of ten each, so the only real risk was a misplaced decimal, not the rounding.

Worked Example

Cart C rolled 3,846 cm in 7.1 s. Its average speed, in cm/s, was closest to:

  1. A. 5
  2. B. 50
  3. C. 500
  4. D. 5,000

Round 3,846 to 4,000 (or even 3,500 — both work). Round 7.1 to 7. Then $\dfrac{4000}{7} \approx 570$, or $\dfrac{3500}{7} = 500$. Either way, the only choice in the neighborhood is 500, Answer C.

Do not compute 3,846 ÷ 7.1 = 541.69. The question said “closest to” and spaced the choices by tens. It is telling you to estimate.

Try It

A pump moved 3,870 mL of water in 90 seconds. The pump's flow rate, in mL per second, was approximately:

  1. A. 4
  2. B. 40
  3. C. 400
  4. D. 4,000

3,870 → 4,000 and 90 → 100 gives $\dfrac{4000}{100} = 40$. (The precise value, 43, is not needed.) Answer B.

A reaction released 0.48 g of gas per minute. Approximately how many grams were released over 2 hours?

  1. A. 1 g
  2. B. 6 g
  3. C. 60 g
  4. D. 120 g

Two hours is 120 minutes. Round 0.48 to 0.5, then $0.5 \times 120 = 60$. Answer C. The exact figure (57.6 g) is closer to 60 than anything else, which is the point.

Number Tools: Decimals, Metric, and Big/Small Numbers

Three small tools that make rounding and unit questions automatic. None of them need a calculator; all of them are about moving a decimal point.

1. The decimal rule

Concept

Multiplying or dividing by 10, 100, or 1,000 never changes the digits — it moves the decimal point. Multiply → move right. Divide → move left. One place per zero.

$3{,}870 \div 100 = 38.7$  (two zeros, two places left)   •   $0.46 \times 1{,}000 = 460$  (three zeros, three places right)

This is why “round until the zeros cancel” works: once the numbers are friendly, the only thing left to do is slide a decimal.

Concept

The test likes to give you millimeters in the table and ask in centimeters, or milliliters in the figure and liters in the question. Every metric conversion is the decimal rule in disguise: each rung of the ladder is a factor of 10, 100, or 1,000.

3. Scientific notation: read it, do not compute it

Concept

Tables and axis labels sometimes show very big or very small numbers as $a \times 10^{n}$. The exponent is a decimal-rule instruction: positive $n$ → move the decimal $n$ places right (big number); negative $n$ → move it $n$ places left (small number).

$3.2 \times 10^{4} = 32{,}000$   •   $4.5 \times 10^{-3} = 0.0045$

On this section you will be asked to read or compare these, almost never to multiply them. To compare two, look at the exponents first — the bigger exponent wins, no matter what the front number is. Only if the exponents tie do you compare the front numbers.

Strategy Tips — The Whole Guide in One Page

Same order as the tabs: get set, circle, read the data, do the math, keep your pace. Reread this the night before the test.

Before you start

1Know the shape of the section

40 questions, 40 minutes — about a minute each. Seven passages: one Conflicting Viewpoints (its own guide), the rest are tables, graphs, and experiments handled by CHART.

2Go to the questions first

Skip the intro paragraph. Question 1 tells you exactly where to look, so let it lead.

On every question: Circle

3Circle the target

The figure, unit, or label the question names. On paper, circle it; online, highlight it. Do this before your eyes leave the question.

4Ask the two questions

Where am I looking? (Figure 2, Table 1, the results of Study 3, or the passage text.) What am I looking for? (A unit, a science term, an axis label, or the phrase the answer choices share.)

5Label before number

Find the right line in the legend or the right column header first. Then read the value.

Reading the data: Trend, Advance, Hop

6Trend: one series at a time

Put a finger on the line or column the question names and follow it left to right. “Increased only” means every step went up — one dip changes the answer.

7Big tables: use only the rows that matter

Read the rows where the question’s variable changes and ignore the rest. If a column is out of order, sort it in your head first.

8Advance: extend the pattern

Pencil on paper, scratch-paper edge on screen, or count the last step and add it again. Keep the direction and the bend. Only one range choice should fit.

9Hop: “Figures 1 and 2” means find the bridge

Look for the axis label or column header that appears in both. Circle → bridge → answer.

The math: Round

10Round until the zeros cancel

No calculator, and the choices are spaced far apart on purpose. Replace 1,184 with 1,200 and 7.1 with 7, then divide.

11Units are just decimal moves

km ↔ m, L ↔ mL, g ↔ mg: slide the decimal three places. cm ↔ m: two. mm ↔ cm: one.

Pacing and staying calm

12Bank the early questions

The first few questions in each passage are lookups. Take them quickly and confidently.

13Do not stall on the last one

The final question or two in a passage are the hard ones. Make your best pick, flag it, and keep moving — you can come back.

14Lost? Reset to the question

The moment you notice you are trying to understand the experiment, stop. Reread the question from its first word and circle the target again.

15Outside-knowledge questions are few

A handful per test ask for a basic idea from bio, chem, or physics. Answer from what you know; the passage will not have it.
