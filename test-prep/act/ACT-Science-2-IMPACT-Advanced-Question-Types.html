What Part 2 Adds

Part 1 gave you CHART — the five moves that answer most of the section. This guide is about the questions that look different: answer choices that are whole sentences, a “cannot be determined” option, equations as answers, mixing two solutions, dots on a scatter plot, and questions that never tell you where to look.

They are only a handful per test, but they are the ones that separate a good score from the score you want — and each has a single move that makes it routine. Every one of them still starts with Circle.

Part 2 of 5. Assumes Part 1 (CHART). Experimental design, last-question tactics, and Conflicting Viewpoints get their own guides.

2

The Six Moves: IMPACT

Six question types, six moves. Remember them as IMPACT.

I
Infer
No location and “most likely”? The answer is in the question. Think; do not go back.
M
Missing
Circle and go look. If the target is not on the page, “cannot be determined” is the answer.
P
Plug
Equations as choices? Pick one point and plug it into each.
A
Average
Mixing two solutions lands in the middle, never the sum.
C
Count
Scatter plots: every dot is one measurement. Count the dots.
T
Target the choices
Full-sentence answers? The target moved into the choices. Read them first; their differences are what you scan for.

IMPACT is how you remember them. The tabs teach them in three groups, in the order you notice them on a question: the choices changed (Target the choices, Missing) → there is nothing to look up (Infer) → the data changed (Count, Plug, Average).

3

How These Fit With CHART

1
Circle first, always. Where am I looking? What am I looking for? Nothing in this guide skips that step — two of the moves (Target the choices, Missing) are really Circle with a twist.
2
Notice the shape of the question. Whole-sentence choices, a “cannot be determined” option, equations, the word mixed, a cloud of dots, or no location at all — each one names its own move.
3
Make the move, then finish with CHART. Plug gives you an equation; Advance uses it. Average gives you a new concentration; Circle reads it off the figure. The new moves get you to the data; the old moves read it.
Paper or online, same moves. Reading the answer choices first works on both. On screen, the option eliminator is your friend on every one of these: knock out the choices whose reason contradicts the data, and what is left is usually the answer.
1

Same Skill, Two Shapes

Here are two questions about the same experiment. There is no data attached on purpose — you cannot answer them yet. Just look at the shape of each one.

Short answers

According to Table 1, which sample had the lowest dissolved oxygen?

  1. A. Sample 1
  2. B. Sample 2
  3. C. Sample 3
  4. D. Sample 4
Full-sentence answers

What action did the students take to keep the yeast evenly mixed?

  1. F. Only 0.5 g of yeast was placed in the flask.
  2. G. The flask was rinsed with distilled water.
  3. H. The flask was placed on a shaker plate at 120 rpm.
  4. J. The water was stirred with a glass rod.

The left one is Circle, exactly as you learned it.

Where am I looking? Table 1. What am I looking for? “Lowest dissolved oxygen.” Off you go — and you never needed the choices. Knowing you will pick from Samples 1–4 does not save you a second.

Now try the same two questions on the right one.

Where? The procedure. What? “…how they kept it mixed” — which is an idea, not a word. You cannot scan for an idea. So read the choices: 0.5 g, distilled water, shaker plate, glass rod. Those you can scan for. The target did not disappear; it moved from the stem into the choices.

That is the whole twist. Target the choices is Circle for questions where the target lives in the answer choices instead of the question. Same two questions, one extra place to look.
2

Target the Choices: Read Them First

When the answer choices are whole sentences, read all four before you go back to the passage. Notice how they differ — a shaker plate versus a glass rod, a distilled-water rinse versus 0.5 g of yeast, 20 °C versus 30 °C. Those differences are the exact words to scan for, and finding an exact phrase takes seconds.

The move: read the choices first, underline what changes from one to the next, then go find those words. For Yes/No choices, check the reason half first — the data settles it — and the Yes or No follows.

The choices are the target

Concept

Whole-sentence choices name the exact words you are looking for. Read them, spot the differences, then go find those words in the passage or the data.

Worked Example

Students measured how yeast affects the dissolved oxygen (DO) in water, using a DO probe (a sensor that reports oxygen concentration in mg/L).

Experiment 1
Steps 1–6 were performed 4 times:

  1. The DO probe was calibrated in air at 20 °C.
  2. A 500 mL flask was rinsed three times with distilled water.
  3. The flask was placed on a shaker plate set to 120 rpm so the water stayed evenly mixed.
  4. A known mass of dry yeast was added to the flask.
  5. Distilled water at 20 °C was added to bring the volume to 500 mL.
  6. After 10 minutes, the DO of the water was measured.

Table 1 shows the mass of yeast used and the DO measured for each of the 4 samples.

Experiment 2
The procedure from Experiment 1 was repeated, except the probe was calibrated at 30 °C and the water added in Step 5 was at 30 °C.

Any undissolved yeast could produce uneven readings. What action did the students take to keep the water and yeast evenly mixed?

  1. A. Only 0.5 g of yeast was placed in the 500 mL flask.
  2. B. The flask was rinsed three times with distilled water.
  3. C. The flask was placed on a shaker plate set to 120 rpm.
  4. D. The water was stirred with a glass rod as the yeast was added.

A student hypothesized that adding more yeast would lower the dissolved oxygen of the water. Do the results of Experiment 1 support this hypothesis?

  1. A. Yes; as the mass of yeast increased, the DO decreased.
  2. B. Yes; as the mass of yeast increased, the DO increased.
  3. C. No; as the mass of yeast increased, the DO decreased.
  4. D. No; as the mass of yeast increased, the DO increased.

Which step, Step 1 or Step 4, changed from Experiment 1 to Experiment 2?

  1. A. Step 1, because the probe was calibrated at 30 °C instead of 20 °C.
  2. B. Step 1, because more yeast was used in Experiment 2.
  3. C. Step 4, because the probe was calibrated at 30 °C instead of 20 °C.
  4. D. Step 4, because more yeast was used in Experiment 2.

Question 1. Read the choices first. They differ by one phrase each: 0.5 g, rinsed with distilled water, shaker plate at 120 rpm, glass rod. Now scan the six steps for those words. Step 3 has the shaker plate — and it even says why (“so the water stayed evenly mixed”). Answer C. The rinse (Step 2) is real but does a different job; the glass rod never appears at all.

Question 2. Yes/No choices. Check the reason half first: Table 1 shows DO falling as yeast rises (9.0 → 7.2 → 5.1 → 3.4). That kills B and D, whose reasons say DO increased. The hypothesis said “more yeast, lower DO” — which is what happened — so Yes. Answer A.

Question 3. The choices split two ways: which step (1 or 4), and why (calibration temperature or yeast mass). Experiment 2’s description names the calibration temperature and the water temperature; it says nothing about more yeast. So the reason is the 30 °C calibration, and calibration lives in Step 1. Answer A. You never had to reread the whole procedure — the choices told you to compare Step 1 and Step 4 only.

Yes/No with a reason: split every choice in half. The reason half is a fact you can check against the table or figure — check it first and cross out every choice whose reason is false. Then decide Yes or No by comparing what the data says to what the claim said. Two of the four choices usually die on the reason alone.

Missing: If the Target Is Not There, That Is the Answer

“Cannot be determined from the given information” is designed to make you second-guess a good read. Do not let it change your approach. Circle the target and go look for it exactly as you would on any other question.

  • If the target is on the page — the number, the unit, the label — answer the question. The “cannot be determined” choice is a distraction.
  • If it is not on the page — the question asks for a count and you only have percentages, or asks about a condition nobody tested — then “cannot be determined” is simply the honest answer, and you can pick it with confidence.

Two flags that it might be missing: the phrase “if it can be determined” in the stem, and a “Suppose…” that introduces information the passage never measured (ages, seasons, a material nobody tested).

Go look; then believe what you find

Concept

Circle the target and search for it. Found it — answer. Not there — “cannot be determined” is correct, not a cop-out.

Worked Example

Study 2
The race was held again the following year, with a new set of runners. This time the organizers recorded both the number of runners registered in each bracket and the number who finished in under 30 minutes (Figure 1).

Question 4. A “Suppose…” that adds a condition nobody tested. Both races were in the morning; nothing in the passage measures the time of day. Cannot be determined, D.

Infer: The Answer Is in the Question

Every move so far starts by asking where am I looking? Inference questions are the one type where the answer is: nowhere. Two signs give them away:

  • The stem does not name a figure, table, study, or the passage. There is no pointer.
  • It uses phrases like “most likely,” “the most likely reason,” or “best explains why.”

Going back to the passage on these costs time and finds nothing, because the passage describes what was done, not why. Instead: read the question, read all four choices, and take a few seconds to think about what a careful person would be trying to accomplish with that step. Everyday science sense — foil blocks light, distilled water is clean, a screen filters — is all it takes.

The move: no pointer + “most likely” = stay on the question. Ask “what problem would this step solve?” and pick the choice that solves it. Cross out any choice that describes something the step could not physically do.

Think it through; do not look it up

Concept

Six inference questions with no passage attached — because you do not need one. For each, decide what the step accomplishes, then pick the choice that says so.

Try It

The researchers rinsed every beaker with distilled water rather than tap water before adding the solutions. They most likely did this to:

  1. A. add minerals that would speed up the reaction.
  2. B. avoid introducing dissolved minerals that could affect the measurements.
  3. C. raise the temperature of the beaker before the trial.
  4. D. make the solutions easier to see through.

Distilled water has had its dissolved minerals removed. Tap water has not. If you want clean, comparable measurements, you rinse with the water that will not leave anything behind. The other choices describe things rinsing does not do.

Each flask was covered with aluminum foil during the 3-day trial. The most likely reason for the foil was to:

  1. A. keep light from reaching the solution.
  2. B. increase the pressure inside the flask.
  3. C. allow the solution to evaporate faster.
  4. D. add aluminum to the solution.

Foil is opaque. Covering a flask with it blocks light (and, as a side benefit, slows evaporation — the opposite of C). Foil does not raise pressure or dissolve into the solution.

In every trial, the students used exactly 250 mL of water. They most likely kept the volume the same so that:

  1. A. the water would boil more quickly.
  2. B. each trial would use less water overall.
  3. C. any difference in the results could be traced to the variable they changed.
  4. D. the thermometer would fit in the beaker.

This is the idea of a controlled variable: hold everything steady except the one thing you are testing, so that thing gets the credit or blame for what changes. The other choices are not reasons to keep a volume constant.

Before the experiment began, the seedlings were kept in complete darkness for 24 hours. The most likely purpose of this step was to:

  1. A. kill any seedlings that were unhealthy.
  2. B. make sure every seedling started with the same amount of stored energy from light.
  3. C. prevent the seedlings from needing water.
  4. D. cool the seedlings to room temperature.

Plants store energy from light. A day in the dark puts every seedling at the same starting point, so differences in the trial come from the trial. The other choices do not follow from darkness.

The researchers ran each trial three times and reported the average of the three results. They most likely did this to:

  1. A. make the experiment take longer.
  2. B. reduce the effect of a single unusual measurement on the results.
  3. C. use up all of the available chemicals.
  4. D. make the three results identical.

Any single measurement can be thrown off by a small mistake or a random wobble. Averaging three smooths that out. Repeating does not make results identical (D) — if it did, there would be no reason to average.

A species of moth that rests on tree bark during the day is most commonly a mottled gray-brown. This coloring most likely:

  1. A. attracts mates from a greater distance.
  2. B. helps the moth absorb more sunlight to stay warm.
  3. C. makes the moth harder for predators to see against the bark.
  4. D. prevents the moth from flying at night.

A moth colored like bark is camouflaged on bark, and a moth that is hard to see is hard to eat. The other choices describe things a color does not do — and this one is not a lab procedure at all. Inference questions can be about any part of a passage, not only the setup.

How this pairs with Target the choices. Inference choices are full sentences too, so read all four before deciding. Usually two of them describe something impossible (foil adding aluminum, rinsing raising temperature). Cross those out and you are choosing between two — then ask which one a careful scientist would actually want.

Count: Every Dot Is One Measurement

A scatter plot is a graph where the data are drawn as separate dots, usually with a smooth best-fit line running through them. The line is a summary; the dots are the actual measurements. Questions about scatter plots tend to ask one thing in several disguises: how often was the sample measured?

  • Find the time axis, then look at the spacing between dots along it. Dots every 3 days means the plant was measured every 3 days.
  • Ignore the story. “How often was the seedling removed from the tray and measured with a ruler” sounds like it needs the passage. It does not. The dots answer it.
  • Watch for a change in spacing. Many figures are measured often at first and less often later. Count within the range the question names.
  • Do not count the line. A smooth curve passing through the dots is not a measurement.

Read the spacing on the time axis

Concept

Locate the time axis, then count the gaps between dots inside the range the question names. That spacing is how often the measurement was taken.

Worked Example

According to Figure 1, from Day 0 to Day 30, how often was the height of each seedling measured?

  1. A. Every day
  2. B. Every 3 days
  3. C. Every 5 days
  4. D. Every 10 days

According to Figure 1, if the seedlings had been measured for 10 more days, the height of Variety B would most likely have:

  1. A. increased slightly, then leveled off.
  2. B. decreased.
  3. C. doubled.
  4. D. varied, with no general trend.

Question 1. Look along the day axis: dots sit at 0, 3, 6, 9, … 30. Ten gaps in thirty days, so every 3 days. Answer B. The stem mentions the height of each seedling, but you did not need to know anything about seedlings or rulers — only where the dots are.

Question 2. Advance in a scatter-plot costume. Variety B’s fit line is nearly flat by Day 30 — still creeping up, but barely. Ten more days adds a little, then levels off. Answer A. “Doubled” ignores the flattening; “decreased” ignores the direction.

Try It

According to Figure 2, from 0 min to 5 min, how often was the water temperature measured?

  1. A. Every 0.5 min
  2. B. Every 1 min
  3. C. Every 2 min
  4. D. Every 5 min

According to Figure 2, from 5 min to 25 min, how often was the water temperature measured?

  1. A. Every 0.5 min
  2. B. Every 1 min
  3. C. Every 2 min
  4. D. Every 5 min

According to Figure 2, how many temperature measurements were taken after the 10 min mark and before the 20 min mark?

  1. A. 3
  2. B. 4
  3. C. 5
  4. D. 10

0 to 5 min: the dots are packed tightly — ten gaps in five minutes, so every half minute. Answer A.

5 to 25 min: the dots spread out to 7, 9, 11, … 25 — two minutes apart. Answer C. Same figure, two different answers: count inside the range the question names.

After 10 and before 20: the dots in that stretch sit at 11, 13, 15, 17, and 19. Five measurements. Answer C. The italics on after and before tell you not to count anything sitting exactly on 10 or 20 — and here nothing does.

Plug: Pick a Point and Plug It In

When the answer choices are equations, do not try to derive the formula. Pick one point from the figure or one row from the table, plug its numbers into each choice, and keep the one that comes out true. Three tips make it fast:

  • Pick an easy, non-zero point. Zero makes half the choices look right (0 = 0). A point like (2, 8) separates them immediately.
  • If two choices survive, plug in a second point — usually the zero point, which is the fastest way to check whether an equation needs a “+ something” at the end.
  • Round. The choices are different enough that a rough check is plenty.
Bonus: the equation you just picked is a shortcut for any Advance question later in the passage. “What would the distance be at 15 seconds?” is one multiplication once you have it.

One point, four plugs

Concept

Choose a point that is easy to read and not zero. Substitute it into every choice. The choice that reproduces the data is the model.

Worked Example

According to Figure 1, the results are best modeled by which equation?

  1. A. distance (m) = 4 × time (s)
  2. B. distance (m) = 0.25 × time (s)
  3. C. distance (m) = 4 × time (s) + 8
  4. D. distance (m) = 2 × time (s)

Based on Figure 1, the distance the cart had traveled after 15 seconds would be:

  1. A. 30 m
  2. B. 45 m
  3. C. 60 m
  4. D. 75 m

Question 1. Pick the point (2 s, 8 m). Plug time = 2 into each choice: A gives 8 ✓, B gives 0.5, C gives 16, D gives 4. Only A returns the 8 m the graph shows. Answer A. One point, four quick multiplications.

Question 2. Now the equation does the extending for you: 4 × 15 = 60 m. Answer C. Without the equation you would draw the line out to 15 s and land in the same place — this is just faster.

Average: Mixing Lands in the Middle

A mixing question usually shows up as the last question of a passage: “Suppose 50 mL of a 100 g/L solution is mixed with 50 mL of a 300 g/L solution…” The instinct to resist is adding. Mixing lightly sweet water with very sweet water does not give you super-sweet water; it gives you medium-sweet water. Equal volumes mix to the average.

  1. Find the two concentrations named in the question.
  2. Take the number in the middle (the average).
  3. Go to the figure and read the value at that new concentration — a Circle move.

100 g/L + 300 g/L, equal volumes → 200 g/L, then read the graph at 200. Not 400.

Unequal volumes? The mixture sits closer to whichever one there is more of. The test rarely goes there; if it does, the answer choices will be far enough apart that “closer to the bigger one” is enough.

Find the middle, then read the figure

Concept

Average the two concentrations (or, if the concentration is the same and the liquids differ, average the two readings). Then Circle the result on the figure.

Worked Example

Suppose 50 mL of a 100 g/L sugar-water solution is mixed with 50 mL of a 300 g/L sugar-water solution. According to Figure 1, the density of the mixture will be closest to:

  1. A. 1.04 g/mL
  2. B. 1.08 g/mL
  3. C. 1.12 g/mL
  4. D. 1.16 g/mL

Two concentrations: 100 and 300, equal volumes. The middle is 200 g/L. Now Circle: go to 200 on the x-axis, up to the Water line (both solutions are sugar-water), across to density: about 1.08. Answer B.

The trap is D, 1.16 — the density at 400 g/L, which is what you get if you add 100 + 300. A and C are the densities of the two original solutions, for anyone who read only one of them.

Try It

Suppose 20 mL of a 100 g/L sugar solution in the alcohol mix is combined with 20 mL of a 200 g/L sugar solution in the alcohol mix. According to Figure 1, the density of the result will be closest to:

  1. A. 0.90 g/mL
  2. B. 0.93 g/mL
  3. C. 0.95 g/mL
  4. D. 1.00 g/mL

Middle of 100 and 200 is 150 g/L. Read the Alcohol mix line at 150: about 0.925. Answer B. Same move, different line — the question named the alcohol mix both times, so that is the only line you use.

Suppose 50 mL of a 200 g/L sugar-water solution is mixed with 50 mL of a 200 g/L sugar solution in the alcohol mix. According to Figure 1, the density of the mixture will be closest to:

  1. A. 0.95 g/mL
  2. B. 1.02 g/mL
  3. C. 1.08 g/mL
  4. D. 2.03 g/mL

This time the concentration is the same (200) but the liquids differ, so you average the two lines instead: water at 200 reads about 1.08, alcohol mix at 200 reads about 0.95. The middle is about 1.02. Answer B. Choice D (2.03) is the two densities added together — the same trap in a new costume.

$$T_{1/2} = \frac{0.7}{k}$$
