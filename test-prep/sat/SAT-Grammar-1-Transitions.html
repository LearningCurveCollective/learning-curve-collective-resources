What a Transition Question Is

Every one of these looks the same: a short passage, a blank, and “Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?” The four answer choices are transition words. A handful show up on every test, and each one takes well under a minute once you have the method down.

The job is always the same: figure out how the sentence with the blank relates to the one before it, then pick the word that says so.

2

Three Families: Same, So, Switch

Every transition word belongs to one of three families. Knowing the family is most of the work.

SAME
keeps going in the same direction
also · in addition · moreover
similarly · for example · in fact
I like American cheese.
Also, I like Swiss cheese.
SO
the second sentence is caused by the first sentence
therefore · as a result
consequently · thus · so
I like American cheese.
Therefore, I like grilled cheese.
SWITCH
the second sentence takes a turn or contradicts the first sentence
however · nevertheless · still
in contrast · on the other hand
I love melted cheese.
However, I hate pizza.

1

Same, So, Switch

Read the second sentence and ask: is it going the same way, is it a result (so), or does it switch direction?

Same: keeps going

Concept

The second sentence continues the first: it adds a point, gives an example, emphasizes, compares, clarifies, or tells what happened next.

Octopuses can change the color of their skin in under a second. Moreover, they can change its texture to mimic coral or sand.

I like American cheese. Also, I like Swiss cheese.

Add: also, furthermore, in addition, moreover
Example: for example, for instance, specifically
Emphasize: in fact, indeed
Compare: likewise, similarly
Clarify: in other words, that is, essentially
Sequence: then, next, subsequently, finally, previously

So: result or reason

Concept

The second sentence happens because of the first.

The town’s only bridge was closed for repairs all summer. As a result, the ferry ran twice as often.

I like American cheese. Therefore, I like grilled cheese.

Result: therefore, thus, hence, consequently, as a result, accordingly, as such, so, to this end  ·  Reason: because, since, for

Switch: changes direction

Concept

The second sentence contrasts with the first, pushes back on it, or offers a different option.

The recipe promises a crisp crust. However, the dough has to rest overnight to get there.

I love melted cheese. However, I hate pizza.

however, but, yet, still, nevertheless, nonetheless, even so, instead, rather, in contrast, on the other hand, on the contrary, conversely, although, though, even though, whereas, while, despite this, in spite of this, meanwhile, otherwise, regardless, in any case, alternatively, alternately

While appears twice on purpose: it can mean “at the same time” (Same) or “whereas” (Switch). Context decides.

As such = “as a” + the noun from the sentence before. It shows up as a choice a lot. Bamboo is a grass. As such, it regrows from its roots = “as a grass, it regrows.” If that swap reads cleanly, it is a So.

For = “because.” Both so and for are So words, but they point opposite ways: the road flooded, so school closed vs. school closed, for the road had flooded. Check which half is the reason.

1

Look Both Ways: The Blank Is Never About One Sentence

A transition links the sentence it sits in to the sentence before it. So read both, all of both.

The method:

  1. Find the blank and cross it out so it cannot pull you toward an answer.
  2. Look left: read the whole sentence before the blank, start to finish.
  3. Look right: read the whole sentence the blank sits in, start to finish, as one clean sentence with the blank gone.
  4. Ask: what does the right sentence do to the left one? Same, So, or Switch. That is the only question the blank is asking.

Where the transition sits changes where you look, not what it does

Concept

A transition can sit at the start of a sentence, in the middle between two commas, or at the end after a comma. In every position it does the same job: it points back at the previous sentence. What changes is how easy it is to see the two sentences you need to compare.

Start Bats navigate by echolocation. However, many species also have excellent eyesight.

Where to look: left and right. The sentence before the transition (Bats navigate by echolocation) and the sentence it opens (many species also have excellent eyesight). Two sentences, side by side.

Middle Bats navigate by echolocation. Many species, however, also have excellent eyesight.

Where to look: cross the transition out first, then read the sentence it was sitting in as one piece (Many species also have excellent eyesight). Now compare that whole sentence with the sentence before it. Do not compare the two halves on either side of the commas: Many species and also have excellent eyesight have no relationship on their own.

End Bats navigate by echolocation. Many species also have excellent eyesight, however.

Where to look: left and right again. The sentence before (Bats navigate by echolocation) and the sentence the transition closes (Many species also have excellent eyesight). The transition is at the very end, but it still reaches back to the previous sentence.

Same two sentences every time. Start, middle, or end, you are always comparing Bats navigate by echolocation with Many species also have excellent eyesight. The position only changes how much crossing out you have to do before you can see them clearly.

One comma or two?

Concept

The commas around a transition tell you where to look.

Two commas (or a period before and a comma after) mean the transition connects two sentences. Look at the sentence before and the sentence it sits in.

One comma, with the transition right after it and a complete thought on each side, means the transition connects the two halves of the same sentence. Look left of the comma and right of the comma: those two halves are your two ideas.

The museum stays open until nine on Fridays, so the galleries are busiest after dinner.

Where to look: left of the comma (the museum stays open until nine on Fridays) and right of the comma (the galleries are busiest after dinner). Open late causes busy after dinner, so so fits. The words that do this one-comma job are short: so, for, yet, but, and, whereas, while, although.

Worked example

Worked Example

The hotel advertises itself as the tallest in the city. Its rooftop pool, is closed for most of the year because of high winds.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. for example,
  2. B. likewise,
  3. C. therefore,
  4. D. however,

Look both ways. Cross out the blank and read the two sentences: The hotel is the tallest in the city. Its rooftop pool is closed most of the year because of wind.

Identify. The first sentence is a boast; the second is a drawback of the very thing being boasted about. Switch.

Name it. “But.”

Knock out. Therefore is So; for example and likewise are Same. Only however is a Switch. Answer: however.

Worked Example

Sound travels about four times faster in water than in air, whales can hear each other across enormous distances.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. whereas
  2. B. for
  3. C. so
  4. D. yet

One comma: so the two halves of this sentence are what you compare: sound travels faster in water and whales hear across huge distances.

The second half is a result of the first. So. Yet and whereas are Switches. For means “because” and points the wrong way: whales hearing far is not the reason sound is fast. Answer: so.

1

Identify: Same, So, or Switch?

Boil each sentence down to its core: who or what, and the point. Skip the jargon; you never need to know the topic. Then compare the two cores: same direction, result, or change of direction?

The method: say each sentence in five words or fewer. Then ask, “Does sentence 2 keep going, follow from, or push against sentence 1?”

Worked example: ignore the vocabulary

Worked Example

Tardigrades can survive being dried out completely, entering a state called cryptobiosis in which their metabolism nearly stops. specimens revived after years in a dried moss sample have gone on to feed and reproduce normally.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. In fact,
  2. B. As a result,
  3. C. Alternatively,
  4. D. Nevertheless,

You do not need to know what cryptobiosis is. Cores: Sentence 1: tardigrades survive drying. Sentence 2: revived ones live normally.

Sentence 2 backs up sentence 1 with a stronger version of the same point. Same, and specifically emphasis. Nevertheless and alternatively are Switches; as a result is So, but living normally is not caused by surviving drying: it is more proof of it. Answer: In fact.

Worked example: which kind of Same?

Worked Example

The city’s new bike lanes are separated from traffic by a raised curb. they are painted bright green so that drivers notice them at intersections.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. Therefore,
  2. B. However,
  3. C. In addition,
  4. D. For example,

Cores: lanes have a curb; lanes are painted green. Two safety features, same direction. Same.

Now the second question: what kind of Same? Green paint is not an example of a curb, so for example is out. It is a second feature, an addition. Answer: In addition.

Inside Same, ask one more question. Is sentence 2 adding a point, giving an example, emphasizing, comparing, clarifying, or telling what happened next? That is what separates for example from in fact from in addition.

Name It: Predict Before You Peek

Say your own transition before you read the choices: “but,” “so,” “for example.” Every choice is written to sound fine on its own, which is why plugging them in one at a time feels like a coin flip. A prediction turns the choices into a matching game.

The method: cover the choices, say the word you would write, then find the choice from that family. On this tab the choices stay hidden until you have typed your word.

Worked example

Worked Example

Most meteorites that reach the ground are small enough to hold in one hand. the largest known meteorite, found in Namibia, weighs about 60 tons.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. However,
  2. B. For example,
  3. C. In fact,
  4. D. As a result,

Cores: most are hand-sized; the biggest is 60 tons. You would write “but.” Switch.

Now the choices are easy: in fact and for example are Same, as a result is So. Answer: However. Notice that in fact would have sounded fine if you had read it first without a prediction.

  2. B. As a result,

  2. B. for
  3. C. whereas
  4. D. yet

  1. A. whereas
  2. B. so

Knock Out: Cross Off by Family

Once you know the family, the answer is close. There is only ever one right family, and inside it only ever one right word.

The method: name the right family, cross off every choice from the other families, and pick the one word that is left. If two from the right family survive, they cannot both be right: reread the two sentences and keep the one that matches exactly.

Worked example: all four moves

Worked Example

The printing press made books far cheaper to produce. books stayed out of reach for most families for another century, because paper itself remained costly.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A. For instance,
  2. B. Nevertheless,
  3. C. In other words,
  4. D. Consequently,

L: Look both ways. Cheaper to produce. Still out of reach for a century.

I: Identify. You would expect cheap books to reach people; they did not. Switch: and specifically “despite this.”

N: Name it. “Even so.”

K: Knock out. Consequently is So. For instance and in other words are Same. One Switch left. Answer: Nevertheless.

If you cannot name the family exactly, name what it is not. Similar ideas → cross out every Switch. Different ideas → cross out every Same and So. That alone is usually two or three eliminations.
2

Twins: Two Synonyms, Neither Can Be Correct

A question has exactly one right answer. So if two choices mean the same thing and work the same way in the sentence: therefore and consequently, say. Neither one can be it. Cross out both before you even read the passage.

The method: scan the four choices for a pair of twins. Strike both. Now you are choosing between two, and they are usually from different families.

Same family is not the same as twins. However and still are both Switch, but still means “despite this” and however does not: those can sit together with one of them right. Twins are interchangeable.

Strategy Tips: The Whole Guide in One Page

By Test Day

1Know the three families cold

Same (adds, example, emphasis, compare, clarify, next), So (result or reason), Switch (contrast). Most questions are decided at the family level.

2You never need to know the topic

Mosses, meteorites, monetary policy: the relationship between the sentences is all that matters.

L: Look both ways

3Read the sentence before, and all of the sentence with the blank

A transition connects two sentences. One sentence is never enough.

4Mid-sentence? Cross it out

Two commas around a transition mean it still points at the previous sentence. Cross it out and read both sentences clean. One comma means it joins the two halves of its own sentence.

I: Identify

5Five words per sentence

Boil each one down to its core, then compare: keeps going, follows from, or pushes against?

6Inside Same, ask which kind

Adding, example, emphasis, comparison, clarification, or sequence. That is the difference between for example, in fact, and in addition.

N: Name it

7Predict before you peek

Say your own word. Then the choices are a match, not a taste test.

8Two words to know cold

As such = “as a” + the noun before it (a So). For = “because”: it points the opposite way from so, so check which half is the reason.

K: Knock out

9Cross off by family first

The wrong families usually take three choices with them. Then compare exact meanings of whatever is left.

10Twins cancel

Two choices that mean the same thing cannot both be right, so neither is. Strike both before you read the passage.

11Use the eliminator

Digital SAT: turn on the ABC button and cross off as you Knock out. What survives is your answer.

Pacing

12These are fast points

A transition question should take well under a minute. Bank it, then spend the saved time on a harder reading question.

13Trust the family

If your family is right, the answer is in front of you. Do not talk yourself into a smooth-sounding word from the wrong family.
