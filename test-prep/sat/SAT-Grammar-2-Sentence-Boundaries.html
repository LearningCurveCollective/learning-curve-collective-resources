What a Boundary Question Is

Every one of these looks the same: a short passage, a blank, “Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?”, and four choices that differ only in punctuation: period, comma, semicolon, colon, dash. College Board calls this category Boundaries. It is the most common grammar question on the test, and it takes about fifteen seconds once you have the method down.

The job is always the same: decide whether the chunk on each side of the blank is a complete sentence, then pick the mark that fits.

1

Sentence or Fragment? Judge Each Side

A sentence needs two things: a subject and a real verb that goes with it. That is the whole test. Whether the chunk makes sense on its own has nothing to do with it: Many consider him the best is a sentence even though you have no idea who him is.

The method: cover the blank, look at the chunk on one side, and ask “subject? real verb?” Decide: sentence or fragment. Repeat on the other side.

Fragment tells

Concept

Five patterns that are never a sentence on their own. Spot one and the post is ✗.

Starts with a w-word
which, who, whose, where, when (in a statement) start a dependent clause. Which is the oldest bridge in the state ✗
-ing where the verb should be
An -ing word is not a verb. The bridge carrying trucks across the river ✗. Choices with being are almost always wrong for this reason.
Starts with because / although / when
Subordinating conjunctions make the clause dependent: after, although, as, because, before, if, since, though, unless, until, when, whereas, while. Because the bridge was closed ✗
Many of which / most of whom
Many of which were rebuilt ✗. But many of them were rebuilt ✓. The which is what makes it dependent.
Stops right after a which-clause
The bridge, which was built in 1824 ✗. The verb was belongs to which, so the bridge never gets one.

Things that do NOT change the answer

Concept

A pronoun subject. It carried trucks is exactly as much a sentence as The bridge carried trucks. Comma + it / he / she / they is the most common comma splice on the test precisely because the pronoun version looks harmless.

A transition or time word. However, it carried trucks and Today it carries bikes are still sentences. Transitions have zero grammatical weight, which is why , however, can never hold two sentences apart.

Eliminate Twins: Same Fence, Same Job

You met twins in the Transitions guide. Here they are even more useful. A period, a semicolon, and a comma + FANBOYS do the same job; they are interchangeable. A colon and a single dash are another matched pair. A question has one right answer, so if two twins are offered, neither can be it. That is why this step comes before you build the fence: twins go before you have judged anything.

The method: scan the choices for twins before you build anything. Strike both. What survives is usually a comma choice, and that tells you the fence was ✓ ▢ ✗ or ✗ ▢ ✓ all along.

Twins, precisely: . = ; = , and / , but / , so  ·  : = —. A colon is not a twin of a period (the colon needs the second half to explain the first), so a colon and a period can both appear with one of them right.

When both chunks are sentences, you have exactly three legal choices (period, semicolon, comma + FANBOYS), plus a colon or dash if the second sentence explains the first. A comma by itself is never one of them.

The method: both posts ✓ → cross out every choice with a bare comma and every choice with no mark at all. What is left is usually one choice.

Comma-splice tells

Concept

A comma splice is a comma trying to do a period’s job. Two patterns give it away instantly:

comma + pronoun: , it   , he   , she   , they   , this

The reef is visible from space, it stretches 1,400 miles. ✗

The reef is visible from space; it stretches 1,400 miles. ✓

comma + transition or time word: , however   , therefore   , today   , then

The panels supply a third of the power, however, the rest comes from the grid. ✗

The panels supply a third of the power; however, the rest comes from the grid. ✓

Transitions connection. A transition word never joins sentences; it only tells you the relationship. So ; however, and . However, are the builds you want, and , however, between two sentences is the most common wrong answer in this whole category.

FANBOYS rules

Concept

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. With a comma in front, a FANBOYS word is a full stop. Three things to know:

The comma + FANBOYS test
Swap , and for a period. Two sentences? Then the comma belongs. The reef is visible from space, and it stretches 1,400 miles. → …space. It stretches… ✓
No repeated subject, no comma
The reef is visible from space and stretches 1,400 miles. Swap in a period: Stretches 1,400 miles is not a sentence, so no comma. That is a ✓ ▢ ✗ fence.
Never after a stop
space. And it and space; and it are both wrong: the period or semicolon already did the job. Cross those out on sight.

When the Choices Change the Right Post

One more pattern that lives on this fence. The chunk on the right is not always fixed. Sometimes the choices swap the first word of the second chunk (which in one choice, it in another), and that word decides whether the chunk is a sentence. So judge that chunk for each choice, then check that the mark in the same choice fits what the chunk turned out to be.

The method: read the choices before you decide about the right-hand chunk. If they start it with different words, judge it once per choice. Only one choice will have a chunk and a mark that agree.

Strategy Tips: The Whole Guide in One Page

By Test Day

1A comma is a pause, not a stop

It can never hold two sentences apart. Period, semicolon, and comma + FANBOYS are the stops.

2Read the period and colon choices first

If the test offers you two sentences, it is often checking whether you noticed there are two.

3Watch out: the choices can change the right post

If one choice says it and another says which, the right chunk is a sentence in one and a fragment in the other. Judge it once per choice; only one will have a chunk and a mark that agree.

Sentence or fragment?

4Subject + real verb, nothing else

Meaning does not matter. It grows is a sentence.

5Know the five fragment tells

Starts with a w-word, -ing for a verb, starts with because/although/when, many of which, stops right after a which-clause.

Eliminate twins

6Period = semicolon = comma + FANBOYS

Colon = dash. Two twins offered means both are wrong, and the survivor is usually a comma, which tells you one chunk was a fragment.

✓ ▢ ✓ Two sentences

7Cross out every bare comma

And every choice with no mark. Comma + pronoun and comma + however are the splice tells.

8FANBOYS: comma in front, never after a stop

Swap , and for a period to check. No repeated subject means no comma at all.

✓ ▢ ✗ Sentence + fragment

9Cross out every period and semicolon

Then choose between comma, colon/dash, and nothing.

10Which pause?

Which and an -ing phrase take a comma. A list or explanation takes a colon or dash, but never before such as. A because or when clause usually takes nothing; though / although keep their comma.

✗ ▢ ✓ Fragment + sentence

11Fragment first: a comma, then the sentence

A dependent clause or an -ing/-ed opener ends at the comma. A period or semicolon strands it.

12One conjunction per pair of clauses

Although…, but is one too many. The first conjunction may be a whole line back.

Pacing

13Fifteen seconds

Build the fence, cross out by fence type, pick what is left. Bank the time for reading questions.

14Trust your call

If your ✓ / ✗ call is right, the answer follows. Do not talk yourself into a smooth-sounding comma between two sentences.
