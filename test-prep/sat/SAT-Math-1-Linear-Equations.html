Linear rules

A linear equation is a line, and the SAT asks the same handful of questions about it: read the slope and the intercepts, move between forms, find a value from two points, compare two lines, and count how many solutions a system has. Below, $m$ is the slope, $b$ is the $y$-intercept, and $(x_1, y_1)$ is a point you know.

★ Essential: the rules behind most SAT linear questions. Start here.Unstarred: shows up less often. Add it once the starred rules are automatic.
★Rule 1Five forms of a line

Same line, different costumes. Read the form you are handed; convert only at the end, to match the answer choices.

FormFormulaWhat it showsExample
Slope-intercept$y = mx + b$$m$ is the slope, $b$ is the $y$-intercept$y = -2x + 5$: falls $2$ per step right, crosses the $y$-axis at $5$
Point-slope$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$a known point $(x_1, y_1)$ and the slopeslope $3$ through $(2, -1)$: $y + 1 = 3(x - 2)$
Standard form$Ax + By = C$$m = -\dfrac{A}{B}$
$y$-intercept $\dfrac{C}{B}$,   $x$-intercept $\dfrac{C}{A}$		$3x + 4y = 24$: $m = -\tfrac{3}{4}$, $y$-intercept $6$, $x$-intercept $8$
General form$ax + by + c = 0$standard form moved to one side
$m = -\dfrac{a}{b}$,   $y$-intercept $-\dfrac{c}{b}$		$2x - 5y + 10 = 0$: $m = \tfrac{2}{5}$, $y$-intercept $2$
Intercept form$\dfrac{x}{a} + \dfrac{y}{b} = 1$$x$-intercept $a$ and $y$-intercept $b$, in plain sight$\dfrac{x}{8} + \dfrac{y}{6} = 1$ is $3x + 4y = 24$ again
Most errors in this family are sign slips in $-\tfrac{A}{B}$. When in doubt, solve for $y$ once.
★Rule 2Slope from two points
slope
$m = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$   (the same between any two points on the line)
function notation
$f(a) = c$  means the point  $(a, c)$
Example A“$f$ is linear with $f(1) = 4$ and $f(4) = 13$” is two points, $(1, 4)$ and $(4, 13)$, so $m = \dfrac{13 - 4}{4 - 1} = 3$
Example Bwith $m$ in hand, step to any other value: $f(10) = f(4) + 3(10 - 4) = 31$. Or find $b$ first: $4 = 3(1) + b$ gives $b = 1$, so $f(x) = 3x + 1$
Example Ca table is a list of points. Take the slope from two complete rows, then fill each blank by stepping: equal steps in $x$ give equal steps in $y$
A horizontal line ($y = 4$) has slope $0$. A vertical line ($x = 4$) has no slope at all. “Parallel to the $x$-axis” means slope $0$.
★Rule 3Slope is a rate of change
change in the output
$\Delta y = m \cdot \Delta x$   (the intercept never enters a change)
average rate on a curve
$\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$   (the slope of the line through the two endpoints)
Example A$F = \tfrac{9}{5}C + 32$. A rise of $45$ degrees in $F$ is a rise of $45 \div \tfrac{9}{5} = 25$ degrees in $C$. The $32$ never enters
Example Bin context, the slope is “per”: $P = 0.08t + 15$ charges $0.08$ dollars per minute on top of a $15$ dollar base. Fifty more minutes cost $0.08(50) = 4$ dollars more
Example Cfor $g(x) = x^2$ from $x = 1$ to $x = 4$: average rate $= \dfrac{16 - 1}{4 - 1} = 5$
“Which interval has the greatest average rate of change?” means: compute rise over run for every interval. The steepest secant wins, not the interval with the biggest values.
★Rule 4Parallel and perpendicular
parallel
$m_1 = m_2$   (and $b_1 \neq b_2$, or it is the same line)
perpendicular
$m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$,   so $m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}$: flip it and negate it
Example Anegative reciprocals: $\tfrac{2}{3} \to -\tfrac{3}{2}$,   $-5 \to \tfrac{1}{5}$,   $1 \to -1$
Example Bthe line parallel to $4x + 2y = 8$ through $(3, -2)$: the given slope is $-\tfrac{4}{2} = -2$, so $y + 2 = -2(x - 3)$, which is $y = -2x + 4$
Example Cthe line perpendicular to $2x - 3y = 6$ through $(4, -1)$: the given slope is $\tfrac{2}{3}$, the perpendicular slope is $-\tfrac{3}{2}$, so $y + 1 = -\tfrac{3}{2}(x - 4)$
A horizontal line is perpendicular to a vertical one even though their slopes cannot be multiplied.
★Rule 5How many solutions does a system have?

Compare the two lines, written as $y = m_1x + b_1$ and $y = m_2x + b_2$, or as $a_1x + b_1y = c_1$ and $a_2x + b_2y = c_2$:

OutcomeSlope-intercept testStandard-form test
One solution (the lines cross)$m_1 \neq m_2$$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} \neq \dfrac{b_1}{b_2}$
No solution (parallel)$m_1 = m_2$ and $b_1 \neq b_2$$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} \neq \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$
Infinitely many (same line)$m_1 = m_2$ and $b_1 = b_2$$\dfrac{a_1}{a_2} = \dfrac{b_1}{b_2} = \dfrac{c_1}{c_2}$
Example A$y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 3$: parallel, no solution.   $y = 2x + 1$ and $2y = 4x + 2$: the same line, infinitely many
Example B$3x - 4y = 7$ and $6x + ky = 20$ have no solution when $\dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{-4}{k}$, so $k = -8$. (Check the constants: $\tfrac{7}{20} \neq \tfrac{1}{2}$, so the lines are different. ✓)
Example C$2x + 7y = 5$ and $ax + by = 15$ have infinitely many solutions when the second is an exact multiple of the first: the multiplier is $15 \div 5 = 3$, so $a = 6$ and $b = 21$
Example Done solution: eliminate (line up one variable, then add or subtract), substitute, or graph both lines in Desmos and click the intersection
“Infinitely many” hides a multiplier: read it off the terms you can see, then apply it to the unknowns.
Rule 6Midpoint and distance
midpoint
$M = \left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\; \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$   (average each coordinate)
distance
$d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$   (Pythagoras with $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ as legs)
point splitting $AB$ as $a : b$
$P = \left(\dfrac{b\,x_1 + a\,x_2}{a + b},\; \dfrac{b\,y_1 + a\,y_2}{a + b}\right)$   (the midpoint is the $1 : 1$ case)
Example Athe midpoint of $(2, -3)$ and $(10, 5)$ is $(6, 1)$. Given the midpoint and one end, double and subtract: if $M = (6, 1)$ and $A = (2, -3)$, then $B = (2 \cdot 6 - 2,\; 2 \cdot 1 + 3) = (10, 5)$
Example Bthe distance from $(1, 2)$ to $(7, 10)$ is $\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$
Example Cfor $A(0, 0)$, $B(10, 5)$ and $AP : PB = 2 : 3$: $P = \left(\tfrac{3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 10}{5}, \tfrac{3 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 5}{5}\right) = (4, 2)$
Rare on the digital SAT, but cheap to keep. When a midpoint has unknown coordinates, write one small equation per coordinate.
Rule 7Area enclosed by lines
area
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$,   with the base laid on an axis
the vertices
intercepts (set $x = 0$ or $y = 0$) and intersections (solve the system)
Example A$y = -\tfrac{1}{2}x + 4$ and the two axes: $x$-intercept $8$, $y$-intercept $4$, area $\tfrac{1}{2}(8)(4) = 16$
Example B$f(x) = 2x - 4$, $g(x) = -x + 5$, and the $y$-axis: the lines meet where $2x - 4 = -x + 5$, so $x = 3$ and $y = 2$. The $y$-intercepts $-4$ and $5$ make a base of $9$ on the $y$-axis, and the height is the horizontal distance to $x = 3$. Area $= \tfrac{1}{2}(9)(3) = 13.5$
Put the base on an axis whenever one is available; the height is then just a coordinate of the third vertex.
Rule 8Reflecting a line
Reflect acrossPoint ruleIn the equationEffect on $y = mx + b$
the $x$-axis$(x, y) \to (x, -y)$replace $y$ with $-y$$m \to -m$,   $b \to -b$
the $y$-axis$(x, y) \to (-x, y)$replace $x$ with $-x$$m \to -m$,   $b$ stays
the line $y = x$$(x, y) \to (y, x)$swap $x$ and $y$$m \to \dfrac{1}{m}$
the origin$(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$replace both$m$ stays,   $b \to -b$
Example Areflect $y = 2x - 3$ across the $x$-axis: $-y = 2x - 3$, which is $y = -2x + 3$
Example Bacross the $y$-axis: $y = 2(-x) - 3 = -2x - 3$. The $y$-intercept stays, so a line and its $y$-axis mirror always meet on the $y$-axis
Example Cin standard form, $2x - 3y = 6$ becomes $2x + 3y = 6$ across the $x$-axis and $-2x - 3y = 6$ across the $y$-axis
Reflect the equation, not the picture: the point rule tells you which variable to replace.
Moves that are not allowed

The statements below look reasonable but are false, and each one generates a standard wrong answer. Keep them in mind.

Case 1not allowed:   reading $A$ as the slope of $Ax + By = C$
The slope is $-\tfrac{A}{B}$. For $3x + 4y = 24$ it is $-\tfrac{3}{4}$, not $3$ and not $-3$. Solve for $y$ once if you doubt it.
Case 2not allowed:   half of a perpendicular flip
For a line perpendicular to slope $\tfrac{2}{3}$, both $-\tfrac{2}{3}$ (sign only) and $\tfrac{3}{2}$ (reciprocal only) are wrong, and both are in the choices. The answer is $-\tfrac{3}{2}$. Multiply the two slopes: anything but $-1$ is wrong.
Case 3not allowed:   “same slope” means no solution
Same slope with the same intercept is the same line: infinitely many solutions. Check the constants after you check the slopes.
Case 4not allowed:   $f(2) = 5$ as the point $(5, 2)$
The input is $x$. $f(2) = 5$ is the point $(2, 5)$. Write the points down before you subtract anything.
Case 5not allowed:   plugging a change into the whole equation
If $F$ rises by $45$, that is $\Delta F = 45$, not $F = 45$. Use $\Delta y = m\,\Delta x$: the intercept drops out of every change.
Case 6not allowed:   finding the $x$-intercept by setting $x = 0$
The $x$-intercept is where $y = 0$. Setting $x = 0$ gives the $y$-intercept. Say it out loud: “on the $x$-axis, $y$ is zero.”

Worked examples & practice

Everything on the rules tab runs on one idea: a line is a starting value plus a constant rate. The intercept is where you start, the slope is how fast you move, and every SAT question about lines is asking you to find one of those two numbers, or to compare them across two lines.

The walkthrough below takes the rules one at a time, each with a worked example, the trap it pairs with, and a ten-second check. The starred steps are the essentials. Reading a rule isn't knowing it; the check is the point.
