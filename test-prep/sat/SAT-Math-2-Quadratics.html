Quadratic rules

A quadratic is a parabola, and the SAT asks the same handful of questions about it: read the roots, the vertex, and the $y$-intercept from whichever form you are handed, factor or use the formula to solve, use the discriminant to count solutions, and find where a line meets the curve. Below, the quadratic is $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ with $a \neq 0$, its roots (the $x$-intercepts) are $r_1$ and $r_2$, and its vertex is $(h, k)$.

★ Essential: the rules behind most SAT quadratic questions. Start here.Unstarred: shows up less often. Add it once the starred rules are automatic.
★Rule 1Three forms of a parabola

Same parabola, different costumes. Each form puts a different feature in plain sight, and the SAT asks outright which form shows which feature.

FormFormulaWhat it showsExample
Standard$y = ax^2 + bx + c$$c$ is the $y$-intercept
$a > 0$ opens up, $a < 0$ opens down
axis of symmetry $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$		$y = x^2 - 6x + 5$: crosses the $y$-axis at $5$, opens up, axis $x = 3$
Factored$y = a(x - r_1)(x - r_2)$the roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ are the $x$-intercepts
the vertex sits halfway between them		$y = (x - 1)(x - 5)$: $x$-intercepts $1$ and $5$, axis $x = 3$
Vertex$y = a(x - h)^2 + k$vertex $(h, k)$
$k$ is the minimum value ($a > 0$) or the maximum value ($a < 0$)		$y = (x - 3)^2 - 4$: vertex $(3, -4)$, minimum value $-4$
Example Aall three are the same parabola: $(x - 1)(x - 5) = x^2 - 6x + 5 = (x - 3)^2 - 4$. Expand to move right in the table; factor or complete the square to move left
Example B“Which form displays the $x$-intercepts as constants?” is factored form. “The vertex” or “the minimum value” is vertex form. “The $y$-intercept” is standard form
Example C$a$ is the same number in every form and never moves the axis: $y = 2(x - 1)(x - 5)$ still has roots $1$ and $5$ and vertex at $x = 3$, but now $k = 2(3 - 1)(3 - 5) = -8$
The $y$-intercept of vertex or factored form is found by setting $x = 0$: for $y = 2(x - 3)^2 - 8$ it is $2(9) - 8 = 10$, not $-8$.
★Rule 2Factor, then set each factor to zero

Factoring writes an expression as a product. A product is zero only when one of its factors is zero, so a factored equation hands you its solutions.

PatternExpandedFactoredExample
Perfect square$a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$(a + b)^2$$x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2$: the middle term is twice the product of the two square roots
Perfect square$a^2 - 2ab + b^2$$(a - b)^2$$4x^2 - 12x + 9 = (2x - 3)^2$
Difference of squares$a^2 - b^2$$(a + b)(a - b)$no middle term: $x^2 - 49 = (x + 7)(x - 7)$,   $9n^2 - 1 = (3n + 1)(3n - 1)$
Trinomial, $a = 1$$x^2 + bx + c$$(x + p)(x + q)$ with $p + q = b$, $pq = c$$x^2 - 2x - 3 = (x - 3)(x + 1)$: the numbers $-3$ and $1$ add to $-2$ and multiply to $-3$
Trinomial, $a \neq 1$$ax^2 + bx + c$two numbers with product $ac$ and sum $b$, then group$6x^2 + x - 1$: product $-6$, sum $1$: $3$ and $-2$. $6x^2 + 3x - 2x - 1 = (3x - 1)(2x + 1)$
Common factor first$12x^2 + 2x - 2$$2(6x^2 + x - 1)$$= 2(3x - 1)(2x + 1)$: pull the greatest common factor before anything else
zero product
$(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0$  means  $x = r_1$  or  $x = r_2$
square root
$x^2 = n$ gives $x = \pm\sqrt{n}$;   $(x - h)^2 = n$ gives $x = h \pm \sqrt{n}$
Example A$x^2 - 7x + 12 = 0$: $(x - 3)(x - 4) = 0$, so $x = 3$ or $x = 4$. The signs inside the factors are the opposite of the roots
Example B$2x^2 + 5x - 12 = 0$: product $ac = -24$, sum $5$: $8$ and $-3$. $2x^2 + 8x - 3x - 12 = 2x(x + 4) - 3(x + 4) = (2x - 3)(x + 4)$, so $x = \tfrac{3}{2}$ or $x = -4$
Example Cstructure, not solving: if $(x - 3)(x + 3) = a$, then $(2x - 6)(x + 3) = 2(x - 3)(x + 3) = 2a$. Look for the piece you already know inside the new expression
Example D$x^2 - y^2 = 24$ with positive integers: $(x - y)(x + y) = 24$, and the two factors must be both even or both odd. $2 \cdot 12$ gives $x = 7$, $y = 5$; $4 \cdot 6$ gives $x = 5$, $y = 1$
Desmos factors for you: graph $y = 2x^2 + 5x - 12$ and click the $x$-intercepts. Use that to check. “Equivalent expression” questions have nothing to click, so the patterns above still have to be automatic.
★Rule 3The quadratic formula and the discriminant
quadratic formula
$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$   (for $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$; set it equal to zero first)
discriminant
$D = b^2 - 4ac$   (the part under the root; its sign counts the real solutions)
tangent
a line is tangent to a parabola exactly when the combined equation has $D = 0$
DiscriminantReal solutionsGraph of $y = ax^2 + bx + c$
$D > 0$two (rational if $D$ is a perfect square, irrational if not)crosses the $x$-axis twice
$D = 0$one, a double root at $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$touches the $x$-axis at its vertex: the axis is tangent
$D < 0$nonenever meets the $x$-axis: entirely above it ($a > 0$) or entirely below it ($a < 0$)
Example A$x^2 - 6x + 7 = 0$: $D = 36 - 28 = 8$, two irrational solutions, $x = \dfrac{6 \pm \sqrt{8}}{2} = 3 \pm \sqrt{2}$
Example B$x^2 + 2x + k = 0$ has exactly one solution when $4 - 4k = 0$, so $k = 1$.   $2x^2 + 3x + 5 = 0$ has $D = 9 - 40 < 0$: no real solutions
Example C$y = 2x + k$ is tangent to $y = x^2 - 4$: set them equal, $x^2 - 2x - 4 - k = 0$. Tangent means $D = 0$: $4 + 4(4 + k) = 20 + 4k = 0$, so $k = -5$
Set the equation equal to zero before you read $a$, $b$, and $c$. In $x^2 - 2x = 2x + k$, $b$ is $-4$, not $-2$.
★Rule 4Vertex, axis of symmetry, maximum and minimum
axis of symmetry
$h = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = \dfrac{r_1 + r_2}{2}$   (halfway between the roots, or between any two points with the same $y$)
vertex value
$k = f(h)$: the minimum if $a > 0$, the maximum if $a < 0$
completing the square
$x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \dfrac{b^2}{4} + c$
symmetry
$f(h - t) = f(h + t)$ for every $t$
Example A$y = x^2 - 6x + 5$: $h = 3$ and $k = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4$. Completing the square agrees: $(x - 3)^2 - 9 + 5 = (x - 3)^2 - 4$
Example Bin context, $h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t + 80$ is a ball's height. It starts at $80$ (set $t = 0$), peaks at $t = \dfrac{-64}{2(-16)} = 2$ with height $h(2) = 144$, and lands when $h = 0$: $-16(t - 5)(t + 1) = 0$, so $t = 5$
Example Cif $f(2) = f(8)$ for a quadratic $f$, the vertex is at $x = 5$, whether those two outputs are $0$ or $7$
“The minimum value” is $k$; “the $x$ at which the minimum occurs” is $h$. Both are always in the answer choices.
★Rule 5A line meets a parabola

Set the two $y$'s equal, move everything to one side, and solve the quadratic. Every solution $x$ is an intersection; plug it into the line, the easier equation, to get $y$.

OutcomeAlgebra testPicture
Two solutions$D > 0$ for the combined equationthe line cuts through the parabola at two points
One solution$D = 0$the line is tangent to the parabola
No solution$D < 0$the line misses the parabola
Example A$y = 2x + 1$ and $y = x^2 - 5x + 7$: $x^2 - 5x + 7 = 2x + 1$ gives $x^2 - 7x + 6 = (x - 1)(x - 6) = 0$. At $x = 1$, $y = 3$; at $x = 6$, $y = 13$. The solutions are $(1, 3)$ and $(6, 13)$
Example B$y = x^2 - x - 12$ and $y = 3x$: $x^2 - 4x - 12 = 0$ has $D = 16 + 48 = 64 > 0$, so the system has two solutions ($x = 6$ and $x = -2$)
Example Cwhen a line and a parabola enclose a region, the intersections are the corners. From there it is the area rule from the linear sheet: base on an axis, or split into triangles and trapezoids
In Desmos, graph both and click the intersection points. For a question with an unknown $k$, add a slider and drag it until the line just touches the curve; then confirm with $D = 0$.
Rule 6Sum and product of the roots
sum
$r_1 + r_2 = -\dfrac{b}{a}$
product
$r_1 r_2 = \dfrac{c}{a}$
build from the roots
$x^2 - (r_1 + r_2)\,x + r_1 r_2 = 0$
two useful rewrites
$\dfrac{1}{r_1} + \dfrac{1}{r_2} = \dfrac{r_1 + r_2}{r_1 r_2}$,   $r_1^2 + r_2^2 = (r_1 + r_2)^2 - 2 r_1 r_2$
Example Awhere it comes from: $a(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = ax^2 - a(r_1 + r_2)\,x + a\,r_1 r_2$. Match the coefficients with $ax^2 + bx + c$
Example Bone root of $2x^2 + x - k = 0$ is $1$. The sum of the roots is $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the other root is $-\tfrac{1}{2} - 1 = -\tfrac{3}{2}$. ($k$ was never needed; it is $3$)
Example Cif $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the roots of $x^2 + 4x - 12 = 0$, then $\dfrac{1}{\alpha} + \dfrac{1}{\beta} = \dfrac{\alpha + \beta}{\alpha\beta} = \dfrac{-4}{-12} = \tfrac{1}{3}$. Check by factoring: $(x + 6)(x - 2)$ gives $-\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}$. ✓
Rare on the digital SAT, but it turns a two-minute factoring problem into a ten-second one, and a question with an unknown constant in the $c$ slot is built for it.
Rule 7Shifting and reflecting a parabola
MoveNew equationEffect on the vertex $(h, k)$
up $n$ units$f(x) + n$$(h, k + n)$
right $n$ units$f(x - n)$$(h + n, k)$
across the $x$-axis$-f(x)$$(h, -k)$, and $a \to -a$: a minimum becomes a maximum
across the $y$-axis$f(-x)$$(-h, k)$
Example A$f(x) = (x - 2)^2 - 5$ shifted left $3$ and up $4$: $g(x) = (x + 1)^2 - 1$, vertex $(-1, -1)$
Example Bthe graph of $y = x^2 - 4x$ translated down $3$: $y = x^2 - 4x - 3$. Its new $x$-intercepts, $2 \pm \sqrt{7}$, come from the new equation, not from sliding the old ones
Example C$-f(x)$ flips the parabola: $y = -(x - 2)^2 + 5$ opens down with maximum $5$. The $x$-intercepts stay put, because they sit on the mirror line
Inside the parentheses the sign runs backwards: $(x - 2)^2$ is a shift to the right by $2$.
Moves that are not allowed

The statements below look reasonable but are false, and each one generates a standard wrong answer. Keep them in mind.

Case 1not allowed:   reading the roots with the signs as written
$(x - 3)(x + 5) = 0$ has roots $3$ and $-5$. Set each factor to zero: $x - 3 = 0$ gives $x = 3$. The signs flip.
Case 2not allowed:   dividing both sides by $x$
$x^2 = 5x$ has two solutions, $0$ and $5$. Dividing by $x$ throws away $x = 0$. Move everything to one side and factor: $x(x - 5) = 0$.
Case 3not allowed:   a square root with one sign
$(x - 3)^2 = 16$ gives $x - 3 = \pm 4$, so $x = 7$ or $x = -1$. Half of the answer lives in the minus sign.
Case 4not allowed:   $(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 9$
The middle term is $2 \cdot 3 \cdot x$: $(x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9$. For the same reason $\sqrt{x^2 + 9}$ is not $x + 3$.
Case 5not allowed:   reading $a$, $b$, $c$ before the equation equals zero
$x^2 - 2x = 2x + k$ becomes $x^2 - 4x - k = 0$, so $b = -4$ and $c = -k$. The discriminant uses the moved version, every time.
Case 6not allowed:   copying the sign inside the parentheses into the vertex
$y = 2(x + 1)^2 - 3$ has vertex $(-1, -3)$: $h$ flips sign, $k$ does not. Check by plugging in $x = -1$, which makes the square zero.

Worked examples & practice

Everything on the rules tab runs on one idea: a quadratic is a product of two linear factors, and its graph is symmetric about its vertex. The factors hand you the roots, the symmetry hands you the vertex, and every SAT question about a parabola is asking for one of those, for how many roots there are, or for where a line meets the curve.

The walkthrough below takes the rules one at a time, each with a worked example, the trap it pairs with, and a ten-second check. The starred steps are the essentials. Reading a rule isn't knowing it; the check is the point.
