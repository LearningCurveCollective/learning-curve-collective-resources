Triangle rules

Triangle questions on the SAT draw on a short list of facts: the angles that parallel lines make, the angles inside a triangle, the Pythagorean theorem, and the two special right triangles. Three less common ideas complete the list: which side lengths can form a triangle, how areas compare when triangles share a height, and what the altitude to the hypotenuse does. Most of these questions come with a figure, so mark what you know on the figure before you compute. As you practice, start by identifying the question type. Then decide which rule fits the figure, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1Parallel lines and a transversal

When a line crosses two parallel lines at a slant, it forms eight angles but only two different measures. All the acute angles are equal, all the obtuse angles are equal, and one of each adds to $180^\circ$. Decide which kind of pair you have before you write an equation.

PairWhere to find itRelationship
Vertical anglesacross from each other at one crossingequal
Corresponding anglesthe same corner at each crossingequal
Alternate interior anglesbetween the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversalequal
Same-side interior anglesbetween the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversaladd to $180^\circ$
Linear pairnext to each other on a straight lineadd to $180^\circ$
Example AOne of the eight angles measures $112^\circ$. Three others also measure $112^\circ$, and the remaining four measure $180^\circ - 112^\circ = 68^\circ$.
Example BSame-side interior angles $(3x + 10)^\circ$ and $(2x + 20)^\circ$ add to $180^\circ$: $5x + 30 = 180$, so $x = 30$ and the angles are $100^\circ$ and $80^\circ$.
Example CAlternate interior angles $(5x - 8)^\circ$ and $(3x + 26)^\circ$ are equal: $2x = 34$, so $x = 17$ and each angle is $77^\circ$.
These relationships need parallel lines. Look for a statement such as $\ell \parallel m$ before you use them. After solving, put $x$ back in and confirm that the angle measures make sense.
★Rule 2Angles in a triangle

The three interior angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$. Two facts follow from that and come up often: an exterior angle equals the sum of the two interior angles that do not touch it, and equal sides are across from equal angles.

angle sum
$a + b + c = 180$
exterior angle
$d = a + b$   (the two interior angles that do not touch it)
isosceles
two equal sides, so the two angles across from them are equal
equilateral
three equal sides and three $60^\circ$ angles
right triangle
the two acute angles add to $90^\circ$
Example ATwo angles measure $47^\circ$ and $68^\circ$. The third is $180^\circ - 115^\circ = 65^\circ$, and the exterior angle at that vertex is $47^\circ + 68^\circ = 115^\circ$.
Example BAn isosceles triangle with a $40^\circ$ angle between its equal sides has two more angles of $\dfrac{180^\circ - 40^\circ}{2} = 70^\circ$. If the $40^\circ$ angle is one of the two equal angles instead, the angles are $40^\circ$, $40^\circ$ and $100^\circ$.
Example CWith parallel lines: in the figure below, $\ell \parallel m$. Draw a line through the bend that is parallel to both. Alternate interior angles carry the $35^\circ$ and the $50^\circ$ to the bend, so the angle there measures $35^\circ + 50^\circ = 85^\circ$.
When a triangle or a bend sits between parallel lines, use Rule 1 to move an angle to where you need it. Then finish with the angle sum or the exterior angle.
★Rule 3The Pythagorean theorem

In a right triangle, the squares of the two legs add up to the square of the hypotenuse, the side across from the right angle. Use it when you know two sides of a right triangle and need the third.

the theorem
$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$   ($c$ is the hypotenuse)
a missing leg
$a^2 = c^2 - b^2$   (subtract when the missing side is a leg)
common triples
$3$-$4$-$5$,   $5$-$12$-$13$,   $8$-$15$-$17$,   $7$-$24$-$25$
multiples work too
$6$-$8$-$10$,   $9$-$12$-$15$,   $10$-$24$-$26$
as areas
the squares built on the legs have areas $a^2$ and $b^2$; together they equal the area of the square built on the hypotenuse
Example ALegs $9$ and $12$: $81 + 144 = 225$, so the hypotenuse is $15$. This is $3$-$4$-$5$ with each side tripled.
Example BHypotenuse $26$ and one leg $10$: $676 - 100 = 576$, so the other leg is $24$.
Example CLegs $4$ and $6$: $16 + 36 = 52$, so the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{52} = 2\sqrt{13}$. Answer choices usually show the simplified radical.
Example DSquares built on the legs have areas $15$ and $34$. The square on the hypotenuse has area $49$, so the hypotenuse is $7$. The leg lengths are not needed. The same addition works for any three similar shapes built on the sides, such as semicircles or equilateral triangles.
Recognizing a triple can save the arithmetic. When the numbers are not a triple, Desmos can do the arithmetic: $\sqrt{4^2 + 6^2}$ is about $7.21$, which you can compare with the decimal value of each choice.
★Rule 4Special right triangles

Two right triangles have fixed side ratios, so one side gives you the other two. A $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle is half of a square, cut along the diagonal. A $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle is half of an equilateral triangle, cut along the height.

TriangleSidesHow to use itExample
$45$-$45$-$90$$x : x : x\sqrt{2}$the legs are equal; multiply a leg by $\sqrt{2}$ for the hypotenuse; divide the hypotenuse by $\sqrt{2}$ for a leglegs $5$: hypotenuse $5\sqrt{2}$
$30$-$60$-$90$$x : x\sqrt{3} : 2x$the short leg $x$ is across from the $30^\circ$ angle; the hypotenuse is twice the short leg; the long leg is the short leg times $\sqrt{3}$short leg $4$: long leg $4\sqrt{3}$, hypotenuse $8$
start with $x$
find the short leg (or a leg of the $45$-$45$-$90$) first, then build the other sides from it
equilateral, side $s$
height $\dfrac{s\sqrt{3}}{2}$,   area $\dfrac{s^2\sqrt{3}}{4}$
square, side $s$
diagonal $s\sqrt{2}$
dividing by a root
$\dfrac{12}{\sqrt{3}} = 4\sqrt{3}$   and   $\dfrac{14}{\sqrt{2}} = 7\sqrt{2}$
Example AA $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $14$: each leg is $\dfrac{14}{\sqrt{2}} = 7\sqrt{2}$.
Example BA $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $18$: the short leg is $9$ and the long leg is $9\sqrt{3}$.
Example CA $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with long leg $12$: the short leg is $\dfrac{12}{\sqrt{3}} = 4\sqrt{3}$ and the hypotenuse is $8\sqrt{3}$.
Example DAn equilateral triangle with side $10$: the height is $5\sqrt{3}$ and the area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(10)(5\sqrt{3}) = 25\sqrt{3}$.
A question may not name the triangle for you. Look for $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ or $60^\circ$ angles, squares, and equilateral triangles. Rule 5The triangle inequality

Three lengths form a triangle only when the two shorter ones add up to more than the longest. With two sides known, that limits the third side to a range. Inside a triangle, the longest side is across from the largest angle.

the third side
$|a - b| < c < a + b$   (more than the difference, less than the sum)
quick test
add the two shortest lengths; the total has to be greater than the longest
equal is not enough
$4$, $5$, $9$ does not form a triangle: $4 + 5 = 9$, so the sides lie flat
side and angle order
longest side across from the largest angle; shortest side across from the smallest angle
Example ASides $5$ and $8$: the third side is between $3$ and $13$. If it is an integer, it can be $4$ through $12$, which is nine values.
Example B$7$, $10$, $18$ is not a triangle, because $7 + 10 = 17$ is less than $18$. $7$, $10$, $16$ is a triangle, because $17$ is greater than $16$.
Example CA triangle has angles of $50^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $70^\circ$. The side across from the $70^\circ$ angle is the longest, and the side across from the $50^\circ$ angle is the shortest.
When a side is written with a variable, such as $2x - 1$, write the range for the whole side first. Then solve for $x$.
Rule 6Triangles that share a height

The area of a triangle is $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$. When two triangles have the same height, the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the height are the same in both, so their areas compare the way their bases do.

same height
$\dfrac{\text{area}_1}{\text{area}_2} = \dfrac{\text{base}_1}{\text{base}_2}$
where to look
triangles that share a vertex and have their bases on the same line
a median
equal bases, so the two triangles have equal areas
part of the whole
bases in the ratio $2 : 5$ give areas that are $\tfrac{2}{7}$ and $\tfrac{5}{7}$ of the whole triangle
Example AIn the figure, $QS = 4$ and $SR = 10$. If the area of triangle $PQS$ is $18$, each unit of base carries $18 \div 4 = 4.5$ units of area. The area of triangle $PSR$ is $45$, and the area of triangle $PQR$ is $63$.
Example BA point on the diagonal of a rectangle: if $T$ divides diagonal $\overline{WY}$ of rectangle $WXYZ$ so that $WT : TY = 1 : 3$, then triangles $WXT$ and $TXY$ share the height from $X$, and their areas are in the ratio $1 : 3$.
Example CThe diagonal of a rectangle splits it into two triangles of equal area. Once you know the area of one of them, the rectangle is twice that.
The ratio works because of the shared height. Before you use it, confirm that the two triangles have the same vertex across from bases on one line.
Rule 7The altitude to the hypotenuse

Draw the altitude from the right angle to the hypotenuse, and a right triangle splits into two smaller right triangles. Each one shares an angle with the original, so all three triangles are similar and their sides are proportional. Three relationships follow.

the altitude
$h^2 = p \cdot q$
each leg
$a^2 = p\,(p + q)$   and   $b^2 = q\,(p + q)$   (the piece beside the leg, times the whole hypotenuse)
area two ways
$\tfrac{1}{2}ab = \tfrac{1}{2}(p + q)\,h$,   so   $h = \dfrac{ab}{p + q}$
Example AThe altitude splits the hypotenuse into pieces of $4$ and $9$: $h^2 = 36$, so $h = 6$.
Example BPieces of $2$ and $6$: the leg beside the $2$ has $a^2 = 2 \cdot 8 = 16$, so $a = 4$. The other leg has $b^2 = 6 \cdot 8 = 48$, so $b = 4\sqrt{3}$.
Example CLegs $14$ and $48$: the hypotenuse is $50$, and the altitude to it is $\dfrac{14 \cdot 48}{50} = 13.44$.
If you forget one of these, the Pythagorean theorem still works in each of the three right triangles. These relationships are a shorter path to the same result. Similar triangles have their own sheet, which covers the scale factor and how lengths and areas grow: SAT Math 4C: Similar Figures →
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common setup and interpretation mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1using a parallel-line pair when the lines are not parallel
Corresponding and alternate interior angles are equal only when the two lines are parallel. Look for $\ell \parallel m$ in the question before you set two angles equal.
Common mistake 2setting same-side interior angles equal
They add to $180^\circ$. If one is $70^\circ$, the other is $110^\circ$. After solving, put $x$ back in: a negative angle measure means the setup needs another look.
Common mistake 3adding in the angle beside an exterior angle
The exterior angle equals the two interior angles that do not touch it. The interior angle beside it is its supplement, and it is not part of that sum.
Common mistake 4adding the squares when the missing side is a leg
Hypotenuse $13$ and leg $5$: the other leg is $\sqrt{169 - 25} = 12$, not $\sqrt{194}$. The hypotenuse is the longest side, so a leg that comes out longer than the hypotenuse signals a slip.
Common mistake 5putting $x\sqrt{3}$ on the hypotenuse
In a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle the hypotenuse is $2x$ and the long leg is $x\sqrt{3}$. Since $\sqrt{3}$ is about $1.73$, the hypotenuse is still the longest side.
Common mistake 6calling the given side $x$ without checking
The given side might be the long leg or the hypotenuse. Mark which angle each side is across from, then decide whether the given length is $x$, $x\sqrt{3}$ or $2x$.
Common mistake 7including the endpoints in the triangle inequality
With sides $5$ and $8$, the third side cannot be $3$ or $13$. At those lengths the three sides lie flat on one line.
Common mistake 8stopping at a piece when the question asks for the whole
After a ratio gives you one triangle’s area, reread the question. It may ask for the other piece, the whole triangle, or a rectangle that is twice the triangle.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: a figure tells you more than it states. Parallel lines give you equal angles, a right angle gives you the Pythagorean theorem, and a $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ or $60^\circ$ angle gives you a side ratio. Each question asks you to notice which of these the figure contains, then use it.

The walkthrough covers each rule with a worked example, a common mistake to watch for, and a short check for understanding. Start with the starred rules, and try each problem on paper before you reveal the solution.

SAT-Style Practice: Choose Your Approach

Ten SAT-style questions in the two digital-test formats: four-choice multiple choice and student-produced response (type the number). They move from easier questions through medium to medium-hard at the end, and six include a figure. A ★ starred rule chip means the question uses one of the starred rules from tab 1. Before you compute, identify the question type, then mark what you know on the figure, or sketch one if none is given. Desmos is available throughout the SAT Math section, so it is available here: the button in the corner opens it. For these questions it works mostly as a calculator, for example to square, add and take a square root in one line. When the choices contain radicals such as $7\sqrt{3}$, an exact answer from the side ratios is usually quicker than a decimal.
