Special right triangle rules

Two right triangles have fixed side ratios: the $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle and the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle. Once you know one side of either triangle, the pattern gives you the other two. This sheet has two goals. The first is to know both patterns from memory, including which side is across from which angle. The second is to notice these triangles when a question does not name them: inside a square, a circle or an equilateral triangle. As you practice, start by identifying which special triangle the figure contains. Then decide which side you were given, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1The $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle

A right triangle with two $45^\circ$ angles has two equal legs, so it is an isosceles right triangle. It is also half of a square, cut along the diagonal. If each leg is $x$, the hypotenuse is $x\sqrt{2}$.

the pattern
$x : x : x\sqrt{2}$   (leg, leg, hypotenuse)
leg to hypotenuse
multiply by $\sqrt{2}$
hypotenuse to leg
divide by $\sqrt{2}$
why it works
$x^2 + x^2 = 2x^2$, and $\sqrt{2x^2} = x\sqrt{2}$
the order of the sides
$\sqrt{2}$ is about $1.41$, which is between $1$ and $2$, so $x < x\sqrt{2} < 2x$. The hypotenuse is longer than a leg, but it is not double a leg
how to remember it
Small to tall, root them all. List the angles from smallest to largest: $45^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $90^\circ$. Across from them, the sides are $x$ times $\sqrt{1}$, $\sqrt{1}$, $\sqrt{2}$. Since $\sqrt{1} = 1$, that is $x$, $x$, $x\sqrt{2}$
Example AEach leg is $8$: the hypotenuse is $8\sqrt{2}$.
Example BThe hypotenuse is $9\sqrt{2}$: each leg is $9$.
Example CThe hypotenuse is $20$: each leg is $\dfrac{20}{\sqrt{2}} = 10\sqrt{2}$. Rule 3 shows that division one step at a time.
The hypotenuse is across from the right angle, and it is the longest side. If a leg comes out longer than the hypotenuse, check which side you gave the $\sqrt{2}$.
★Rule 2The $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle

A right triangle with a $30^\circ$ angle and a $60^\circ$ angle has three different side lengths. It is half of an equilateral triangle, cut along the height. Call the shortest side $x$. The hypotenuse is $2x$, and the longer leg is $x\sqrt{3}$.

AngleSide across from itLength
$30^\circ$  (the smallest angle)short leg$x$
$60^\circ$long leg$x\sqrt{3}$
$90^\circ$  (the largest angle)hypotenuse$2x$
the pattern
$x : x\sqrt{3} : 2x$   (short leg, long leg, hypotenuse)
sides follow angles
the smallest angle is across from the shortest side, and the largest angle is across from the longest side
the order of the sides
$\sqrt{3}$ is about $1.73$, which is between $1$ and $2$, so $x < x\sqrt{3} < 2x$
why it works
$(2x)^2 - x^2 = 3x^2$, and $\sqrt{3x^2} = x\sqrt{3}$
how to remember it
Small to tall, root them all. List the angles from smallest to largest: $30^\circ$, $60^\circ$, $90^\circ$. Across from them, the sides are $x$ times $\sqrt{1}$, $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{4}$. Since $\sqrt{4} = 2$, that is $x$, $x\sqrt{3}$, $2x$
Example AThe short leg is $3$: the long leg is $3\sqrt{3}$ and the hypotenuse is $6$.
Example BThe hypotenuse is $50$: the short leg is $25$ and the long leg is $25\sqrt{3}$.
Example CA right triangle has a $60^\circ$ angle, and the leg that touches that angle has length $13$. The third angle is $30^\circ$, and this leg is across from it, so it is the short leg. The other leg is $13\sqrt{3}$ and the hypotenuse is $26$.
Before you write $x$ on a side, find the $30^\circ$ angle. If the figure marks only the $60^\circ$ angle, the third angle is $30^\circ$. The side across from the $30^\circ$ angle is $x$.
★Memory aidSmall to tall, root them all

One rhyme holds both patterns. List the angles from smallest to largest, write a square root across from each one, then simplify. The numbers under the roots are $1$, $1$, $2$ for the $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle and $1$, $3$, $4$ for the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle.

45-45-90small to tall
Angles$45^\circ$$45^\circ$$90^\circ$
Root them all$\sqrt{1}$$\sqrt{1}$$\sqrt{2}$
Simplify, times $x$$x$$x$$x\sqrt{2}$
30-60-90small to tall
Angles$30^\circ$$60^\circ$$90^\circ$
Root them all$\sqrt{1}$$\sqrt{3}$$\sqrt{4}$
Simplify, times $x$$x$$x\sqrt{3}$$2x$
say it
“one, one, two” for the $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle, and “one, three, four” for the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle
recall the numbers
both lists start at $1$. Two equal angles repeat the $1$, and three different angles skip to $3$. The last number is the first two added: $1 + 1 = 2$ and $1 + 3 = 4$
why adding works
squaring a square root gives the number under it, so $1 + 1 = 2$ and $1 + 3 = 4$ are the Pythagorean theorem for these two triangles. If your three numbers do not add up, check the pattern
where each side goes
each side is across from the angle above it. The smallest angle is across from the shortest side, however the triangle is turned
The roots belong to the pattern, not to the lengths in the question. If the hypotenuse of a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle is $10$, it stays $10$. It matches $2x$, so $x = 5$. Rule 3 shows how to find $x$ from any side.
★Rule 3Find $x$ first

Each side of a special right triangle is $x$ times a fixed number. Write the pattern on the figure, set the side you know equal to its piece of the pattern, and solve for $x$. Then build the side the question asks for.

TriangleYou are givenWriteThen
$45$-$45$-$90$a leg$x = \text{leg}$hypotenuse $= x\sqrt{2}$
$45$-$45$-$90$the hypotenuse$x\sqrt{2} = \text{hypotenuse}$divide by $\sqrt{2}$
$30$-$60$-$90$the short leg$x = \text{short leg}$long leg $= x\sqrt{3}$, hypotenuse $= 2x$
$30$-$60$-$90$the hypotenuse$2x = \text{hypotenuse}$divide by $2$
$30$-$60$-$90$the long leg$x\sqrt{3} = \text{long leg}$divide by $\sqrt{3}$
dividing by a root
multiply the top and the bottom by the root: $\dfrac{24}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{24\sqrt{3}}{3} = 8\sqrt{3}$   and   $\dfrac{20}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{20\sqrt{2}}{2} = 10\sqrt{2}$
in words
dividing by $\sqrt{2}$ gives half of the number, times $\sqrt{2}$. Dividing by $\sqrt{3}$ gives a third of the number, times $\sqrt{3}$
squaring a root
$(5\sqrt{2})^2 = 25 \cdot 2 = 50$   and   $(4\sqrt{3})^2 = 16 \cdot 3 = 48$
size check
moving to a longer side makes the number larger, and moving to a shorter side makes it smaller
Example AA $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with long leg $24$: $x\sqrt{3} = 24$, so $x = 8\sqrt{3}$. The hypotenuse is $2x = 16\sqrt{3}$.
Example BA $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $30$: $x\sqrt{2} = 30$, so each leg is $15\sqrt{2}$.
Example CA $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with long leg $5$: $x = \dfrac{5}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}$. The numbers do not have to come out as whole numbers for the pattern to work.
Example DA $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle with hypotenuse $6$: each leg is $3\sqrt{2}$, and the area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(3\sqrt{2})(3\sqrt{2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}(18) = 9$.
Get $x$ by itself before you do anything else. To undo a multiplication by $\sqrt{3}$, divide by $\sqrt{3}$. Desmos can confirm a simplified radical: $24/\sqrt{3}$ and $8\sqrt{3}$ both show about $13.86$.
★Rule 4Squares and circles: where the $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle appears

The diagonal of a square cuts it into two $45$-$45$-$90$ triangles, so the side and the diagonal of a square follow the pattern $s : s\sqrt{2}$. Circles bring in the same triangle when a square is drawn inside a circle or around one. A rectangle that is not a square is different: its diagonal does not make $45^\circ$ angles.

square with side $s$
diagonal $= s\sqrt{2}$
square with diagonal $d$
side $= \dfrac{d}{\sqrt{2}}$,   area $= \dfrac{d^2}{2}$
square inscribed in a circle
the corners of the square are on the circle, so the diagonal of the square is a diameter of the circle
circle inscribed in a square
the circle touches all four sides, so the side of the square equals the diameter of the circle
isosceles right triangle
a right triangle with two equal legs is a $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle, whether or not the angles are marked
Example AA square with side $15$ has a diagonal of $15\sqrt{2}$.
Example BA square with diagonal $22$ has area $\dfrac{22^2}{2} = 242$. Each side is $11\sqrt{2}$, and $(11\sqrt{2})^2 = 242$ agrees.
Example CA square is inscribed in a circle of radius $3$. The diagonal of the square is the diameter, $6$. Each side is $\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{2}} = 3\sqrt{2}$, and the area is $18$.
Example DA circle is inscribed in a square whose diagonal is $8\sqrt{2}$. The side of the square is $8$, so the diameter of the circle is $8$ and the radius is $4$.
If the diagonal of the square is not drawn, draw it. Then decide what matches the diameter of the circle: the diagonal when the square is inside the circle, the side when the circle is inside the square.
★Rule 5Equilateral triangles: where the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle appears

An equilateral triangle has three equal sides and three $60^\circ$ angles. Its height cuts it into two $30$-$60$-$90$ triangles. In each one, the hypotenuse is a side of the equilateral triangle, the short leg is half of the base, and the long leg is the height. The height and area formulas come from that picture, so you can rebuild them whenever you need them.

short leg
half of the base: $\dfrac{s}{2}$
height (the long leg)
$\dfrac{s}{2} \cdot \sqrt{3} = \dfrac{s\sqrt{3}}{2}$
area
$\dfrac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$ $= \dfrac{1}{2} \cdot s \cdot \dfrac{s\sqrt{3}}{2}$ $= \dfrac{s^2\sqrt{3}}{4}$
from the area to the side
set $\dfrac{s^2\sqrt{3}}{4}$ equal to the area, divide both sides by $\sqrt{3}$, and solve for $s$
from the height to the side
the height is $x\sqrt{3}$: solve for $x$, then double it
Example ASide $6$: the short leg is $3$, the height is $3\sqrt{3}$, and the area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(6)(3\sqrt{3}) = 9\sqrt{3}$. The formula agrees: $\dfrac{6^2\sqrt{3}}{4} = 9\sqrt{3}$.
Example BArea $64\sqrt{3}$: $\dfrac{s^2\sqrt{3}}{4} = 64\sqrt{3}$, so $\dfrac{s^2}{4} = 64$. Then $s^2 = 256$ and $s = 16$.
Example CHeight $9\sqrt{3}$: the short leg is $9$, so each side is $18$.
Example DHeight $6$: $x\sqrt{3} = 6$, so $x = 2\sqrt{3}$ and each side is $4\sqrt{3}$. The area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(4\sqrt{3})(6) = 12\sqrt{3}$.
The Bluebook reference sheet shows both special right triangles, but it does not list the height or the area of an equilateral triangle. If you forget $\dfrac{s^2\sqrt{3}}{4}$, draw the height and use $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \text{base} \cdot \text{height}$. The Bluebook Tools tab of SAT Desmos 1: Core Moves has a copy of the reference sheet.
Rule 6More figures built from these triangles

A few other figures contain the same two triangles. In each one, add a height, a radius or a diagonal, and a $30$-$60$-$90$ or $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle appears.

Example AA regular hexagon with side $2$: each of the six triangles has area $\dfrac{2^2\sqrt{3}}{4} = \sqrt{3}$, so the hexagon has area $6\sqrt{3}$.
Example BAn isosceles triangle has two sides of length $8$ with a $120^\circ$ angle between them. The height from that angle is the short leg of each $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle: $4$. Each long leg is $4\sqrt{3}$, so the third side is $8\sqrt{3}$ and the area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(8\sqrt{3})(4) = 16\sqrt{3}$.
Example CIn a circle of radius $9$, two radii that are $60^\circ$ apart have a chord of $9$ between their endpoints. Two radii that are $90^\circ$ apart have a chord of $9\sqrt{2}$.
These figures come up less often than squares and equilateral triangles. The approach is the same: find the $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ or $60^\circ$ angle, add the segment that makes a right angle, and use the pattern.
Rule 7Sine, cosine and tangent of $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$

If you have studied right triangle trigonometry, the two patterns also give exact values of sine, cosine and tangent at $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $60^\circ$. Sketch the triangle with $x = 1$, find the sides opposite and adjacent to the angle you are using, and read the ratio. The size of the triangle does not matter, because $x$ cancels.

Anglesinecosinetangent
$30^\circ$$\dfrac{1}{2}$$\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$
$45^\circ$$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$1$
$60^\circ$$\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$\dfrac{1}{2}$$\sqrt{3}$
the three ratios
$\sin = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$,   $\cos = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$,   $\tan = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$
a pattern to notice
$\sin 30^\circ = \cos 60^\circ$ and $\sin 60^\circ = \cos 30^\circ$, because the two angles belong to the same triangle
small to tall again
for $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, $60^\circ$, sine is $\dfrac{\sqrt{1}}{2}$, $\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$. Cosine is the same list in reverse
in radians
$30^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{6}$,   $45^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$,   $60^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$
Example AIn a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle with sides $1$, $\sqrt{3}$ and $2$, the side opposite the $30^\circ$ angle is $1$ and the hypotenuse is $2$, so $\sin 30^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
Example BA right triangle has a hypotenuse of $10$ and a $30^\circ$ angle. The side opposite that angle is $10 \cdot \sin 30^\circ = 5$. That is the short leg, half of the hypotenuse, as the pattern says.
There is no separate list to memorize here. Sketch the triangle, label it $1$, $\sqrt{3}$, $2$ or $1$, $1$, $\sqrt{2}$, and read the ratio.
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common setup and interpretation mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1putting $x\sqrt{3}$ on the hypotenuse
In a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle the hypotenuse is $2x$. Since $\sqrt{3}$ is about $1.73$, the side $x\sqrt{3}$ is shorter than $2x$, so it is the longer leg.
Common mistake 2doubling a leg of a $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle
The hypotenuse of a $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle is $x\sqrt{2}$, which is about $1.41x$. The side that doubles belongs to the $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle.
Common mistake 3calling the given side $x$ without checking
The given side might be the long leg or the hypotenuse. Find the $30^\circ$ angle first: the side across from it is $x$. Then decide whether the given length is $x$, $x\sqrt{3}$ or $2x$.
Common mistake 4multiplying by the root when the side should get shorter
With a long leg of $24$, the short leg is $\dfrac{24}{\sqrt{3}} = 8\sqrt{3}$, which is about $13.9$. Multiplying gives $24\sqrt{3}$, about $41.6$, which is longer than the long leg. Compare sizes after each step.
Common mistake 5using half of the base as the height of an equilateral triangle
With a side of $30$, half of the base is $15$, but the height is the long leg, $15\sqrt{3}$. The area is $\tfrac{1}{2}(30)(15\sqrt{3}) = 225\sqrt{3}$, not $\tfrac{1}{2}(30)(15)$.
Common mistake 6treating a rectangle like a square
A square is the only rectangle whose diagonal makes $45^\circ$ angles with its sides. For another rectangle, use the Pythagorean theorem, unless the question gives a $30^\circ$ or $60^\circ$ angle.
Common mistake 7using the diameter as the side of an inscribed square
When a square is inscribed in a circle, the diameter is the diagonal of the square. The side is the diameter divided by $\sqrt{2}$.
Common mistake 8stopping at $x$
After you find $x$, reread the question. It may ask for the hypotenuse, a perimeter, an area or a radius.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: one side and one pattern give you the whole triangle. A $45$-$45$-$90$ triangle is $x$, $x$, $x\sqrt{2}$. A $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle is $x$, $x\sqrt{3}$, $2x$. Each question asks you to notice which triangle the figure contains, decide which side you were given, and build the side you need.

The walkthrough covers each rule with a worked example, a common mistake to watch for, and a short check for understanding. Start with the starred rules, and try each problem on paper before you reveal the solution.

SAT-Style Practice: Choose Your Approach

Ten SAT-style questions in the two digital-test formats: four-choice multiple choice and student-produced response (type the number). They move from easier questions through medium to medium-hard at the end, and six include a figure. A ★ starred rule chip means the question uses one of the starred rules from tab 1. Before you compute, identify the question type: which special triangle does the figure contain, and which of its sides were you given? Sketch a figure if none is provided. Desmos is available throughout the SAT Math section, so it is available here: the button in the corner opens it. For these questions it works mostly as a calculator, for example to compare $\dfrac{24}{\sqrt{3}}$ with $8\sqrt{3}$. When the choices contain radicals, an exact answer from the side pattern is usually quicker than a decimal.
