Similar figure rules

Two figures are similar when one is a scaled copy of the other: the same shape at a different size. Matching angles are equal, and every pair of matching lengths has the same ratio, called the scale factor. This sheet has two goals. The first is to match the parts correctly and set up the proportion. The second is to know what the scale factor does to lengths, areas and volumes. As you practice, start by identifying which figures are similar and which parts match. Then decide whether the question is about a length, an area or a volume, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1Matching sides and the scale factor

Similar figures have equal matching angles and proportional matching sides. The similarity statement tells you which parts match: in $\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$, vertex $A$ matches $D$, $B$ matches $E$, and $C$ matches $F$. Divide a length in one figure by the matching length in the other to get the scale factor $k$.

the proportion
$\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{BC}{EF} = \dfrac{AC}{DF}$
scale factor
$k = \dfrac{\text{a length in the second figure}}{\text{the matching length in the first figure}}$
a missing side
multiply its matching side by $k$, or solve the proportion
two angles are enough
if two angles of one triangle equal two angles of another, the triangles are similar
Example AIn the figure, $\overline{AB}$ matches $\overline{DE}$, so $k = \dfrac{12}{8} = 1.5$. The other sides follow: $EF = 1.5 \cdot 10 = 15$ and $DF = 1.5 \cdot 12 = 18$.
Example BA triangle has sides of $9$, $12$ and $18$. A similar triangle has a shortest side of $6$. The scale factor is $\dfrac{6}{9} = \dfrac{2}{3}$, so its other sides are $8$ and $12$.
Match sides by the order of the letters, or by the angle each side is across from, not by where they sit on the page. One of the figures may be turned or flipped.
★Rule 2Parallel lines make similar triangles

Parallel lines create equal angles, and two pairs of equal angles make two triangles similar. Two pictures come up most often. In the nested picture, a segment parallel to one side cuts off a smaller triangle that shares an angle with the whole triangle. In the bowtie picture, two segments cross between parallel lines.

nested
if $\overline{DE} \parallel \overline{BC}$, then $\triangle ADE \sim \triangle ABC$ and $\dfrac{AD}{AB} = \dfrac{AE}{AC} = \dfrac{DE}{BC}$
use the whole side
the side of the large triangle is $AB = AD + DB$, not $DB$
bowtie
if $\overline{PQ} \parallel \overline{RS}$ and the segments cross at $M$, then $\triangle PQM \sim \triangle SRM$ and $\dfrac{PQ}{SR} = \dfrac{PM}{SM} = \dfrac{QM}{RM}$
which vertices match
$P$ matches $S$, and $Q$ matches $R$: matching vertices sit at opposite ends of each crossing segment
right triangles
two right triangles that share an acute angle are similar
Example AIn $\triangle ABC$, $\overline{DE} \parallel \overline{BC}$, $AD = 6$, $DB = 3$ and $BC = 12$. The whole side is $AB = 9$, so $\dfrac{DE}{12} = \dfrac{6}{9}$ and $DE = 8$.
Example BSegments $\overline{PS}$ and $\overline{QR}$ cross at $M$, with $\overline{PQ} \parallel \overline{RS}$. If $PQ = 4$, $RS = 10$ and $PM = 6$, then $\dfrac{SM}{6} = \dfrac{10}{4}$ and $SM = 15$.
The equal angles come from the parallel lines: corresponding angles in the nested picture, alternate interior angles and vertical angles in the bowtie. For a review of those angle facts, see SAT Math 4: Triangles.
★Rule 3Every length scales by $k$

The scale factor applies to every length in the figure, not only to the sides. Heights, diagonals, radii and perimeters all grow or shrink by the same factor $k$. Angles do not change.

perimeters
$\dfrac{\text{perimeter of the second figure}}{\text{perimeter of the first figure}} = k$
heights and diagonals
matching heights, medians and diagonals have the ratio $k$
circles
any two circles are similar: radius, diameter and circumference share one ratio
angles
matching angles stay equal: they are not multiplied by $k$
Example ATwo similar triangles have a pair of matching sides of $6$ and $10$, so $k = \dfrac{10}{6} = \dfrac{5}{3}$. If the smaller triangle has a perimeter of $24$, the larger has a perimeter of $24 \cdot \dfrac{5}{3} = 40$.
Example BCircles with radii of $4$ and $10$ have circumferences of $8\pi$ and $20\pi$. Both ratios reduce to $2 : 5$.
Example CA $5$ by $12$ rectangle has a diagonal of $13$. A similar rectangle with a diagonal of $39$ has $k = 3$, so its sides are $15$ and $36$.
A ratio of any two matching lengths is the scale factor. If the perimeters are in the ratio $3 : 4$, each pair of matching sides is in the ratio $3 : 4$ too.
★Rule 4Areas scale by $k^2$

An area is built from two lengths multiplied together, and each one is multiplied by $k$. So areas are multiplied by $k^2$. If matching lengths are in the ratio $a : b$, the areas are in the ratio $a^2 : b^2$.

the rule
$\dfrac{\text{area of the second figure}}{\text{area of the first figure}} = k^2$
as a ratio
lengths $a : b$  give  areas $a^2 : b^2$
why it works
for a triangle, $\tfrac{1}{2}(kb)(kh) = k^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}bh$
going back
take a square root: areas in the ratio $16 : 25$ mean lengths in the ratio $4 : 5$
circles
the ratio of the areas is the square of the ratio of the radii
Example ATwo similar triangles have matching sides in the ratio $2 : 5$. If the smaller has an area of $12$, the larger has an area of $12 \cdot \dfrac{25}{4} = 75$.
Example BThe circumferences of two circles are in the ratio $3 : 4$, so their areas are in the ratio $9 : 16$. If the smaller circle has an area of $54$, the larger has an area of $54 \cdot \dfrac{16}{9} = 96$.
Example CTwo similar pentagons have areas of $49$ and $100$, so their matching sides are in the ratio $7 : 10$. If a side of the smaller pentagon is $21$, the matching side of the larger is $30$.
Decide what kind of quantity each number is before you set up a ratio. A ratio of sides, perimeters or radii is $k$. A ratio of areas is $k^2$.
Rule 5Regions between similar figures

When one figure sits inside a similar one, the region between them is the larger area minus the smaller area. Write each area as a number of equal parts using the squared ratio, subtract the parts, and then find the size of one part.

a ring
radii $a : b$  give  circle areas of $a^2$ and $b^2$ parts, so the ring is $b^2 - a^2$ parts
a slice of a triangle
matching sides $a : b$  give  triangle areas of $a^2$ and $b^2$ parts, so the slice is $b^2 - a^2$ parts
one part
divide a known area by its number of parts
ratios along one side
measure both triangles from the vertex they share before you square
tangent circles
when circles sit in a row along a diameter of a larger circle, their diameters add up to the large diameter
Example ATwo circles have the same center, and the radius of the larger is $\dfrac{4}{3}$ times the radius of the smaller. The radii are in the ratio $3 : 4$, so the areas are $9$ parts and $16$ parts, and the ring is $7$ parts. If the smaller circle has an area of $18$, one part is $2$ and the ring has an area of $14$.
Example BIn $\triangle ABC$, $\overline{DE} \parallel \overline{BC}$ and $AD : DB = 1 : 2$. Measured from $A$, the matching sides are $AD : AB = 1 : 3$, so the areas are $1$ part and $9$ parts. Quadrilateral $DBCE$ is $8$ parts. If its area is $88$, one part is $11$ and $\triangle ABC$ has an area of $99$.
Square first, then subtract. For radii in the ratio $3 : 4$, the ring is $16 - 9 = 7$ parts, not $(4 - 3)^2 = 1$ part.
Rule 6Volumes scale by $k^3$

A volume is built from three lengths, so volumes are multiplied by $k^3$. A surface area is still an area, so it is multiplied by $k^2$.

QuantityMultiplied byWith $k = 3$
lengths (edges, heights, radii)$k$$3$
areas (faces, surface area)$k^2$$9$
volumes$k^3$$27$
as a ratio
lengths $a : b$  give  volumes $a^3 : b^3$
going back
take a cube root: volumes in the ratio $8 : 125$ mean lengths in the ratio $2 : 5$
Example ATwo similar cylinders have heights of $4$ and $6$, so $k = \dfrac{3}{2}$ and $k^3 = \dfrac{27}{8}$. If the smaller holds $40$ cubic inches, the larger holds $40 \cdot \dfrac{27}{8} = 135$ cubic inches.
Example BTwo similar cones have volumes in the ratio $8 : 125$. Their heights are in the ratio $2 : 5$, and their surface areas are in the ratio $4 : 25$.
A scale model works the same way. If each length of the model is $\dfrac{1}{10}$ of the original, its volume is $\dfrac{1}{1{,}000}$ of the original.
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common setup and interpretation mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1matching sides by where they sit on the page
A side matches the side across from the equal angle, which is not necessarily the side in the same position on the page. Use the order of the letters in the similarity statement, or mark the equal angles first.
Common mistake 2using a piece of a side where the whole side belongs
In the nested picture with $AD = 6$ and $DB = 3$, the triangles compare $AD$ with $AB = 9$. The ratio $\dfrac{6}{3}$ compares two pieces of one side, and it is not the scale factor.
Common mistake 3adding where you should multiply
If matching sides are $8$ and $12$, a side of $10$ does not match $10 + 4 = 14$. Similar figures keep ratios equal, so it matches $10 \cdot 1.5 = 15$.
Common mistake 4flipping the ratio
Decide first whether the answer should be larger or smaller than the number you start with. If the result moves the other way, the scale factor is upside down.
Common mistake 5scaling an area by $k$
If each side of a figure is doubled, an area of $10$ does not become $20$. Areas use the squared ratio: $10 \cdot 2^2 = 40$.
Common mistake 6using an area ratio on a length
If two areas are in the ratio $16 : 25$, the perimeters are not in that ratio. Take the square root first: lengths are in the ratio $4 : 5$.
Common mistake 7subtracting before squaring
For a ring whose radii are in the ratio $2 : 5$, the areas are $4$ parts and $25$ parts, so the ring is $21$ parts. Computing $(5 - 2)^2 = 9$ gives a different number.
Common mistake 8answering for a different figure
After you find one part or one area, reread the question. It may ask for the larger figure, the smaller figure, or the region between them.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: one scale factor runs the whole figure. Each length is multiplied by $k$, each area by $k^2$, and each volume by $k^3$. Each question asks you to find the similar figures, match their parts, and decide which power of $k$ applies.
