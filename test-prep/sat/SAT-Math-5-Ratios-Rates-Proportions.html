Ratio, rate and proportion rules

A ratio compares two quantities. A rate is a ratio of two different units, such as miles per hour. A proportion says that two ratios are equal. Most questions on this sheet come down to one habit: write the comparison as a fraction, label what is on top and what is on the bottom, and keep that order the same on both sides. As you practice, start by identifying the question type. Then decide whether a proportion or an equation fits it better, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1Ratios: part to part and part to whole

A ratio of $4$ to $7$ can be written $4 : 7$ or $\dfrac{4}{7}$. Before you use a ratio, decide what it compares: one part to another part, or one part to the whole. The same mix gives different fractions depending on which comparison you need.

part to part
$a : b$   (for example, red tiles to blue tiles)
part to whole
$\dfrac{a}{a + b}$ and $\dfrac{b}{a + b}$   (the whole is $a + b$ parts)
a ratio as multiples
if $x : y = 4 : 7$, then $x = 4k$ and $y = 7k$ for the same number $k$
three parts
$a : b : c$ works the same way: the whole is $a + b + c$ parts
Example ARed and blue tiles are in the ratio $4 : 7$. The red tiles are $\dfrac{4}{11}$ of the tiles, not $\dfrac{4}{7}$. If there are $55$ tiles, each part is $55 \div 11 = 5$ tiles, so there are $20$ red tiles and $35$ blue tiles.
Example BIf $\dfrac{x}{y} = \dfrac{3}{4}$ and $3x + 2y = 85$, write $x = 3k$ and $y = 4k$. Then $9k + 8k = 85$, so $k = 5$, $x = 15$ and $y = 20$.
A ratio alone does not give the amounts. $4 : 7$ could be $4$ and $7$, $8$ and $14$, or $20$ and $35$. One more fact (a total, a difference, or one of the amounts) gives you $k$.
★Rule 2Proportions and unit rates

A proportion says two ratios are equal. Set one up when a question says “at this rate” or “at the same rate.” Label the units, keep the same quantity on top on both sides, and solve.

the setup
$\dfrac{\text{pages}}{\text{minutes}} = \dfrac{\text{pages}}{\text{minutes}}$   (the same units in the same places)
cross-multiply
$\dfrac{a}{b} = \dfrac{c}{d}$ means $ad = bc$
unit rate
divide to find the amount for one unit, then multiply by the number of units you need
scale it
if one quantity is multiplied by $n$, the other is multiplied by $n$ too
Example AA printer prints $45$ pages in $6$ minutes. At this rate, how many pages does it print in $16$ minutes? Proportion: $\dfrac{45}{6} = \dfrac{p}{16}$, so $6p = 720$ and $p = 120$. Unit rate: $45 \div 6 = 7.5$ pages per minute, and $7.5 \times 16 = 120$.
Example BA recipe uses $2.5$ cups of flour for $20$ muffins. For $52$ muffins: $\dfrac{2.5}{20} = \dfrac{f}{52}$, so $f = \dfrac{2.5 \times 52}{20} = 6.5$ cups.
Check the units before you solve. If one time is in minutes and the other is in hours, convert one of them first. Rule 4 shows how.
★Rule 3Direct variation: $y = kx$

When $y$ is directly proportional to $x$, the ratio $\dfrac{y}{x}$ is the same for every pair of values. That constant ratio is $k$, and the equation is $y = kx$. A question may say “varies directly with,” “is directly proportional to,” or “is proportional to.” All three describe the same relationship.

$y = 1.5x$: proportional
$y = x + 3$: linear, not proportional
$y = 0.5x^2$: proportional to $x^2$
Where you see itWhat to look for
Equation$y = kx$, with nothing added, and $k = \dfrac{y}{x}$
Table$\dfrac{y}{x}$ is the same for every pair: $\dfrac{3}{2} = \dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{9}{6} = 1.5$
Grapha line through the origin with slope $k$
Two pairs$\dfrac{y_1}{x_1} = \dfrac{y_2}{x_2}$, which is a proportion
Proportional to $x^2$$y = kx^2$: a parabola with its vertex at the origin. Doubling $x$ multiplies $y$ by $4$
Example A$y$ is directly proportional to $x$, and $y = 33$ when $x = 6$. Then $k = \dfrac{33}{6} = 5.5$, so $y = 5.5x$. When $x = 10$, $y = 55$.
Example B$y$ is directly proportional to $x^2$, and $y = 12$ when $x = 2$. Then $12 = k(2)^2$, so $k = 3$. When $x = 5$, $y = 3(25) = 75$.
A line such as $y = x + 3$ is linear but not proportional, because its graph misses the origin. In $y = kx$, the constant $k$ is the slope of the line. SAT Math 1: Linear Equations covers slope in detail.
★Rule 4Rates and average speed

Distance, rate and time are tied together by one equation. For a trip with more than one part, the average speed is the total distance divided by the total time. It equals the plain average of the speeds only when the same amount of time is spent at each speed.

the equation
$d = rt$, so $r = \dfrac{d}{t}$ and $t = \dfrac{d}{r}$
average speed
$\dfrac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$
same distance, two speeds
find each time with $t = \dfrac{d}{r}$, then add. If no distance is given, choose one that both speeds divide evenly
unit conversion
multiply by a fraction equal to $1$ so that the unit you do not want cancels: $\dfrac{60 \text{ min}}{1 \text{ hr}}$ or $\dfrac{1 \text{ hr}}{60 \text{ min}}$
Example AA driver travels for $2$ hours at $35$ miles per hour and then for $3$ hours at $55$ miles per hour. Total distance: $70 + 165 = 235$ miles. Total time: $5$ hours. Average speed: $\dfrac{235}{5} = 47$ miles per hour.
Example BA driver goes to a lake at $36$ miles per hour and returns on the same road, in heavy traffic, at $18$ miles per hour. Choose a distance of $36$ miles each way: the times are $1$ hour and $2$ hours. Average speed: $\dfrac{72}{3} = 24$ miles per hour, not $27$.
Example CA drone flies at $15$ meters per second. In kilometers per hour: $\dfrac{15 \text{ m}}{1 \text{ s}} \times \dfrac{3600 \text{ s}}{1 \text{ hr}} \times \dfrac{1 \text{ km}}{1000 \text{ m}} = 54$ kilometers per hour.
When the distances are equal, more time is spent at the slower speed, so the average speed is closer to the slower speed. In Example B, $24$ is closer to $18$ than to $36$.
Rule 5Inverse variation: $xy = k$

When two quantities are inversely proportional, their product stays the same. If one is multiplied by $3$, the other is divided by $3$. Common pairs: workers and days for one job, speed and time for one trip, and people and cost per person for one bill.

Direct variationInverse variation
Equation$y = kx$$xy = k$, or $y = \dfrac{k}{x}$
What stays the samethe ratio $\dfrac{y}{x}$the product $xy$
Two pairs$\dfrac{y_1}{x_1} = \dfrac{y_2}{x_2}$$x_1 y_1 = x_2 y_2$

Example A$6$ painters can paint a building in $10$ days. The job is $6 \times 10 = 60$ painter-days. With $15$ painters working at the same rate, it takes $60 \div 15 = 4$ days.
Example B$y$ is inversely proportional to $x$, and $y = 20$ when $x = 3$. The product is $k = 60$. When $x = 5$, $y = 12$. When $x = 12$, $y = 5$.
Check the direction with common sense. More workers should mean fewer days, and a faster speed should mean less time. If your answer moved the other way, the setup was a direct proportion.
Rule 6Combined work: add the rates

When two people or machines work on the same job, their times do not add. Their rates do. Write each rate as the fraction of the job finished in one hour.

WorkerTime aloneRate (jobs per hour)
First$a$ hours$\dfrac{1}{a}$
Second$b$ hours$\dfrac{1}{b}$
Together$t$ hours$\dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b} = \dfrac{1}{t}$
work rate
$\dfrac{1}{\text{time alone}}$ of the job per hour
two workers together
$t = \dfrac{ab}{a + b}$, which comes from $\dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b} = \dfrac{1}{t}$
equal workers in a group
count worker-hours: $\text{workers} \times \text{hours}$ measures the size of the job
Example AOne pump fills a tank in $6$ hours, and a second pump fills it in $12$ hours. Together they fill $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{12} = \dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}$ of the tank per hour, so they need $4$ hours.
Example BTwo machines finish an order together in $6$ hours. The first machine alone needs $10$ hours. The rate of the second machine is $\dfrac{1}{6} - \dfrac{1}{10} = \dfrac{1}{15}$, so alone it needs $15$ hours.
Example C$6$ workers take $10$ hours to tile $4$ identical floors. That is $60$ worker-hours for $4$ floors, or $15$ worker-hours per floor. For $12$ floors, $8$ workers need $\dfrac{12 \times 15}{8} = 22.5$ hours.
Working together takes less time than either worker needs alone. If your answer is larger than one of the solo times, check the setup.
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common setup and interpretation mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1using a part-to-part ratio as a fraction of the whole
A ratio of $4 : 7$ means $4$ parts and $7$ parts, so the whole is $11$ parts. The first part is $\dfrac{4}{11}$ of the whole, not $\dfrac{4}{7}$.
Common mistake 2a proportion with the units in different places
If the left side is $\dfrac{\text{pages}}{\text{minutes}}$, the right side has to be $\dfrac{\text{pages}}{\text{minutes}}$ too. Write the units next to the numbers before you cross-multiply, and convert first if one side is in minutes and the other is in hours.
Common mistake 3adding the same amount instead of multiplying
If $y$ is proportional to $x$ and $x$ goes from $4$ to $14$, then $y$ does not go up by $10$. It is multiplied by the same factor as $x$, here $\dfrac{14}{4} = 3.5$.
Common mistake 4treating a line that misses the origin as proportional
$y = x + 3$ is a line, but $\dfrac{y}{x}$ changes from point to point. A proportional relationship has the form $y = kx$, and its graph passes through the origin.
Common mistake 5averaging the two speeds
For equal distances at $36$ and $18$ miles per hour, the average speed is $24$, not $27$. Use total distance divided by total time.
Common mistake 6using a direct proportion for an inverse relationship
If $6$ painters need $10$ days, then $15$ painters need fewer days, not more. When more of one quantity means less of the other, keep the product constant: $6 \times 10 = 15 \times d$.
Common mistake 7adding or averaging the times of two workers
Pumps that need $6$ hours and $12$ hours alone do not need $18$ hours together, or $9$. Add the rates: $\dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}$, so together they need $4$ hours.
Common mistake 8stopping one step early
After you find $k$, a unit rate, or the size of one part, reread the question. It may ask for the other part, the total, or the value of $y$ at a new $x$.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: a ratio is a comparison you can scale. Multiply or divide both quantities by the same number, and the relationship stays the same. Each question asks you to decide what is being compared, set up the comparison with matching units, and find the missing value.

The walkthrough covers each rule with a worked example, a common mistake to watch for, and a short check for understanding. Start with the starred rules, and try each problem on paper before you reveal the solution.

SAT-Style Practice: Choose Your Approach

Ten SAT-style questions in the two digital-test formats: four-choice multiple choice and student-produced response (type the number). They move from easier questions through medium to medium-hard at the end, and three include a table or a figure. A ★ starred rule chip means the question uses one of the starred rules from tab 1. Before you compute, identify the question type: does a ratio stay the same, does a product stay the same, or do rates add? Then write the units next to the numbers as you set up. Desmos is available throughout the SAT Math section, so it is available here: the button in the corner opens it. For these questions it works mostly as a calculator. It can also graph an equation in one variable, such as $\dfrac{x}{20} + \dfrac{x}{12} = 4$, so that you can read the solution from the graph. For a one-step proportion, cross-multiplying by hand is usually quicker.
