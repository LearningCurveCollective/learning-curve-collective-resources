Exponent rules

An exponent counts repeated multiplication. That idea is behind two kinds of SAT questions. In the first kind, you rewrite an expression: combine powers, move a negative exponent, or turn a root into a fractional exponent. In the second kind, a quantity is multiplied by the same factor again and again, and you build or read the model that describes it. Rules 1 to 4 cover the expressions. Rules 5 and 6 cover the models, and Rule 7 adds two model questions that come up less often. Different forms reveal different information, so notice what you have been given before you start the algebra. As you practice, start by identifying the question type. Then decide whether Desmos or algebra makes more sense, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1The exponent laws

An exponent tells you how many times the base is used as a factor: $5^3 = 5 \cdot 5 \cdot 5$. Each law below comes from counting factors. If you forget one, write out a small case and count.

multiply, same base
$a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n}$   (add the exponents)
divide, same base
$\dfrac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n}$   (subtract the exponents)
power of a power
$\left(a^m\right)^n = a^{mn}$   (multiply the exponents)
power of a product
$(ab)^n = a^n b^n$
power of a quotient
$\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^n = \dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$
why it works
$x^2 \cdot x^3 = (x \cdot x)(x \cdot x \cdot x) = x^5$, and $\left(x^2\right)^3 = x^2 \cdot x^2 \cdot x^2 = x^6$
Example A$x^7 \cdot x^5 = x^{12}$. The bases match, so add the exponents.
Example B$\left(3x^4\right)^2 = 3^2 \cdot \left(x^4\right)^2 = 9x^8$. The power applies to the coefficient too.
Example C$\dfrac{12x^9}{4x^3} = 3x^6$. Divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents.
These laws need a shared base or a shared exponent. There is no law for a sum: $x^2 + x^3$ does not combine, and $(a + b)^2$ is not $a^2 + b^2$.
★Rule 2Negative and zero exponents

A negative exponent means divide by the factor instead of multiplying by it. The power moves to the other side of the fraction bar, and its exponent turns positive. A negative exponent does not make the value negative.

negative exponent
$a^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{a^n}$
in a denominator
$\dfrac{1}{a^{-n}} = a^n$
a fraction
$\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-n} = \left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^n$   (flip, then raise)
zero exponent
$a^0 = 1$ for $a \neq 0$
why it works
$2^3 = 8$,   $2^2 = 4$,   $2^1 = 2$,   $2^0 = 1$,   $2^{-1} = \dfrac{1}{2}$,   $2^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{4}$. Each step down divides by $2$
negative bases
$(-2)^4 = 16$ and $(-2)^3 = -8$: an even power is positive, and an odd power is negative. Without parentheses, $-2^4 = -16$
Example A$4^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{4^2} = \dfrac{1}{16}$.
Example B$\dfrac{x^3 y^{-2}}{x^{-1}} = \dfrac{x^3 \cdot x^1}{y^2} = \dfrac{x^4}{y^2}$. Each negative power crosses the fraction bar.
Example C$\left(\dfrac{2}{5}\right)^{-2} = \left(\dfrac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{25}{4}$.
The subtraction law still works with negative exponents: $\dfrac{x^3}{x^{-1}} = x^{3-(-1)} = x^4$. Use whichever view is easier for you to keep track of.
★Rule 3Fractional exponents and roots

A fractional exponent is a power and a root written together. The numerator is the power, and the denominator is the root. Rewriting a radical this way lets you use the laws from Rule 1.

one over $n$
$a^{\frac{1}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a}$, so $a^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sqrt{a}$ and $a^{\frac{1}{3}} = \sqrt[3]{a}$
$m$ over $n$
$a^{\frac{m}{n}} = \sqrt[n]{a^m} = \left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m$
how to remember it
Power over root. In $a^{\frac{m}{n}}$, the top number is the power and the bottom number is the root
why it works
$\left(a^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)^2 = a^1$, so $a^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is the number whose square is $a$
Example A$27^{\frac{2}{3}} = \left(\sqrt[3]{27}\right)^2 = 3^2 = 9$. Taking the root first keeps the numbers small.
Example B$\sqrt[4]{x^3} = x^{\frac{3}{4}}$, and $\sqrt{x^5} = x^{\frac{5}{2}}$.
Example C$\left(x^6 y^{10}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} = x^3 y^5$. A power of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ halves each exponent.
Example D$16^{-\frac{3}{4}} = \dfrac{1}{16^{3/4}} = \dfrac{1}{2^3} = \dfrac{1}{8}$. Handle the negative sign with Rule 2, then the fraction.
When a question asks which expression is equivalent to one with radicals, rewrite each radical as a fractional exponent first. Then add, subtract or multiply the exponents as in Rule 1.
★Rule 4One base, then compare exponents

If two powers of the same base are equal, their exponents are equal. When an equation mixes bases such as $4$, $8$ and $2$, rewrite each number as a power of one base before you do anything else.

the idea
if $b^x = b^y$, then $x = y$   (for $b > 0$ and $b \neq 1$)
powers of $2$
$4 = 2^2$,   $8 = 2^3$,   $16 = 2^4$,   $32 = 2^5$,   $64 = 2^6$
powers of $3$ and $5$
$9 = 3^2$,   $27 = 3^3$,   $81 = 3^4$,   $25 = 5^2$,   $125 = 5^3$
adding equal powers
$3^5 + 3^5 + 3^5 = 3 \cdot 3^5 = 3^6$   (count the copies, then multiply)
same exponent instead
if $p^n = q^n$ and both bases are positive, then $p = q$
Example A$9^x = 27^4$. Write both sides in base $3$: $3^{2x} = 3^{12}$, so $2x = 12$ and $x = 6$.
Example B$5^4 + 5^4 + 5^4 + 5^4 + 5^4 = 5 \cdot 5^4 = 5^5$. Five copies of $5^4$ make a product, so the exponents add.
Example C$p^{-2} = 3^{-4}$ with $p > 0$. Rewrite the right side with an exponent of $-2$: $3^{-4} = \left(3^2\right)^{-2} = 9^{-2}$, so $p = 9$.
Adding powers and multiplying powers are different operations. $2^3 \cdot 2^3 = 2^6$, but $2^3 + 2^3 = 2 \cdot 2^3 = 2^4$.
★Rule 5Growth and decay factors

When a quantity changes by the same percent each period, it is multiplied by the same number each period. That number is the multiplier from SAT Math 6: Percentages. In a model it is called the growth factor or the decay factor. Multiply by it once for each period, and the exponent counts the periods.

growth
$P(t) = P_0(1 + r)^t$   the growth factor $1 + r$ is greater than $1$
decay
$P(t) = P_0(1 - r)^t$   the decay factor $1 - r$ is between $0$ and $1$
the starting amount
$P_0$ is the value when $t = 0$, because a factor raised to the power $0$ is $1$
the rate
$r$ is the percent rate written as a decimal: $4\%$ is $0.04$, and $0.4\%$ is $0.004$
factors without a percent
doubling is a factor of $2$, tripling is a factor of $3$, and halving is a factor of $\dfrac{1}{2}$
Example AA deposit of $\$800$ grows by $3\%$ each year. The growth factor is $1.03$, so the model is $A(t) = 800(1.03)^t$.
Example BA sample with a mass of $250$ grams loses $12\%$ of its mass each hour. The decay factor is $1 - 0.12 = 0.88$, so the model is $M(t) = 250(0.88)^t$.
Example CAfter $2$ years, the deposit in Example A is worth $800(1.03)^2 = 800(1.0609) = 848.72$ dollars.
Rates below $1\%$ need care with the decimal point. A rate of $0.6\%$ is $0.006$, so the growth factor is $1.006$ and the decay factor is $0.994$.
★Rule 6Reading a model

A model written as $y = a \cdot b^t$ shows its starting value and its factor directly. Different parts of the model answer different questions, so identify which part a question asks about before you compute.

PartWhere it sitsWhat it tells you
$a$in frontthe initial value: the amount when $t = 0$, and the $y$-intercept of the graph
$b$the basethe factor for one period: greater than $1$ is growth, between $0$ and $1$ is decay
$b - 1$ or $1 - b$from the basethe percent rate as a decimal: $1.03$ is $3\%$ growth, and $0.97$ is $3\%$ decay
$t$the exponentthe number of periods, so check the unit: years, months, hours
factor to rate
$1.03 \to 3\%$ increase,   $1.3 \to 30\%$ increase,   $1.003 \to 0.3\%$ increase,   $0.97 \to 3\%$ decrease
from a table
divide one output by the output before it. If the ratio stays the same, that ratio is $b$
a time unit in the exponent
$b^{\frac{t}{3}}$ means one factor of $b$ for each $3$ units of time
linear or exponential
adding the same amount each period is linear. Multiplying by the same factor, or changing by the same percent, is exponential
Example A$y = 2{,}400(1.035)^t$: the initial value is $2{,}400$, and the quantity grows by $3.5\%$ each period.
Example B$y = 760(0.91)^t$, with $t$ in years: the quantity decreases by $9\%$ each year, because $1 - 0.91 = 0.09$.
Example C
$x$$0$$1$$2$$3$
$y$$80$$120$$180$$270$
Each output is $1.5$ times the one before it, and the output at $x = 0$ is $80$, so $y = 80(1.5)^x$.
Example D$N(t) = 50(2)^{\frac{t}{4}}$, with $t$ in hours: the quantity doubles once for each $4$ hours. After $12$ hours, $N = 50(2)^3 = 400$.
The factor and the rate are different numbers. In $y = 760(0.91)^t$ the factor is $0.91$, and the percent decrease is $9\%$. Check which one the question asks for.
Rule 7A constant to find, and average rate of change

Two more model questions come up less often, and neither needs a new formula. In the first, a model contains an unknown constant, and the question gives one input with its output. In the second, the question asks how fast a function changes, on average, between two inputs.

a constant to find
substitute the known input and output, then solve for the constant
average rate of change
$\dfrac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$   the slope of the line through the two points on the graph
Example A$C(x) = \dfrac{150x - 600}{x} + k$ and $C(30) = 310$. Substitute: $\dfrac{150(30) - 600}{30} + k = 310$, so $130 + k = 310$ and $k = 180$.
Example BFor $f(x) = x^2$, the average rate of change from $x = -1$ to $x = 4$ is $\dfrac{f(4) - f(-1)}{4 - (-1)} = \dfrac{16 - 1}{5} = 3$.
Example CFor $g(t) = 100(2)^t$, the average rate of change from $t = 1$ to $t = 3$ is $\dfrac{800 - 200}{3 - 1} = 300$. From $t = 3$ to $t = 5$ it is $\dfrac{3{,}200 - 800}{2} = 1{,}200$. An exponential model does not change at a constant rate.
Evaluate the function at each input first, then subtract. $f(4) - f(-1)$ is not the same as $f(5)$ or $f(3)$.
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common setup and interpretation mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1multiplying the exponents in a product
$x^3 \cdot x^4 = x^7$, not $x^{12}$. Exponents multiply only for a power of a power, such as $\left(x^3\right)^4 = x^{12}$.
Common mistake 2leaving the coefficient out of the power
$\left(2x^3\right)^4 = 2^4 \cdot x^{12} = 16x^{12}$. The exponent outside the parentheses applies to each factor inside.
Common mistake 3reading a negative exponent as a negative number
$5^{-2} = \dfrac{1}{25}$, which is positive. The negative sign in the exponent means a reciprocal.
Common mistake 4adding powers as if they were multiplied
$2^5 + 2^5 = 2 \cdot 2^5 = 2^6$, not $2^{10}$. Count how many copies are added, then multiply.
Common mistake 5swapping the root and the power
In $8^{\frac{2}{3}}$ the $3$ is the root and the $2$ is the power: $\left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 4$. Reading it the other way gives $\sqrt{8^3}$, which is about $22.6$.
Common mistake 6using the rate as the factor
Growth of $4\%$ per year means multiplying by $1.04$ each year, not by $0.04$ or by $4$. For a decrease of $4\%$, the factor is $0.96$.
Common mistake 7misplacing the decimal point in a small rate
$0.2\%$ is $0.002$, so a decrease of $0.2\%$ gives a factor of $0.998$. A factor of $0.98$ would be a $2\%$ decrease.
Common mistake 8answering with the factor when the question asks for the rate
In $y = 3{,}000(0.82)^t$ the factor is $0.82$, and the percent decrease is $18\%$. Reread the question to see which number it asks for.
Common mistake 9treating a repeated percent change as one subtraction
A value that falls $10\%$ a year for $3$ years is multiplied by $0.9^3 = 0.729$. That is a decrease of $27.1\%$, not $30\%$.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: an exponent counts repeated multiplication. The laws in Rules 1 to 4 are ways of counting factors. The models in Rules 5 and 6 multiply a starting amount by the same factor once per period. Each question asks you to notice which of these you are looking at, and then to count with care.

The walkthrough covers each rule with a worked example, a common mistake to watch for, and a short check for understanding. Start with the starred rules, and try each problem on paper before you reveal the solution.
