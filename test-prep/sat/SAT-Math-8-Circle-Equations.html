Circle equation rules

A circle is every point at the same distance from one point, its center. That distance is the radius. In the $xy$-plane, the equation of a circle states that distance with the Pythagorean theorem, so one form of the equation shows the center and the radius at a glance. Most questions on this sheet come down to getting the equation into that form and reading it. As you practice, start by identifying the question type. Then decide whether Desmos or algebra makes more sense, and check that your answer addresses what the question asks.

★ Essential: the rules you will use most often. Start here.Unstarred: comes up less often. Come back to these after you feel comfortable with the starred rules.
★Rule 1Standard form: read the center and the radius

Different forms reveal different information. Standard form shows the center and the radius directly, so recognize whether you have been given it before doing any algebra.

standard form
$(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$
center
$(h, k)$   (each sign is the opposite of the one you see)
radius
$r$, the square root of the right side
center at the origin
$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$
why it works
the legs $x - h$ and $y - k$ and the radius $r$ form a right triangle, so the Pythagorean theorem gives the equation
Example A$(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$ has center $(3, -2)$ and radius $5$. Read $y + 2$ as $y - (-2)$.
Example B$x^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 12$ has center $(0, 4)$ and radius $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$. When $x$ is squared on its own, the center is on the $y$-axis.
Example CA circle with center $(-1, 5)$ and radius $3$ has the equation $(x + 1)^2 + (y - 5)^2 = 9$.
The diameter is $2r$. If a question asks for the diameter, find the radius first and double it.
★Rule 2Completing the square to reach standard form

When the squares have been multiplied out, the center and the radius are hidden. Completing the square rebuilds standard form: group the $x$ terms, group the $y$ terms, and turn each group into a perfect square.

the pattern
$x^2 + bx + \left(\dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \dfrac{b}{2}\right)^2$
what to add
half of the coefficient of $x$, squared; then the same for $y$
keep it balanced
add the same numbers to the other side of the equation
a number in front of the squares
if the equation starts $2x^2 + 2y^2$, divide every term by $2$ first
Example A$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y = 12$. Group the terms: $(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 4y) = 12$. Half of $-6$ is $-3$, and $(-3)^2 = 9$. Half of $4$ is $2$, and $2^2 = 4$. Add $9$ and $4$ to both sides: $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25$. The center is $(3, -2)$ and the radius is $5$.
Example B$x^2 + 8x + y^2 = 20$. Only the $x$ terms need work. Add $16$ to both sides: $(x + 4)^2 + y^2 = 36$. The center is $(-4, 0)$ and the radius is $6$.
Example C$2x^2 + 2y^2 - 12x + 4y = 78$. Divide by $2$: $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 2y = 39$. Add $9$ and $1$ to both sides: $(x - 3)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 49$. The radius is $7$.
Desmos can graph a circle from either form of its equation, so typing the equation as it is written is a good way to check the center and radius you found. Want more practice with completing the square? It is covered in SAT Math 2: Quadratics →
★Rule 3Area and circumference from the equation

Once the equation is in standard form, the right side is $r^2$. The area uses that number as it stands. The circumference and the diameter need $r$, so take the square root first.

area
$\pi r^2$, which is $\pi$ times the right side of standard form
circumference
$2\pi r$
diameter
$2r$
working backward
an area of $49\pi$ means $r^2 = 49$, and a circumference of $14\pi$ means $r = 7$
Example A$(x - 1)^2 + (y - 6)^2 = 49$: the area is $49\pi$. The radius is $7$, so the circumference is $14\pi$.
Example B$x^2 + y^2 + 10y = 119$. Add $25$ to both sides: $x^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 144$. The area is $144\pi$, and the circumference is $2\pi(12) = 24\pi$.
Example C$(x + 2)^2 + y^2 = 20$: the area is $20\pi$. The radius is $\sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}$, so the circumference is $4\sqrt{5}\,\pi$.
The right side is already $r^2$. In Example A the area is $49\pi$, not $49^2\pi$.
★Rule 4Writing the equation from a center and a point, or from a diameter

To write the equation of a circle you need two things: the center and $r^2$. A question may give them indirectly, as the center and one point on the circle, or as the two ends of a diameter.

a center and a point
$r^2 = (x_1 - h)^2 + (y_1 - k)^2$, the squared distance from the center to the point
the ends of a diameter
the center is the midpoint: $\left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$
then the radius
the distance from the center to either end, which is half of the diameter
testing a point
substitute it into the left side: equal to $r^2$ means on the circle, less means inside, greater means outside
Example AA circle has center $(2, -1)$ and passes through $(5, 3)$. Then $r^2 = (5 - 2)^2 + (3 + 1)^2 = 9 + 16 = 25$, and the equation is $(x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = 25$.
Example BA diameter has endpoints $(0, 1)$ and $(6, 5)$. The center is the midpoint, $(3, 3)$. From the center to $(6, 5)$: $r^2 = 3^2 + 2^2 = 13$. The equation is $(x - 3)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 13$.
Example CIs $(5, 2)$ on the circle from Example A? $(5 - 2)^2 + (2 + 1)^2 = 9 + 9 = 18$, which is less than $25$, so the point is inside the circle. For $(6, 2)$ the left side is $16 + 9 = 25$, so that point is on the circle.
You need $r^2$ to write the equation, not $r$. In Example B, leaving $r^2 = 13$ as it is avoids a square root.
Rule 5Circles that touch an axis

A circle is tangent to a line when it touches the line at one point. When that line is an axis, the radius drawn to the touching point is perpendicular to the axis, so the radius equals the distance from the center to that axis.

tangent to the $x$-axis: $r = 3$
tangent to the $y$-axis: $r = 3$
tangent to the x-axis
$r = |k|$, and the circle touches the axis at $(h, 0)$
tangent to the y-axis
$r = |h|$, and the circle touches the axis at $(0, k)$
where a circle crosses an axis
set $y = 0$ to find $x$-intercepts, or $x = 0$ to find $y$-intercepts
Example AA circle with center $(4, 3)$ is tangent to the $x$-axis. The center is $3$ units above the axis, so $r = 3$ and the equation is $(x - 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 9$. The circle touches the axis at $(4, 0)$.
Example B$\overline{AB}$ is a diameter parallel to the $x$-axis, $B$ is the point $(11, 4)$, and the circle is tangent to the $x$-axis at $P(k, 0)$. A diameter passes through the center, so the center is $4$ units above the axis and $r = 4$. The center is $4$ units to the left of $B$, at $(7, 4)$, so $k = 7$.
Example CWhere does $(x - 6)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 25$ cross the $x$-axis? Set $y = 0$: $(x - 6)^2 + 16 = 25$, so $(x - 6)^2 = 9$ and $x = 3$ or $x = 9$.
A quick sketch helps. Mark the center, draw a radius straight to the axis, and label its length.
Rule 6Symmetry and semicircles

A circle is symmetric about the vertical line and the horizontal line through its center. Two points on the circle at the same height sit the same distance to the left and to the right of the center. Semicircle questions lean on this.

equal y-coordinates
the $x$-coordinates average to $h$, so $x_1 + x_2 = 2h$
highest and lowest points
$(h, k + r)$ and $(h, k - r)$
leftmost and rightmost points
$(h - r, k)$ and $(h + r, k)$
a semicircle on the x-axis
the center is the midpoint of the two endpoints, and the highest point is $(h, r)$
Example AA semicircle meets the $x$-axis at $(1, 0)$ and $(11, 0)$. The center is $(6, 0)$ and the radius is $5$. Two points with equal $y$-coordinates have $x$-coordinates that add to $12$, such as $3$ and $9$.
Example BThe highest point of a semicircle is $(-1, 6)$, and its diameter lies on the $x$-axis. The center is straight below the highest point, at $(-1, 0)$, and $r = 6$. The endpoints are $(-7, 0)$ and $(5, 0)$.
Example COn the circle $(x - 4)^2 + (y - 9)^2 = 25$, the greatest $y$-coordinate is $9 + 5 = 14$ and the least is $9 - 5 = 4$.
If two points on a circle have the same $x$-coordinate instead, their $y$-coordinates average to $k$.
Common mistakes and what to check

These are common reading and setup mistakes. Review them so you know what to check when an answer seems off.

Common mistake 1keeping the signs you see
$(x + 3)^2 + (y - 8)^2 = 4$ has center $(-3, 8)$, not $(3, -8)$. Standard form subtracts the coordinates of the center, so $x + 3$ means $h = -3$.
Common mistake 2using the right side as the radius
In $(x - 1)^2 + (y - 6)^2 = 49$ the right side is $r^2$. The radius is $7$, not $49$.
Common mistake 3squaring the right side again for the area
The area is $\pi r^2$, and the right side of standard form is already $r^2$. For $(x - 1)^2 + (y - 6)^2 = 49$ the area is $49\pi$.
Common mistake 4adding to one side only
To complete the squares in $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y = 12$ you add $9$ and $4$ on the left. Add them on the right as well: $12 + 9 + 4 = 25$.
Common mistake 5completing the square with a number in front
In $2x^2 + 2y^2 - 12x + 4y = 78$, divide every term by $2$ before you complete the squares, including the $78$.
Common mistake 6using the whole diameter as the radius
When two points are the ends of a diameter, the distance between them is $2r$. Measure from the midpoint to one end, or halve the distance, before you square it.
Common mistake 7choosing the other coordinate for a tangent circle
A circle with center $(5, -4)$ that is tangent to the $x$-axis has radius $4$, its distance from the $x$-axis. The $5$ is its distance from the $y$-axis.
Common mistake 8stopping one step early
After you find the center and the radius, reread the question. It may ask for the diameter, the area, the circumference, or one coordinate of the center.

Worked examples & practice

The main idea behind these rules is simple: a circle is every point at one fixed distance from its center. Standard form states that distance with the Pythagorean theorem, so the center and the radius can be read from it. Each question asks you to get the equation into that form, read what it shows, and answer what was asked.
