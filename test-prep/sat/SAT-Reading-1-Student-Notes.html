1

What a Student Notes Question Is

These are easy to spot: instead of a passage, you see bullets. A box of four or five notes on the left, and on the right a sentence that starts The student wants to… with four full-sentence choices. They come up as the last questions of each Reading & Writing module, usually one to three of them. Here is what one looks like:

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
  • The Ashford footbridge was built in 1904 from riveted wrought iron.
  • It was designed by a local mill engineer, Nora Quill, who had never built a bridge before.
  • The bridge carried foot traffic between two mill towns until 1971.
  • In 2019 it was restored and reopened as part of a rail-trail.
  • Its 210-foot span was the longest single-span iron footbridge in the state when it opened.

The student wants to emphasize the length of the Ashford footbridge to an audience unfamiliar with it. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to achieve this goal?

  1. A. The Ashford footbridge, a 1904 iron span that once linked two mill towns, stretched 210 feet, the longest single-span iron footbridge in the state when it opened.
  2. B. Designed by Nora Quill, a mill engineer with no bridge experience, the Ashford footbridge opened in 1904 and served two mill towns.
  3. C. At 210 feet, the span was the longest of its kind in the state.
  4. D. The bridge was restored in 2019 and now carries rail-trail users rather than mill workers.
2

The Strategy

The strategy is really simple here. Read the question, identify what the answer must contain, then grade every multiple-choice answer on every requirement. Pick the choice that scores full marks. That is your answer!

The Five Moves: SCORE

Five moves, in the order you make them. Remember them as SCORE, because that is what you are doing.

S
Skip the passage
Go straight to the question. The notes are almost never needed.
C
Circle the requirements
Circle the 2 or 3 things the question wants the answer to have.
O R
One point · Rate
Each requirement is worth one point. Each answer choice gets one point per requirement it directly addresses.
E
Eliminate
Eliminate all but the highest-scoring answer. Circle it and move on!
Why this works. Every choice is true: each one comes straight from the notes. So truth is never the test. The only thing that separates the four is whether they do the job the question asked for, and the question tells you the job in plain words.
1

Skip the Passage: Read the Question First

The notes are the biggest block of text on the screen, and they are the least useful. Every choice is built from them, so reading them cannot help you choose. What can help is the question: it names exactly what the right answer has to do.

The move: eyes go to the right-hand column first. Read the sentence that starts The student wants to. Do not read the bullets. If two choices tie on the scorecard later, the notes are your safety net: and that is the only time you need them.

Try it: no notes at all

Concept

Here is a question with the notes removed. Answer it anyway.

The student wants to present a similarity between hawk moths and hummingbirds. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to achieve this goal?

  1. A. Hummingbirds are among the few birds able to hover in place.
  2. B. Hawk moths feed at dusk, whereas most hummingbirds feed during the day.
  3. C. Like hummingbirds, hawk moths hover in front of flowers as they feed on nectar.
  4. D. A hawk moth can beat its wings as many as 70 times each second.

You did not need a single bullet. The question asked for a similarity; only one choice says two things are alike (Like hummingbirds, hawk moths…). One choice is a contrast (whereas), and two describe a single animal.

Why skipping saves the question, not just time

Concept

Reading the notes first does something worse than waste thirty seconds: it fills your head with five true facts, and then every choice looks familiar and reasonable. Reading the question first fills your head with one job, and then three choices look obviously off. The order you read in changes what you see.

Time math. Five bullets take 30–40 seconds to read carefully. Over three Student Notes questions that is nearly two minutes: two full questions’ worth of time: handed back to you for the hard reading items.
1

Circle the Requirements

Everything after The student wants to is a list of what the right answer has to have. Usually two or three things. Circle each one.

The move: circle the requirements. Count what you circled. That number is how many rows your scorecard has.

What you might circle

Concept

The same handful of requirements show up again and again. Learn to spot them on sight.

A similarity or a difference
present a similarity between… · emphasize a difference between…. The answer must name both things and compare them.
A specific focus
the cause of · how it works · the goal of the study · the lasting impact · the variety of · the scale of. The answer must state that exact thing, not just be about the topic.
An unfamiliar audience
to an audience unfamiliar with…. The answer must include basic background (full name, what it is). Usually the longest choice.
A familiar audience
to an audience familiar with…. The answer must skip the background and get to the point. A choice that introduces the subject loses.
The audience line is always its own requirement. It counts just as much as the main one. A choice that nails the focus but ignores the audience scores 1 out of 2, and that is not the answer.

Sprint: Read the Question, Name the Job

The whole method rests on one reflex: look at the question sentence and know instantly what shape it is and how many rows the scorecard has. One question at a time, no notes, no choices. Tap the shape, then the number of requirements. Under four seconds is test speed.

Keyboard: 1 Comparison · 2 Familiar · 3 Unfamiliar · 4 Single focus, then 1 / 2 for the row count.

1

One Point and Rate

Now build the card. One row per requirement, one column per choice. Read choice A against row 1: does it state that requirement? Mark it. Row 2. Then choice B. You are not asking “is this true?” or “is this about the topic?”: only “does this sentence do the job in this row?”

The move: grade every cell. Being on-topic is not a point: a sentence about wolves’ signals is not a sentence about their body language unless it names something they do with their bodies.

Signal words that earn the point

Concept
Similarity
like, both, similarly, also, as well, share. A choice about one thing alone cannot score.
Difference
unlike, whereas, while, but, however, in contrast, than. A choice about one thing alone cannot score.
Cause / how / why
because, results from, by, when…then, using. A choice that names the effect without the mechanism does not score.
Significance / impact
led to, underlies, still in use, changed, supported. A date or a description is not impact.

1

Eliminate All but the Highest Score

Cross out every choice that is not the top score. Exactly one column will have a ✓ in every row. Circle it and move on; there is no need to reread the notes to confirm, because every choice was already true.

The tiebreaker: if you are unsure about one cell and two columns look close, go with the highest score. A choice that clearly does two of two jobs beats a choice that maybe does two. Only if two columns are exactly tied do you open the notes.

The three shapes a question takes

Concept

Most Student Notes questions are one of three kinds. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what the winning choice will look like before you read a single option.

Comparison
present a similarity / a difference. The winner names both things and uses a comparison word. Any choice about one thing is out on sight.
Familiar / unfamiliar
to an audience unfamiliar with → the winner carries background and is often the longest. Familiar → the winner skips background; a choice that introduces the person loses.
Single focus
present the cause / specify how / emphasize the impact. One row. The winner states that exact thing; on-topic neighbors (the effect, the date, the definition) score zero.

SCORE Overview: The Whole Guide in One Page

Same order as the tabs. Reread this the night before the test.

S: Skip the passage

1Read the question first

Eyes to the right-hand column. The sentence after The student wants to is the whole question.

2The notes are almost never needed

Every choice is already true. Reading five bullets cannot help you choose: it just costs you the time. Open them only if two choices tie.

C: Circle the requirements

3Verb + what comes after it

Present a similarity, emphasize the impact, specify how. That phrase is row one.

4The audience line is row two

Unfamiliar → the answer must include background (often the longest choice). Familiar → the answer must skip it.

O R: One point and rate

5One row per requirement, one column per choice

Two requirements means every choice gets graded twice.

6On-topic is not a point

A choice earns a ✓ only if it states the requirement. About the subject is not the same as doing the job.

7Know the signal words

Similarity: like, both. Difference: unlike, whereas, than. Cause: because, by, when. Impact: led to, still in use.

E: Eliminate

8Cross out everything but the top score

Exactly one column is perfect. Circle it and move on. No need to confirm against the notes.

9When in doubt, highest score wins

A clear 2-of-2 beats a maybe 2-of-2. Ties are the only time the notes come out.
