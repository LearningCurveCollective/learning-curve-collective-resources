Strategy: your word first, then theirs

Words-in-context questions are designed so that every choice sounds plausible if you read the choices first. So don't. Here is the order that works.

  1. Cover the answer choices. Literally, with your hand or by not scrolling down. The choices are the last thing you look at.
  2. Read the sentence and find the clue. Somewhere near the blank is a phrase that tells you what the blank must mean: a contrast ("far from," "although," "instead"), a restatement, or an example. Underline it.
  3. Fill the blank with your own word. A plain, everyday word is best: "blocked," "helped," "random," "profitable." If a simple word will not come, decide at least whether the blank is positive or negative.
  4. Now look at the choices and find the closest synonym to your word. You are matching, not choosing. Cross out anything that does not match your word, even if it sounds sophisticated or fits the topic.
  5. Reread the sentence with your answer in the blank. If it says what the clue says, you are done.

Try it on Question 1

Tips that come up on every set

The list word is not always the answer

In these sets the study word is the correct answer only about a quarter of the time. Sometimes it is the tempting wrong choice. Recognizing a word is not the same as it fitting the sentence, so always match to the clue.

Topic match is the trap

The most common wrong answer is a word that belongs to the sentence's subject (farming, science, art) but does not match the clue. If the sentence is about earnings, the answer is about money, not about the crop.

Find the charge first

Decide whether the blank is positive, negative, or neutral before anything else. That alone eliminates two choices on most questions.

Contrast words flip the meaning

"Although," "but," "instead," "far from," "rather than," and "on the other hand" mean the blank is the opposite of what came before. "In fact" and "indeed" mean it agrees.

"Most nearly means" questions

The word is already in the passage. Cover the choices, reread that sentence, and swap in your own word. Then match. The passage's meaning wins over the dictionary's first definition.

"Applying the word" and "correct usage" questions

Turn the definition into a test: "premeditated" means planned ahead, so the right situation must show planning before the act. Check each choice against the test, not against how it sounds.
