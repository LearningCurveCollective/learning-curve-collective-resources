PSAT/NMSQT Fact Sheet

The PSAT/NMSQT is the SAT’s slightly gentler sibling: same Bluebook app, same two adaptive sections, same timing, same question types, scored 320–1520. Schools give it in October. For sophomores it is a baseline; for juniors it is also the National Merit qualifying test.

Ten-minute break between sections. No essay. Scores are not sent to colleges.

What is on it

Concept

Reading & Writing: 54 questions

Information & Ideas
12–14 questions
Main idea, supporting details, using evidence (text or a graph), and drawing a sound inference.

Urban beekeepers in Detroit have found that hives on rooftops produce more honey per season than hives in nearby suburbs. Researchers attribute the difference to the city’s variety of flowering plants, which bloom in sequence from early spring to late fall.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  1. A. Beekeepers in Detroit’s suburbs are moving hives to city rooftops to take advantage of a longer season.
  2. B. Rooftop hives in Detroit outproduce suburban hives, likely because the city offers a longer bloom season.
  3. C. Flowering plants in Detroit bloom earlier in the spring and later in the fall than suburban plants do.
  4. D. Researchers are still uncertain why rooftop hives in Detroit differ from hives in nearby suburbs.

Main idea means the whole text in one line: the finding (rooftop hives produce more) plus the explanation (a longer bloom season). The other choices each stretch one detail past what the text says.

Standard English Conventions
11–15 questions
Sentence boundaries, punctuation, subject–verb and pronoun agreement, verb forms.

The telescope’s mirror is 8 meters it took nearly a decade to grind and polish.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  1. A. across,
  2. B. across;
  3. C. across
  4. D. across; and

Complete sentence on each side, so you need a stop: the semicolon. A comma alone is a splice, nothing at all is a run-on, and a semicolon plus and doubles up.

Advanced Math
12–14 questions
Quadratics, exponential growth, polynomials, and nonlinear systems.

The function f is defined by f(x) = x2 − 6x + 5. What is the minimum value of f(x)?

  1. A. −9
  2. B. −4
  3. C. 3
  4. D. 5

The vertex sits at x = 3, and f(3) = 9 − 18 + 5 = −4. Graph it in Desmos and tap the vertex: ten seconds.

Problem Solving & Data Analysis
7–9 questions
Ratios, percents, statistics, probability, and reading tables and graphs.

A jacket priced at $80 is on sale for 25% off. A 10% sales tax is then applied to the sale price. What is the total cost of the jacket?

  1. A. $62
  2. B. $64
  3. C. $66
  4. D. $68

Sale price 80 × 0.75 = 60; with tax 60 × 1.10 = 66. Read the last sentence first so you know the target is the total, not the sale price.

Geometry & Trigonometry
4–6 questions
Area and volume, angles, triangles, circles, and right-triangle trig.

  1. A. 5/12
  2. B. 5/13
  3. C. 12/13
  4. D. 13/5

Hypotenuse 13 (a 5–12–13 triangle). Sine is opposite over hypotenuse: 5/13. Two of the wrong choices are the other ratios of the same triangle, write SOH before you pick.

C · Clock it

The trick to pacing the test is to check your time at two checkpoints: halfway through the section and ¾ of the way through. Checking the clock after every question wastes time and focus; never checking means the last five questions get rushed. Two checkpoints keep you on pace without the stress.

L · Lead with the question

Read the question first: before the passage, the graph, or the three-sentence word problem. Leading with the question turns reading into searching, so know what you are looking for.

Try it: Math

Try It

Read the last sentence first, then solve.

A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 and a length of 9. What is the area of the rectangle?

  1. A. 6
  2. B. 15
  3. C. 27
  4. D. 54

Leading with the question, you know the target is area, not width. Perimeter 30 with length 9 gives a width of 6; area is 9 × 6 = 54.

Six is the number you get if you stop one step early. Knowing the target before you start is what keeps you going that one more step.

Try it: Reading

Try It

Read the question, then the text.

Marine biologist Ana Ortiz has spent eleven summers surveying the same stretch of reef off the coast of Belize. When a record heat wave arrived in 2023, she expected the reef’s coral to bleach, as it had during two smaller warming events earlier in the decade. Most of the outer reef did exactly that, turning bone white within weeks. Colonies in the shaded lagoon behind the reef, however, kept their color. The lagoon’s cloudy water had long been considered a drawback, since it limits how much sunlight reaches the coral below. Ortiz now suspects that this same cloudiness filters the light that stresses coral during a heat wave. She plans to test the idea next season by measuring light levels at several depths in both locations.

Based on the text, what does Ortiz think about the lagoon’s cloudy water?

  1. A. It is the main reason the coral bleached.
  2. B. It may protect coral by filtering light.
  3. C. It has always been considered beneficial.
  4. D. It became clearer during the heat wave.

The question names cloudy water, so you read hunting for that phrase instead of studying every sentence. It shows up in the fifth sentence, and the sentence right after it carries Ortiz’s view: the cloudiness filters the light that stresses coral.

Seven sentences, one targeted lookup. That is the whole payoff of leading with the question.

E · Eliminate on screen

Your brain will happily reconsider a choice you think you already ruled out. That means more time per question and more second-guessing, and both add up over a whole section. If you know it is wrong, CROSS IT OUT! With two choices crossed out, even a guess is a coin flip, and most of the time you will not need to guess: once two are gone, the right answer is usually clear.

In Bluebook, the ABC button above the choices turns on the answer eliminator; tap a choice to strike it through.In Bluebook, the ABC button above the choices turns on the answer eliminator; tap a choice to strike it through.

Three ways to eliminate on every test

Concept
Twins
On the SATPSAT there is only ever one right answer. So if you see two answer choices that would do exactly the same job, neither one can be right: cross out both. Two transition words from the same family, or a period and a semicolon in the same spot, are the classic pair.
Wrong direction
In Math, a choice that grows when the story shrinks, has the wrong sign, or is off by a factor of ten is out before you compute anything.
Not in the text
In reading, a choice that says something the passage never says is out, however reasonable it sounds. The answer always needs to be supported by words in the passage.
Then decide
With two left, reread the question once and pick the one that answers exactly that. Do not un-cross anything.

Try it: Twins

Try It

Tap the ABC button on the question to turn on the cross-out tool, just like Bluebook. Then tap the small letter beside any choice you know is wrong.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Sea otters eat up to a quarter of their body weight each day. they spend most of their waking hours hunting.

  1. A. However,
  2. B. In contrast,
  3. C. Consequently,
  4. D. For instance,

However and In contrast would do the same job, so neither can be the answer, cross both out before you even think about meaning.

Eating that much and hunting all day are cause and effect, so Consequently wins over For instance, which would need an example to follow.

Try it: Wrong direction

Try It

A tank drains at a constant rate from 500 gallons to 140 gallons in 6 hours. Which equation gives the number of gallons g in the tank t hours after it starts draining?

  1. A. g = 500 + 60t
  2. B. g = 140 − 60t
  3. C. g = 60t − 500
  4. D. g = 500 − 60t

The tank is draining, so g must go down as t goes up: anything with +60t or 60t − 500 is out. It starts at 500, so 140 − 60t is out.

One choice left, and the rate checks: (500 − 140) ÷ 6 = 60 gallons per hour. g = 500 − 60t.

Concept
Pick a letter of the day

Fill every blank first
With 90 seconds left, stop solving. Answer everything unanswered, then spend what is left on one marked question.

The math of guessing. Four blind guesses average one right answer. Four guesses after crossing out two choices each average two. Over a whole test, that is the difference between a handful of points and a real score bump: for zero risk.

R · Return, don’t stall

Every question is worth the same one point. Spending four minutes on a hard one is spending three easy questions’ worth of time. The rule is the 30-second rule: if you have no path after 30 seconds, pick a placeholder, mark it, and move on. You will come back with fresh eyes and the clock on your side.

If 3x + 2y = 22 and x − y = −1, what is the value of xy?

  1. A. 12
  2. B. 20
  3. C. 24

In Desmos, type 3x + 2y = 22 on one line and x - y = -1 on the next. Tap the point where the lines cross: (4, 5).

So x = 4 and y = 5, and xy = 20. By hand: x = y − 1, so 3(y − 1) + 2y = 22, which gives y = 5.

Formatting your answers

About a quarter of SAT Math questions have you type in an answer rather than choosing from multiple choice. These questions are called Student-Produced Responses (SPR, sometimes called grid-ins): you enter the numerical answer yourself.

A right answer typed the wrong way means you do not get the points, so learn the format now.

Mixed numbers

Percents, dollars and units
No symbols in the box. The question tells you the unit: if it asks “what percent” and the answer is 25%, type 25. If it asks for dollars and the answer is $1,250, type 1250.

Strategy Tips: the one-pager

Everything in this playbook, compressed. Read it the night before; that is all the studying that night should be.

1The week before

One full-length practice test under real timing, on the real app if you can (Bluebook practice tests). Then review the misses, not the score.

2Two nights before

That is your good night of sleep. The night before the test is usually a little restless no matter what, so bank the rest early.

3Night before

Charge the device to 100% and pack the charger. Bag: admission ticket or school instructions, approved calculator with fresh batteries, two pencils, a snack and water for the break, layers.

4Morning of

Eat something with protein. Arrive early. No new material, skim your CLEAR letters and your checkpoints, nothing else.

5First minute of each module

Say the five letters. Write your halfway time if you are on paper. Breathe once. Go.

6Read the question first

Every question, every section. It is the single habit with the biggest payoff.

7Cross out on screen

If you know a choice is wrong, cross it out so you never look at it again. Use the eliminator, not your memory.

8Placeholder, mark, move

Never leave a question blank on the way past. Never spend more than 30 seconds without a plan.

9Type fractions as fractions

On the SAT and PSAT, a fraction needs no rounding decision. If it fits, type it.

10Halfway glance

One look at the clock at the halfway question. Adjust once, not constantly.

11Use the break

Stand up, eat, drink water, do not talk about the section you just finished. It is over and it went fine.

12Last 90 seconds

Stop solving. Fill every blank with your letter of the day, then return to one marked question.
